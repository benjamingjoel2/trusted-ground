On Istanbul's Historic Peninsula, two of the world's great domed buildings face each other across a square where chariots once raced, and a short walk away brings you into covered lanes heavy with the scent of spice. This compact area, enclosed by ancient walls and by the Bosphorus, the Sea of Marmara and the Golden Horn, has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1985. With a couple of days and some planning around worship and crowds, you can see its essentials on foot.

## The Historic Peninsula and the Hippodrome

Go Türkiye, the country's official tourism body, describes the peninsula, also known as Suriçi (the walled city), as the former capital of both the Eastern Roman and Ottoman empires.

Its open heart is Sultanahmet Square, the old Hippodrome. Go Türkiye says it once hosted Roman chariot races and, later, Ottoman ceremonies and Ramadan festivities. Look for:

- **The Serpent Column**, around 2,500 years old
- **The Obelisk of Theodosius**, brought from Egypt
- **The German Fountain**, a gift from Emperor Wilhelm II

## Hagia Sophia

Go Türkiye calls the Hagia Sophia Grand Mosque perhaps the most iconic structure in Istanbul. It was completed in 537 after about five years of construction, under Emperor Justinian. Its central dome is 32.6 metres in diameter and rises more than 55 metres. For over 1,000 years it was the world's largest cathedral.

Its history is layered. After the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453, Sultan Mehmet II converted it into a mosque. The great Ottoman architect Mimar Sinan later reinforced it with buttresses and added minarets. It served as a museum for about 70 years and has functioned as a mosque again since 2020. Go Türkiye describes it as open to all as a monument, and notes that the nearby Hagia Sophia History and Experience Museum traces the building's story digitally.

Because Hagia Sophia is a working mosque, visitor routes, opening times and charges for foreign visitors can differ from those for worshippers and have changed in recent years. Check current arrangements through official channels shortly before you go.

## The Blue Mosque

Facing Hagia Sophia, the Sultanahmet Mosque overlooks the square with its six minarets. Go Türkiye explains that it is widely known as the Blue Mosque because of the blue tones in its İznik tiles and painted decoration. It remains one of the city's most important religious landmarks, so remember that sightseers are guests in a place of worship.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) advises dressing modestly when visiting a mosque or shrine to avoid causing offence, and following local dress codes during Ramadan.

## The Basilica Cistern and Topkapı Palace

Just across from Hagia Sophia, steps lead down into the Basilica Cistern. Go Türkiye says it was built in 532, spans about 10,000 square metres and has more than 300 marble columns rising from the water. Two Medusa heads sit upside down beneath column bases, and the "Weeping Column" is carved with teardrop patterns.

A few minutes away, Topkapı Palace occupies the tip of the peninsula where the Golden Horn, the Sea of Marmara and the Bosphorus meet. Construction began in 1459, and for nearly 400 years it was the Ottoman sultans' residence and seat of government. Today it is one of Türkiye's most visited museums, with the harem, imperial kitchens, Chinese porcelain and treasures given to the sultans.

## The Grand Bazaar and the Spice Bazaar

Go Türkiye describes the Grand Bazaar (Kapalıçarşı) as one of Istanbul's oldest commercial centres and one of the largest covered markets in the world. Its labyrinthine corridors sell carpets, jewellery, decorative objects and handicrafts, and many workshops still make goods by hand. The tourism body is candid that haggling is part of the experience: the main question is how far you bargain to get the right price.

The Spice Bazaar (Mısır Çarşısı) stands next to the Yeni Mosque in Eminönü. Built in the second half of the 17th century, it sells spices, Turkish delight, dried fruits and herbal teas, and Go Türkiye notes that locals still shop here alongside visitors.

The bazaars are crowded, so stay alert. The FCDO says street robbery and pickpocketing are common in Istanbul's major tourist areas, so keep belongings secure. It also warns to be wary of strangers who offer to change money or take you to a restaurant or nightclub, to buy your own drinks and keep sight of them, and to use registered, metered taxis rather than unofficial drivers.

## Before you go

- Check current opening hours and visitor arrangements for Hagia Sophia and Topkapı Palace.
- Check visiting times for working mosques before you go: worship comes first.
- Carry a scarf or layer so you can dress modestly at religious sites.
- Agree a price before you buy in the bazaars, and haggle politely.
- Keep valuables secure in crowded areas and use registered, metered taxis.
