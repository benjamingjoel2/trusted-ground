Madagascar's protected forests, from misty eastern rainforest to dry western woodland and southern sandstone canyons, are where travellers go to look for lemurs. In most national parks you must walk with a certified guide and carry a ticket. For many parks the dry months, roughly May to November, are the recommended time to visit. The rainy season, from December to April, can make roads hard going.

## Why lemurs matter

Seeing lemurs in the wild is a privilege, because most species are now in serious trouble. In its 2020 Red List update, the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) reported that 103 of the 107 surviving lemur species were threatened, almost a third of them (31%) Critically Endangered. Verreaux's sifaka and Madame Berthe's mouse lemur were moved up to Critically Endangered. The ring-tailed lemur, perhaps the best-known species, is listed as Endangered.

The IUCN names deforestation and hunting as the main threats. Slash-and-burn farming and logging for charcoal and fuelwood destroy habitat, and hunting continues although it is illegal.

## Four parks and what they protect

Madagascar National Parks publishes a page for each protected area. Four of them show how much the landscapes vary:

- **Analamazaotra (Andasibe area).** About 140 km east of Antananarivo on the RN2, this is one of the closer parks to the capital. The park lists 14 lemur species, including the indri, the diademed sifaka, the common brown lemur and the aye-aye.
- **Ranomafana.** This rainforest park lies roughly 64 km north-east of Fianarantsoa. It also has 14 lemur species, among them the golden bamboo lemur (*Hapalemur aureus*). The park counts four of its species as Critically Endangered and three as Endangered.
- **Ankarafantsika.** In the north-west, in the Boeny region, this park is dry forest rather than rainforest. It lists eight lemur species, including Coquerel's sifaka (*Propithecus coquereli*) and the mongoose lemur (*Eulemur mongoz*).
- **Isalo.** A park of sandstone canyons, plateaux and natural pools on the RN7, with its reception office at Ranohira. It covers 81,540 hectares. Its lemur list includes *Lemur catta* (the ring-tailed lemur) and Verreaux's sifaka.

## When to go

The parks' own advice points to the drier months:

- **Analamazaotra:** May to October for wildlife. November to April brings flowering and lush forest, but more rain.
- **Ranomafana:** April to November, with visits in the morning, although the park is open all year.
- **Ankarafantsika:** June to November for lemur watching.
- **Isalo:** open all year. The park does not name a best season.

The weather affects travel as well as wildlife. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) says Madagascar's cyclone season normally runs from November to April. It also warns that in the rainy season, December to April, many secondary roads can only be used by four-wheel-drive vehicles and bridges are often washed away.

## Park rules: guides, tickets and wildlife

The rules are much the same from park to park:

- **Guides are mandatory.** Analamazaotra and Ankarafantsika require a certified guide. At Ranomafana and Isalo you must always be accompanied by a guide.
- **Carry your ticket.** Analamazaotra and Ankarafantsika say you must bring a ticket on every visit. At Isalo you show a validated ticket at the reception office in Ranohira. Fees are published separately by Madagascar National Parks and change from time to time, so check the current rates before you go.
- **Local charges.** Ranomafana says foreign visitors pay a communal tax of 5,000 ariary per person before entering the park's circuits (at the time of writing, November 2026).
- **Hands off.** Several parks forbid touching animals or buying forest products.

## Getting there safely

Road travel is the hardest part of many wildlife trips in Madagascar. The FCDO says road conditions vary greatly and are often poor, and it advises against driving at night. It also advises against using taxi-brousses (multi-passenger bush taxis) because of robbery and road deaths. The FCDO names several roads that need extra care, including the RN7, which runs south towards Isalo. It also lists the area near Isalo National Park among places where attacks have taken place. Read the regional advice before you plan a route through the south and west.

## Before you go

- Check the FCDO's regional risks page for the roads on your route, and avoid travelling after dark.
- Look up current entrance fees on Madagascar National Parks' site, and budget for guide fees and any local charges.
- Plan for the dry season if lemurs are your priority, and expect flooded roads from December to April.
- Book your guide through the park office or visitor centre, and ask which circuits suit the species you hope to see.
- Pack rain gear even in the dry months: Analamazaotra's own page notes that the park has no real dry season.
- Keep your distance from wildlife, and never feed or touch lemurs.
