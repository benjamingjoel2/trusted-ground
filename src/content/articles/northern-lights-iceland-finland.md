You need three things at once: auroral activity, darkness and a clear sky. In Iceland and Finnish Lapland, darkness rules out the summer months, so the realistic season runs from roughly late August to early April, and the weeks either side of the equinoxes tend to be the most active. On any given night, cloud is the thing most likely to stop you, so the cloud forecast matters as much as the aurora forecast.

## When to go

The aurora happens all year, but you can only see it when the sky is dark. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center points out that the latitudes where aurora occurs are also where it does not get dark in summer, so a midsummer trip for the lights "usually doesn't work".

Within the dark season, timing still matters:

- **Equinoxes.** NOAA notes a tendency for larger geomagnetic storms, and so better auroras, around the spring and autumn equinoxes. The Finnish Meteorological Institute (FMI) says the same: late winter and early autumn are statistically the best periods.
- **March in Finland.** FMI singles out March as a recommended time to wait for the lights, because spring is on average less cloudy.
- **The season itself.** Visit Finland gives Finland's season as late August to early April, and suggests September–October and February–March for strong activity with milder temperatures than midwinter.
- **The hour.** NOAA says the best displays are usually within an hour or two of midnight, roughly 10pm to 2am local time. FMI also points to the hours either side of midnight, and Visit Finland gives 9pm to 2am.

## Where your odds are best

Going further north helps. NOAA describes the best viewing zone as lying between about 60 and 75 degrees north. Both Iceland and Finnish Lapland are in or near that band.

**Finland.** FMI's statistics show auroras on three nights out of four in the far north of Lapland, at places such as Kilpisjärvi and Utsjoki, as long as it is dark and clear. Visit Finland puts it a little differently: in Lapland, roughly every other clear night between August and April, falling to around 10 to 20 nights a year in southern Finland. FMI notes that auroras occur over Helsinki about once a month in the dark season, but city lights usually hide them.

**Iceland.** The Icelandic Meteorological Office (IMO) explains that the auroral oval sits just north of Iceland during the day and moves over the country in the evening. When activity is strong, the oval widens and moves further south.

Wherever you are, NOAA's advice is the same: get away from town lights and find a place with a clear view to the north. A full moon will not weaken the aurora, but it makes it look fainter.

## How the forecasts work

Two separate forecasts matter: space weather (how active the aurora is) and ordinary weather (whether you will see the sky).

**The Kp index.** IMO's aurora forecast uses the Kp index, a scale from 0 to 9 measuring three-hourly disturbance in the Earth's magnetic field. According to IMO, Kp is between 0 and 4 about 90% of the time, and values of 7 to 9 occur only about 1% of the time. You do not need a big number: IMO notes that even grade 2 (low activity) "can be beautiful" and grade 3 "can be dazzling". NOAA likewise says that if you are under the auroral zone you can see very good displays at Kp 3 or 4.

**How far ahead you can trust it.** IMO explains that solar wind particles take about one to three days to reach Earth, which is why major storms can usually be predicted. Because the sun rotates roughly every 27 days as seen from Earth, some disturbances repeat on that cycle, so longer outlooks are possible but less certain. IMO describes the short-range graph, covering the next few hours, as the most accurate.

**Iceland's forecast page.** IMO's aurora page combines a cloud-cover map (green is cloudy, white is clear) with a 0 to 9 activity forecast for midnight and the times of sunset, darkness and moonrise for Reykjavík. Check the cloud map first. Activity alone tells you nothing if the sky is overcast.

**Finland's forecast page.** FMI publishes live readings from its magnetometer stations. Yellow bars or markers mean auroras are possible but usually dim. Red means they are very likely. These show what is happening now rather than a forecast days ahead, so they are most useful on the night.

## On the night

- Check cloud cover and be ready to move. Visit Finland notes that cloud is the most common reason people miss the lights, and that conditions can differ a lot over a few kilometres.
- Dress for standing still in the cold. Clear northern nights are often well below freezing.
- In Iceland, weather and road conditions can change fast. Safetravel, run by the Icelandic Association for Search and Rescue (ICE-SAR), recommends sharing a travel plan with someone you trust. Its app lets hikers send their GPS location to the 112 emergency service.
- Build in several nights. A single night is a gamble on the weather, whatever the forecast says.

## Questions to ask your operator

- How do you decide whether a tour goes ahead: which cloud and activity forecasts do you check, and when?
- If the night is cloudy or the tour is cancelled, do I get a refund or a free rebooking, and is that in writing?
- How far are you willing to drive to find clear skies, and how long is the longest trip likely to be?
- Do you provide warm outer clothing, and is there somewhere warm to wait during a long night?
- In Iceland, who drives in winter conditions, and how do you check road and weather warnings before setting off?
- How many people are in a group, and how much of the time is spent travelling compared with watching?
