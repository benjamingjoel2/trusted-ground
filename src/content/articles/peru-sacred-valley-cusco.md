Cusco, the former capital of the Inca Empire, sits more than 3,399 metres above sea level, and most visitors arrive by plane from Lima in a little over an hour. Plan your first days around the altitude, buy the Boleto Turístico del Cusco for the city's Inca sites and the Sacred Valley ruins, and remember that Machu Picchu needs a separate ticket.

## A city built on Inca stone

PromPerú, Peru's official tourism body, calls Cusco "a living empire". Its historic centre, the Andean Baroque churches and San Pedro Market are the usual starting points. One of the city's must-sees is the Temple and Convent of Santo Domingo, built over the Inca Qorikancha. Above the city lie the ruins of Saqsaywaman and a string of smaller Inca sites.

Beyond the city, the Sacred Valley is about 1 hour 30 minutes by road from central Cusco. It is lower, at 3,274 metres according to PromPerú. Its green fields are watered by the Vilcanota River, below the Urubamba mountains. Maras, Moray, Pisac, Chinchero and Ollantaytambo are its best-known names. PromPerú gives the valley's temperatures as ranging from 1°C to 20°C, so pack for cold mornings and warm afternoons.

## Altitude: take the first days slowly

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that "altitude sickness is a serious risk in parts of Peru, including Cusco". TravelHealthPro, the UK's national travel health service, says altitude illness "usually occurs at altitudes over 2,500m". Its main symptom is headache in someone who has recently arrived at altitude, often with nausea, dizziness, fatigue or weakness.

Flying straight into Cusco means you cannot climb gradually. TravelHealthPro says that in this situation "rest days should be strongly considered before or after such large gains in elevation". Above 3,000 metres, it advises not raising your sleeping altitude by more than 500 metres a day. It also suggests a rest day every three or four days.

The lower Sacred Valley can therefore be a gentler first stop than Cusco itself. PromPerú's own advice for Cusco is to:

- take it easy on your first day and avoid strenuous activity
- drink plenty of water, even if you are not thirsty
- eat light meals
- avoid alcohol and tobacco for the first few days
- seek medical attention if symptoms persist or get worse

TravelHealthPro adds that you should always try to descend if symptoms get worse or become severe. The FCDO notes that medical treatment is harder to reach outside Lima, and the emergency number for an ambulance is 106. There is more on prevention in our guide to [altitude sickness](/articles/altitude-sickness-travel/).

## The Boleto Turístico del Cusco

Most of the region's ruins and several city museums are not ticketed one by one. Entry comes through the Boleto Turístico del Cusco (BTC), an entry fee set under Law No. 28719 and run by COSITUC. At the time of writing (November 2026), COSITUC lists these prices for foreign visitors:

- **Integral ticket (BTCI):** S/ 130, valid for 10 days, covering all three circuits and 16 sites.
- **Partial ticket, Circuit I (Saqsaywaman):** S/ 70, valid for one day. It covers Saqsaywaman, Q'enqo, Puka Pukara and Tambomachay.
- **Partial ticket, Circuit II (City and South Valley):** S/ 70, valid for two days. It covers the Qorikancha Site Museum, several city museums, and the Tipón and Pikillaqta archaeological parks.
- **Partial ticket, Circuit III (Sacred Valley):** S/ 70, valid for two days. It covers the archaeological parks of Pisac, Ollantaytambo, Chinchero and Moray.
- **Student ticket (BEE):** S/ 70, for all three circuits. Ages 10 to 17 need an identity document, and ages 18 to 25 need a valid student card or ISIC card.

COSITUC also sets these rules:

- Validity starts on the day you buy the ticket. COSITUC says covering all 16 sites takes at least three days.
- Children up to the age of nine go free.
- The ticket is personal, so carry the ID you bought it with.
- There are no online sales. Buy in person at the central counter in Galerías Turísticas, Avenida El Sol 103 (open 07:30 to 18:00), or at most of the sites themselves.
- The ticket does not include a guide or transport.

COSITUC's pages do not always agree on which sites the partial tickets cover, and prices can change. Check the current tariff on the COSITUC website before you go.

## What the ticket does not cover

Machu Picchu is not on the BTC. It has its own timed tickets and circuits, explained in our guide to [Machu Picchu tickets and the Inca Trail](/articles/machu-picchu-tickets-inca-trail/). The Maras salt pans are not on COSITUC's list of 16 sites either. Neither is the Qorikancha church and convent: only the Qorikancha Site Museum appears on the BTC list. Check entry arrangements for anything not on the list locally.

## Making sense of the valley

The Sacred Valley rewards more than a single day trip. Pisac pairs an archaeological park with a handicraft market. Ollantaytambo, Chinchero and Moray each have their own archaeological park, and Circuit III's two-day validity lets you spread visits out. PromPerú also suggests cycling around Maras and Moray, riding in the valley or trying chicha, the local corn drink. It recommends bringing a camera, sunscreen and cash.

## Before you go

- Plan one or two easy days on arrival, and think about sleeping in the lower Sacred Valley first.
- Carry the passport or ID you used to buy the Boleto Turístico.
- Have cash in soles for the ticket and for anything outside it, such as the Maras salt pans.
- Book Machu Picchu separately and early. The BTC does not include it.
- Check that your travel insurance covers medical treatment and evacuation at altitude.
- Check the latest COSITUC tariff and FCDO health advice shortly before you travel.
