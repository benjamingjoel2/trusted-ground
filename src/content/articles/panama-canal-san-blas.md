Panama packs three very different worlds into a short trip. In one you watch container ships rise and fall in the locks of the Panama Canal. In another you wander the cobbled plazas of Casco Viejo, the colonial old quarter of Panama City. In the third you take a small boat out to the palm-fringed islands of Guna Yala, an autonomous Indigenous territory on the Caribbean coast. Each is easy to reach from the capital, but each has its own rules and practicalities.

## The canal: watching ships at Miraflores

The Panama Canal opened on 15 August 1914, when the SS *Ancon* passed from the Atlantic to the Pacific. According to the Panama Canal Authority (ACP), the canal has run 24 hours a day since May 1963, when lighting was installed at the locks and the Culebra Cut. By 2021 it had handled more than 1.1 million transits.

The easiest place to see it at work is the Miraflores Visitor Center on the edge of Panama City. At the time of writing (November 2026), the ACP gives these details:

- **Hours.** The centre is open daily from 8am to 6pm, and the ticket office from 8am to 5pm. A visit takes about an hour and a half.
- **Prices for non-residents.** Adults pay B/.17.22 and children aged 6 to 12 pay B/.7.22. Under-6s go free.
- **What is included.** A ground-level terrace and raised stands overlook the locks. The ticket also covers a 45-minute 3D film about the canal's history in the IMAX theatre.
- **Ship times vary.** The ACP publishes a daily schedule of when ships pass Miraflores, so check it before you go.

On the Caribbean side near Colón, the ACP also runs the Agua Clara Visitor Center. Back in the city, the Amador Causeway was built in 1913 from rock dug out of the Culebra Cut. It links the mainland to the islands of Naos, Perico, Culebra and Flamenco and makes a good walk or cycle.

## Casco Viejo: the old quarter

Locals call Panama City's historic district Casco Antiguo, or simply "El Casco". According to the national tourism board, Visit Panamá, the city was founded on this site in 1673 by settlers whose earlier city, now the ruins of Panamá Viejo, had been attacked by pirates. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site of cobbled streets and stone buildings mixing Spanish and French colonial styles, and it is best explored on foot.

Visit Panamá suggests a walk from plaza to plaza:

- **Plaza Santa Ana.** The suggested starting point, home to the long-established Café Coca-Cola, a historic meeting place for politicians and revolutionaries.
- **Plaza Herrera.** Lined with restaurants, and close to the Mola Museum, which explains the textile art of the Guna people.
- **Plaza de Francia.** Its monument honours France's part in building the canal.
- **Paseo Esteban Huertas.** A walkway along the old sea wall that once protected the city from pirates, with views of the modern skyline and the bay.
- **Plaza de la Independencia.** Also called Plaza Catedral or Plaza Mayor. Here you will find the Cathedral Basilica of Santa María la Antigua and the Interoceanic Canal Museum.

The Casco is also one of the city's food and nightlife hubs, with specialty cafés, craft breweries and rooftop bars. Parking is scarce, so take a taxi, a ride-hailing car or the C982 bus to Plaza Herrera.

## Guna Yala: the San Blas islands

Guna Yala, also known as San Blas, is an archipelago of more than 300 islands, many small and uninhabited. Visit Panamá describes it as part of an autonomous Indigenous region inhabited by the Guna people, who have kept their traditions and way of life. The best-known Guna art is the mola, a cloth made from layers of colourful fabric with designs inspired by Guna beliefs.

Expect simple comforts. Visit Panamá says there are no big hotels or chains. Accommodation ranges from hammocks and campsites to small cabins without electricity or running water, plus a few more comfortable over-water cabins and hotels. Some visitors sleep aboard sailing boats and hop between islands.

To get there, take a four-wheel-drive vehicle from Panama City to Cartí, the main port. The trip through the central mountains takes about two and a half hours. From Cartí, small boats take you to the islands. Because this is Guna territory, treat villages as people's homes: ask before taking photographs and follow your hosts' rules.

## Safety and practicalities

The FCDO advises taking sensible precautions against mugging in tourist areas, and it names both Casco Viejo and the Panamá Viejo ruins. It also recommends ride-hailing apps or hotel-arranged transport rather than street taxis. Foreign nationals must carry ID such as a passport, and police usually accept a printed copy.

On the water, the FCDO warns that some beaches have strong currents and few warning signs. It advises against swimming in the Bay of Panama because of pollution. October and November have the heaviest rainfall.

For Guna Yala, note the FCDO's advice on the Colombia border region. It advises travelling east of a line that runs from Punta Carreto in the Comarca de Guna Yala, via Yaviza, to Punta Piña only in an organised group, and making sure the local police know about your visit. Check where your island lies in relation to this line before you book. For more on the country, see our [Panama travel guide](/countries/panama/).

## Before you go

- Check the ACP's daily transit schedule so you reach Miraflores when ships are passing.
- Ask your Guna Yala operator exactly which island you will stay on, what is included (transfers, meals, boat trips) and which charges are paid on arrival.
- Pack cash, a torch and light layers for islands with limited electricity.
- Carry your passport or a printed copy at all times.
- Use ride-hailing apps or hotel transport in Panama City, especially at night.
- Visit the Mola Museum in Casco Viejo before going to Guna Yala to understand what you are seeing and buying.
