Marble-topped tables, Thonet chairs, the day's newspapers and a waiter arriving with a small silver tray: the Viennese coffee house is less a café than a public living room. In 2011 UNESCO recognised Viennese coffee house culture as intangible cultural heritage, according to Austria's national tourism board. The short version for visitors: order a coffee, accept the glass of water that comes with it, and do not feel you need to hurry.

## A tradition born in the 17th century

Austria's national tourism board tells the story this way: after the Ottoman army was defeated at Vienna in 1683, sacks of unfamiliar beans were reportedly found in its abandoned camp, and by 1685 coffee houses were opening across the city. It names Café Frauenhuber as Vienna's oldest coffee house still operating, with guests recorded from 1824, and says that around the 1910s the city had about 600 coffee houses.

The Vienna Tourist Board says coffee house culture peaked at the end of the 19th century, when people came to read, play, debate and write. It quotes Bertolt Brecht's line that Vienna "is built around a few coffeehouses where the population sits together and reads papers", and the official explanation of the UNESCO recognition, which describes coffee houses as places "where patrons consume time and space, but only the coffee appears on the bill".

## What to order

Viennese cafés offer more than 40 coffee varieties, according to the Vienna Tourist Board. A few of the classics, as the tourism boards define them:

- **Kleiner Schwarzer**: a simple mocha in a small cup.
- **Melange**: coffee with warm milk and milk foam, roughly half and half; the Vienna Tourist Board notes it tastes milder than a cappuccino.
- **Verlängerter**: a small black coffee lengthened with the same amount of hot water, close to an Americano.
- **Einspänner**: mocha served in a glass and topped with plenty of whipped cream. The name points to coachmen, whose cream kept their coffee warm.
- **Kapuziner**: a small mocha with a few drops of cream.
- **Franziskaner**: a small mocha with hot water, hot milk and whipped cream.

Coffee is only half of it. The Vienna Tourist Board picks out Gugelhupf and Sachertorte among the signature cakes, and many cafés also serve hearty dishes such as Fiakergulasch and Sacher sausages.

## The glass of water and other customs

Your coffee will usually come on a small silver tray with a glass of water. The Vienna Tourist Board explains that the water was first served so guests had somewhere to put their spoon, and later became a way of showing the coffee was made with clean mountain spring water, after the First Vienna Mountain Spring Pipeline opened in 1873.

Other things to expect:

- **Lingering is normal.** Guests traditionally stay for long stretches, alone or in company, and nobody expects you to leave once your cup is empty.
- **Newspapers are part of the furniture.** Many traditional cafés, such as Café Jelinek and Café Sperl, provide free newspapers.
- **Outdoor seating.** In summer many cafés set up a Schanigarten, a terrace on the street.
- **Music.** Several concert cafés offer live music, and some traditional houses have piano music on certain days.

## Where to go

The Vienna Tourist Board lists a number of traditional houses, each with its own character:

- **Café Sperl** (Gumpendorfer Straße 11), opened in 1880, with furnishings little changed since, billiard tables and Sunday piano music.
- **Café Hawelka** (Dorotheergasse 6), a long-time meeting place for artists, known for its Buchteln (baked yeast buns).
- **Café Korb** (Brandstätte 9), dating from 1904, with all-day breakfast.
- **Café Schwarzenberg** (Kärntner Ring 17), described as Vienna's oldest Ringstrasse café.
- **Café Central** (Herrengasse), opened in 1876 in Palais Ferstel. At the time of writing it had been closed for renovation since March 2026, with reopening planned for winter 2026 and a temporary pop-up at Palais Harrach; check before you go.
- **Café Jelinek** (Otto-Bauer-Gasse 5), with a wrought-iron stove and homemade Gugelhupf.

The tourism board also points out Café Landtmann, which opened on the Ringstrasse in 1873, and Demel, where the tradition of the Demlerinnen, women servers in black and white uniforms, goes back some 200 years.

## Before you go

- Allow plenty of time: lingering over a single coffee is part of the tradition, not an imposition.
- Learn a few drink names (Melange, Verlängerter, Einspänner) so you can order what you actually want.
- Expect a glass of water with your coffee: it is a mark of a traditional Viennese café.
- Leave room for cake: Gugelhupf and Sachertorte are coffee house staples.
- Check opening hours and any renovation closures before you set out, especially for Café Central.
