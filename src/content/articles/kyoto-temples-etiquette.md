Kyoto's temples and shrines are working religious sites set among homes, and the city asks visitors to treat them that way: touch little, talk quietly, follow photography signs and stay off private property. Most temples keep daytime hours, with special evening openings in spring and autumn. To avoid the worst crowds, go before 9am or after 6pm, or head out to the quieter districts on the city's edge.

## A city of more than a thousand sacred sites

According to the Kyoto City Tourism Association, Kyoto has more than a thousand shrines and temples. The city also holds around 20 per cent of Japan's National Treasures and 15 per cent of its Important Cultural Properties. Some of them are famous worldwide. Others are small neighbourhood places tucked between houses, and in both cases people come to pray as well as to look.

That mix is part of the appeal. A visitor can climb a hillside temple veranda in the morning, then end the day walking a lantern-lit lane in Gion. It is also why the city's guidance is mostly about consideration for the people who live and worship there.

## Etiquette at temples, shrines and in the old streets

The tourism association's responsible travel guidance and its "4 guidelines" notice set out what is expected:

- **Touch as little as possible.** Do not touch heritage structures more than you need to, and carry your backpack on your front in crowded or narrow places.
- **Take your shoes off when asked.** Some halls and buildings must be entered in socks, so wear shoes that come off easily.
- **Check before you photograph.** Photography is banned in many parts of Gion, in parts of temples and shrines, and in some restaurants. The city says photography is only allowed in designated areas.
- **Keep your voice down.** Walk and talk quietly in residential areas and at religious sites.
- **Stay off private property** and do not block lanes by stopping to chat or take photos, or by walking side by side in large groups.
- **Leave geiko and maiko alone.** Do not stop, touch, follow or photograph them without permission. The city stresses that they are not tourist attractions. Hotels or licensed guides can point you to public performances.
- **Never carve the bamboo.** Carving on bamboo is property damage and a criminal offence, with fines of up to 300,000 yen. The city names Arashiyama and Fushimi Inari as places where it has happened.
- **Bin your rubbish and smoke only in designated areas.** The city warns that breaking its rules may result in fines.

At Kiyomizu-dera, the temple's own guidance on prayer is simple: put your hands together in front of the statue of Kannon. If you are unsure what to do at any site, watch for signs and follow what worshippers around you are doing.

## Opening hours and night viewings

There is no single timetable. Each temple and shrine sets its own hours, and many change them for special events. Two examples:

- **Kinkaku-ji** (the Golden Pavilion, part of Shokoku-ji) lists opening hours of 9am to 5pm.
- **Kiyomizu-dera** holds special night viewings in spring, summer and autumn. For autumn 2026, the temple lists night viewing from 21 to 30 November, open until 9.30pm with last entry at 9pm. A special viewing of its Jojuin garden runs over the same dates.

The tourism association notes that many temples light up their autumn foliage at night. These illuminations are popular, so check dates on each temple's official website rather than relying on last year's.

Some places need booking. The association's reservation guide says Saiho-ji (the Moss Temple) requires reservations, and recommends booking the Sento Imperial Palace and the Katsura and Shugakuin imperial villas, where reservations open at 5am on the first day of the month, three months ahead. Some temples also require reservations only during the autumn foliage season. In 2026, Ruriko-in required them for part of its autumn opening, and Enko-ji required them from 10 November to 10 December.

## Finding quieter times and places

The tourism association treats anything before 9am as "morning sightseeing" and anything after 6pm as "night sightseeing". It publishes a map of places that open by 9am or stay open after 6pm. Daylight shapes how much of this you can use. The association puts summer sunrise at around 5am and sunset at around 7pm, against roughly 7am and 5pm in winter.

It also runs a Kyoto Travel Congestion Forecast that estimates how busy popular spots will be and suggests places that stay relatively empty even in the daytime. Its YouTube channel has live cameras you can check before setting out. Leaving your luggage at your hotel or a storage service before sightseeing also helps ease congestion, the city says.

The association names Kiyomizudera Monzen (the approach to Kiyomizu-dera), Nishiki Market and Arashiyama as places that draw large numbers of visitors. For calmer temple visits, it points to six outlying districts:

- **Ohara**, with ancient temples and gardens in a rural setting
- **Takao**, with temples on three mountains known together as the Sanbi, and famous for autumn leaves
- **Yamashina**, a district dotted with temples
- **Fushimi**, a canal-side sake-brewing town
- **Nishikyo**, in western Kyoto, with quiet bamboo groves
- **Keihoku**, a rural area known for farm stays

## Season matters too

The tourism association calls spring and autumn the most comfortable times to visit, and April's cherry blossom is especially loved. That makes both seasons busy. Summer can top 35°C, the rainy season runs from early June to mid-July, and winter can drop below 5°C. Each has its own attractions, from hydrangeas in the rain to ryokan stays in winter. ## Before you go

- Check each temple's official website for current hours, fees and night viewing dates.
- Book ahead for Saiho-ji, the imperial villas and any autumn reservation-only openings.
- Wear shoes you can slip off easily, and carry a small bag you can wear on your front.
- Look at the Kyoto Travel Congestion Forecast the evening before, and plan popular sites for before 9am.
- Look for photography signs before raising a camera, especially in Gion.
- Use taxis with green number plates or official ride-hailing apps. The city warns against unlicensed white-plate taxis.
