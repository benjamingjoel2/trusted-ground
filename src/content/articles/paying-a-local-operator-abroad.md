If you are booking from the UK, paying a local operator directly by credit card usually gives you the most ways to get money back if things go wrong. A debit card gives you a weaker fallback. An international bank transfer or a money-transfer app may give you none. Whichever you use, check that the operator is properly licensed before you pay anything. This guide uses Kenya as its example, but the payment rules are the same wherever you book.

## Credit cards: Section 75

Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 lets you claim against your credit card provider if the supplier breaches the contract or misrepresents what it sold you. According to the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS) and Citizens Advice:

- It applies when the **cash price** of the goods or service is more than £100 and no more than £30,000.
- You only need to have paid **part** of the cost by credit card. A deposit on the card can bring the whole price within Section 75.
- There must be a direct link between you, the card provider and the supplier (the law calls this a "debtor-creditor-supplier" agreement). Citizens Advice warns that you usually cannot use Section 75 if you paid through a third party, such as a marketplace or a wallet like PayPal, rather than paying the trader directly.

For a trip abroad, that last point matters most. If your card payment goes to a booking platform, agent or payment intermediary rather than to the operator delivering the trip, Section 75 may not apply. Ask whose name will appear on your card statement before you pay.

Section 75 does not cover debit cards, charge cards or payments funded by an overdraft or ordinary bank loan.

## Debit cards: chargeback

Chargeback follows the rules of the card schemes (Visa, Mastercard and American Express), and each scheme's rules are different. The FOS explains that your bank does not have to raise one, although doing so can be good practice when there are valid reasons, such as a service that was never provided or was not as described.

The time limit matters for trips booked months ahead. The FOS says you usually have around 120 days to raise a chargeback, counted from when you expected the service or from when you received something faulty or not as described. Limits can be longer or shorter, so ask your bank. Citizens Advice notes that a chargeback only covers what you paid on that card, and that the trader can challenge the refund afterwards.

Chargeback is also available on credit card payments that fall outside Section 75.

## Bank transfers and money-transfer services

Many operators abroad ask for a deposit by international bank transfer, and in some countries by mobile money. These are the hardest payments to get back.

- The FOS warns that if you used a payment method that Section 75 and chargeback do not apply to, your bank may not be able to help.
- The UK's reimbursement scheme for authorised push payment (APP) fraud, where someone tricks you into sending money, covers payments made by Faster Payments or CHAPS **from one UK bank account to another**, according to the Payment Systems Regulator. An international transfer to an operator's overseas account falls outside that scheme.

If you do pay by transfer, confirm the bank details by phone using a number you found yourself, not one taken from an email. Be wary of any message telling you the operator's bank details have changed.

## Packages, ATOL and insurance

If you book a package that includes a flight through a UK travel company, you may have ATOL protection. The UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) says you should receive an ATOL Certificate. If the company fails while you are abroad, ATOL helps you continue your trip or reimburses you for replacing the protected parts. ATOL is not travel insurance, and it does not cover a flight bought on its own where you receive an e-ticket straight away.

Booking ground arrangements directly with a local operator usually means no ATOL protection for that part of the trip, so your card protections and insurance matter more. The FOS notes that you can still ask your card provider about Section 75 or chargeback where ATOL or ABTA cover does not apply.

## Check who you are paying

Payment protection works best when you are paying a real, accountable business. In Kenya, the Tourism Regulatory Authority (TRA) licenses tourism businesses, including safari operators, and says licensing helps keep out unscrupulous operators. Ask for the operator's licence details and check them yourself.

Once you arrive, the FCDO advises against carrying large sums of cash in Kenya and warns of thieves posing as police or security guards. Settling as much as possible in advance by a traceable method also cuts down how much cash you need to carry.

If a dispute with your card provider is not resolved, you can complain to them formally. If they do not send a final response within eight weeks, or you are unhappy with it, you can take the complaint to the FOS.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Can I pay the deposit and balance by credit card directly to your company, and whose name will appear on my statement?
- If you only accept bank transfer, why, and how will you confirm your bank details to me other than by email?
- What is your licence number, and which authority issued it?
- When is the balance due, and what exactly do I get back if I cancel, or if you cancel, at each stage?
- Do you charge a card surcharge or currency fee, and in which currency will I be billed?
- Will I get a written confirmation itemising everything I have paid for before I send any money?
