Almost every Antarctic trip is a ship-based expedition that runs during the austral summer, roughly October to April. The size of the ship decides whether you will set foot on the continent at all, and membership of the International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators (IAATO) tells you the operator has signed up to an agreed set of safety and environmental standards.

## What IAATO is, and what membership means

IAATO is a trade association founded in 1991 by seven private tour operators. According to IAATO, it now has more than 100 member companies and organisations. It works within the Antarctic Treaty System and attends the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meeting (ATCM) as an invited expert.

Membership comes in two main types:

- **Operators** run their own programmes in Antarctica and have met IAATO's membership and operational requirements. New operators that have not yet met all of them can hold **Provisional Operator** status.
- **Associates** do not run trips themselves. They include travel agents and tour operators that book customers onto an Operator member's voyage.

IAATO says its member operators coordinate itineraries before the season, take part in its vessel tracking system, have insurance and contingency plans in place and hire bridge officers with suitable experience. It also expects members' expedition teams to be made up of people of whom at least 80% have relevant experience and 50% have previous Antarctic experience.

IAATO points out that not every company taking visitors to Antarctica is a member, although the vast majority are. You can check a company in IAATO's online member directory. If you book through an agent, check which Operator member actually runs the voyage.

## Who regulates Antarctic tourism

Antarctica has no single government. The Antarctic Treaty, in force since 1961, designates the continent as a natural reserve devoted to peace and science, and the Treaty Parties regulate tourism. According to IAATO, every activity has to go through an environmental impact assessment by a national competent authority, and member operators apply for authorisation every year.

You do not need a visa for Antarctica itself, but you may need one for the country you sail from. IAATO notes that most voyages leave from southern South America, for example from Ushuaia in Argentina or Punta Arenas in Chile, and a smaller number go to the Ross Sea from Australia or New Zealand.

## When the season runs

The Antarctic Treaty Secretariat says about 98% of tourism voyages go to the Antarctic Peninsula during the seven-month austral summer, from October to April. Most visitors travel by ship from South American ports, which are a few days' sailing away. IAATO adds that many Peninsula voyages also visit the Falkland Islands (Malvinas) and South Georgia.

Your operator can explain how conditions and itineraries change through the season. Also ask about IAATO's avian influenza protocols, which are published for each season and can affect wildlife visits.

## Ship size and landings

IAATO groups its members' vessels by passenger capacity:

- **C1:** traditional expedition ships carrying 13 to 200 passengers, which make landings
- **C2:** mid-size vessels carrying 201 to 500 passengers, which make landings
- **CR:** vessels carrying more than 500 passengers, which do not make landings (cruise only)
- **YA:** sailing or motor yachts carrying up to 12 passengers

The Treaty Parties' General Guidelines for Visitors set the core landing rules:

- Ships with more than 500 passengers may not make landings in Antarctica.
- Only one ship may visit a site at any one time.
- No more than 100 passengers from a vessel may be ashore at any one time, and fewer where site-specific guidance requires it.
- Landings must keep a ratio of one guide to every 20 passengers, and more guides where site-specific advice requires it.

In practice, on a larger ship only part of the passenger list can be ashore at once, so passengers land in rotation. Ask how many landings are planned and how long each group usually spends ashore. The Treaty Secretariat notes that the ATCM updated the General Guidelines in 2025 (Resolution 4), so your operator should brief you on the current version.

## Rules you will follow ashore

IAATO's visitor guidance repeats the Treaty rules:

- Do not feed, touch or handle birds or seals, and do not approach them in a way that changes their behaviour.
- Do not walk on moss beds or lichen-covered slopes.
- Do not take anything away, including rocks, bones, eggs, fossils or anything from buildings.
- Follow the instructions of your expedition leaders and stay with your group.
- Do not expect a rescue service. IAATO stresses self-sufficiency and planning.

Biosecurity matters too. Under IAATO's "Don't Pack a Pest" guidance, you will usually be asked to clean your boots, clothing and bags so that no seeds or soil come ashore. IAATO members have also agreed not to allow recreational drone use in Antarctica's coastal areas.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Are you an IAATO Operator member, or are you an agent booking me onto another company's voyage? If so, which one?
- What category is the ship, how many passengers does it carry, and how many landings do you plan?
- With the 100-passengers-ashore limit, how is time ashore shared out, and how long does each group typically get?
- Which port do we sail from, and do I need a visa or onward travel documents for that country?
- What insurance do you require, and does it need to cover medical evacuation from Antarctica? (See our insurance guide.)
- What happens to my deposit and balance if the voyage is cancelled or the itinerary changes because of weather or ice? (See deposits and payment protection.)
