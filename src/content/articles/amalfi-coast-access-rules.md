Pastel houses stacked up sheer cliffs, domed churches tiled in majolica, lemon terraces dropping to small bays: the Amalfi Coast is one of Italy's most famous landscapes, and in summer it is also one of its most congested. A single narrow road links the towns, traffic on it is regulated by local ordinances, and historic centres restrict cars. The calmest way to see the coast is to leave the car behind, use buses and boats, and consider the quieter winter months.

## The coast at a glance

Italy's national tourist board, Italia.it, describes the Amalfi Coast as overlooking the Gulf of Salerno and stretching from Positano to Vietri sul Mare. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997, recognised as a cultural landscape shaped by people and nature, with sheer cliffs and "terraces planted with vines, olive and citrus trees".

The coast takes in 16 municipalities in the province of Salerno, among them Amalfi, Atrani, Cetara, Conca dei Marini, Furore, Maiori, Minori, Praiano, Positano, Ravello, Scala, Tramonti and Vietri sul Mare. Each has its own character, according to Italia.it:

- **Amalfi** was a maritime republic from the 9th to the 11th century; its cathedral of St Andrew dominates Piazza Duomo.
- **Atrani**, about 700 metres from Amalfi, is billed as Italy's smallest town.
- **Ravello** is home to Villa Cimbrone and its Terrace of Infinity.
- **Vietri sul Mare** is known for its ceramics, and **Cetara** is a small fishing village.

The local lemon, the elongated Sfusato Amalfitano, has Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) status. For wider travel planning, see our [Italy guide](/countries/italy/).

## Driving: what to check before you go

The coast road, the SS163 Amalfitana, is the only road link between most of the towns. It is narrow and winding, and in busy periods traffic on it has been regulated by official ordinances: the Comune di Amalfi, for example, published an ordinance regulating traffic on the SS163 in 2019. Measures, dates and hours have varied from year to year, and at the time of writing (November 2026) we could not confirm the current season's rules from an official source.

What that means in practice:

- **Check the current rules before you drive.** Look for the latest SS163 ordinance on the websites of ANAS, Italy's state road company, and the coastal municipalities, or ask your hotel or car-hire company.
- **Watch for ZTL signs.** The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that you need a pass to drive into the historic centre of many Italian towns. Limited traffic zones (ZTL) are marked with black letters on a yellow background, and if you enter without a pass, your number plate may be photographed and you can be fined.
- **Bring the right documents.** The FCDO says a UK photocard licence is accepted in Italy, but hire companies may set stricter rules, such as minimum age or requiring an international driving permit.

## Buses and boats

If you leave the car behind, public transport is the obvious alternative. The regional fare consortium, UnicoCampania, sets public transport fares in Campania and sells tickets for its member operators through ticket offices, authorised retailers and the Unico Campania app. Check with the operator which ticket covers your route before you board.

The sea is the other highway. Italia.it notes that small boats leave the Amalfi pier for Spiaggia del Duoglio, and that Santa Croce beach near Amalfi can only be reached by sea. If you plan to hop between towns by ferry, check the operator's current timetable before you travel and again on the day, and have a back-up plan in case sailings are cancelled.

## On foot: the Path of the Gods

Some of the coast is best seen by walking. The Sentiero degli Dei (Path of the Gods) runs for about 9 to 10 kilometres along the Monti Lattari ridge from Agerola to the upper part of Positano, according to Italia.it, which calls it "one of the most beautiful nature trails in the world". Between Maiori and Minori, the Sentiero dei Limoni (Lemon Path) threads through the citrus terraces. Carry water and start early in warm weather.

## The quieter months

If crowds and traffic put you off, Italia.it makes the case for winter. It describes "undisturbed peace, mild temperatures and affordable prices", with hotels cheaper than in summer and driving easier without summer traffic. It says the coast enjoys mild temperatures "especially until the end of the holiday season", and that "quiet reigns everywhere" in December and January.

The Christmas season brings its own events: Amalfi's streets and cathedral façade are lit up, Ravello hosts concerts at the Oscar Niemeyer Auditorium, Atrani is decked in coloured lights, and Minori holds folk-group parades. Not every hotel, restaurant or boat service operates year-round, so check opening dates before you book.

UK visitors can stay in the Schengen area, which includes Italy, for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa, according to the FCDO, and the EU's Entry/Exit System is being implemented at Schengen borders. ## Before you go

- Check the current SS163 traffic ordinance on the ANAS or municipal websites before driving, and confirm the dates and hours that apply.
- Ask your hotel about parking and whether it sits inside a ZTL.
- Buy bus tickets before boarding and check which ticket covers your route.
- Check ferry timetables before you go and again on the day of travel.
- In winter, confirm that hotels, restaurants and boat services you plan to use are open.
