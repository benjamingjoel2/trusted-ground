Most visits to the Seychelles take in three granite islands: Mahé, the largest, home to the capital Victoria; Praslin, home of the Vallée de Mai palm forest; and little La Digue, where bicycles and walking are the way to get around. You need a travel authorisation before you fly, and boats link the islands. Plan your beach days around the monsoon winds, which make different coasts unsafe at different times of year.

## Before you fly: the travel authorisation

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) says British visitors do not need a visa, but must apply and pay for a travel authorisation online before travelling. When you apply you need to show that you meet the entry requirements, including:

- A valid return or onward ticket
- Proof of confirmed accommodation
- Enough money for your stay

At the border you may be asked to show proof of travel insurance covering health costs. If you are carrying cash worth 50,000 Seychellois rupees or more, you must declare it on your travel authorisation application. Other nationalities should check the rules that apply to them.

## Mahé: mountains and Victoria

Mahé is mountainous, and the FCDO warns that its roads are narrow and winding, often with sheer drops and hairpin bends. Inland, Morne Seychellois National Park is, according to the Ministry of Environment, the largest land-based park in the country, covering 3,045 hectares. A trail network runs through it, and trail leaflets are sold at the national parks head office and at an information kiosk in the Victoria Botanical Gardens.

The FCDO advises particular care around Beau Vallon, the back streets of Victoria and isolated areas at night. Crime is generally non-violent, but bag snatching and break-ins at cars, boats and guest houses do happen, so use a hotel safe and keep valuables out of sight.

## Praslin: the Vallée de Mai

Praslin's highlight is the Vallée de Mai, a 19.5-hectare palm forest inscribed as a natural World Heritage Site in December 1983. The Seychelles Islands Foundation, which manages it, calls it one of the world's smallest natural World Heritage Sites. It is the stronghold of the coco de mer palm, found only on Praslin and Curieuse, which can grow taller than 25 metres and bears what the foundation calls "the largest seed in the plant kingdom", weighing up to 20 kg. It is also home to the Seychelles black parrot, the national bird, found only on Praslin.

At the time of writing (November 2026), the foundation lists:

- **Opening hours:** daily 8:30 to 16:30, last entry 15:30, closed on 25 December and 1 January
- **Entrance fee:** SCR 450, which also supports the management of Aldabra Atoll
- **Rules:** the whole site is a strict no-smoking zone

Walking routes in the forest range from about 1.5 to 4 km.

## La Digue: bicycles and beaches

A Ministry of Tourism study describes La Digue as the fourth-largest granitic island, about 10 square kilometres in area and lying east of Praslin. There is no airport, apart from a helicopter landing strip, so almost everyone arrives by ferry at the jetty at La Passe. The study notes the island's tradition of walking, cycling and ox-carts, and its policy of limiting conventional motor vehicles. Most day trippers visit L'Union Estate and Anse Source d'Argent, and the study records heavy pressure on these sites, especially on cruise-ship days between October and April.

Two protected areas are worth fitting in:

- **Veuve Special Reserve.** A 21-hectare reserve at Anse Réunion protecting the Seychelles black paradise flycatcher. The Seychelles Parks and Gardens Authority lists opening hours of 8am to 4pm Monday to Friday and 9am to 3pm on Saturday. At the time of writing, entry for non-citizens is Rs150, and a guided tour is Rs200.
- **Ile Cocos Marine Park.** Three small islets about ten minutes by boat from La Digue, visited on trips arranged by tour operators and boat charters from Praslin or La Digue. Entry for non-residents over 12 is Rs200. The authority says hawksbill turtles are the main attraction, and that whale sharks are sometimes seen from about August to November.

## Ferries, boats and the sea

The FCDO notes that most inner-island resorts can be reached by ferry, and that domestic flights and ferries can be disrupted by bad weather. On any boat trip, listen to the safety briefing and make sure life jackets are provided, especially on smaller excursion boats.

For swimming, the FCDO explains how the seasons affect each coast. The west coast is generally unsafe during the north-west monsoon, around December to March. The east coast is unsafe during the south-east winds, around May to September. Beaches at the southern tip are not suitable for swimming at any time, and Beau Vallon and some other beaches can have dangerous rip currents when the sea is rough. The FCDO also warns that Seychelles has strict drug laws, including for possession in transit. For more planning detail, see the [Seychelles guide](/countries/seychelles/).

## Before you go

- Apply for your travel authorisation online before you fly, with your accommodation confirmed.
- Book inter-island ferries in advance and allow slack for weather disruption.
- Check which coast is sheltered in your travel months before choosing a beach.
- Carry cash or a card for park fees at the Vallée de Mai, Veuve Reserve and Ile Cocos.
- Ask boat operators about life jackets and safety briefings before you board.
- Leave valuables in a hotel safe and avoid isolated areas after dark.
