To visit Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park you need a park pass, which is easiest to buy online before you arrive. Climbing Uluṟu has been banned since October 2019. A respectful visit also means following the requests of the park's Traditional Owners, the Aṉangu: stay on marked tracks, keep out of restricted areas and don't photograph sacred sites.

## A living cultural landscape

Uluṟu rises from the red sands of Central Australia. Parks Australia's photography page notes that the rock looks moody on overcast days, shiny when wet and red at sunset. The park is jointly managed with its Traditional Owners, the Yankunytjatjara and Pitjantjatjara people, who call themselves Aṉangu (pronounced arn-ung-oo) and ask visitors to use that word too. The park is on the UNESCO World Heritage List for both its natural and cultural values.

At the heart of Aṉangu life is Tjukurpa: traditional law, stories and spirituality. In the words the park publishes from Aṉangu, Uluṟu and Kata Tjuṯa "were formed and shaped by our creation ancestors", who "left marks in the land and made laws for us to keep and live by". This explains many of the park's rules. In some places, features of the rock carry knowledge that is not meant to be seen or shared elsewhere.

## Park passes

According to Parks Australia, at the time of writing (November 2026):

- **Adults (18 and over):** AUD 38 for a three-day pass, or AUD 50 for an annual pass.
- **Children and teenagers under 18:** free.
- **Northern Territory-registered vehicles:** a separate annual vehicle pass is available to residents.

You can print your pass or save it to your phone, then scan the QR code at the entry station. If you can't buy one online, you can buy one at the entry station. The pass covers this park only. Kakadu needs its own pass, and other Northern Territory parks are managed by the NT Government.

Parks Australia says all pass revenue goes back into the park. A quarter goes directly to Aṉangu through lease payments that support homelands, training and business development. The rest pays for operations, ranger and cultural programmes, and natural and cultural preservation work such as rock art conservation.

## Why the climb closed

The Uluṟu climb closed permanently on 26 October 2019. Parks Australia explains that Uluṟu has been sacred to Aṉangu for tens of thousands of years, and climbing it "was not generally permitted under Tjukurpa".

Key dates from the park's account:

- **Late 1930s:** visitors begin climbing Uluṟu.
- **1964:** the first section of the climb chain is installed.
- **1985:** the park is handed back to Aṉangu. They allow the climb to continue for the time being, while making clear they don't like people climbing.
- **1990s:** signs at the base ask visitors, on behalf of Aṉangu, "Please Don't Climb".
- **November 2017:** the Board of Management votes unanimously to close the climb. One of its agreed criteria was that the share of visitors climbing had fallen below 20%.
- **26 October 2019:** the climb closes, on the 34th anniversary of the handback.

Climbing Uluṟu is now a breach of the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation (EPBC) Act, and the park says penalties will be issued. A former chairman of the Board of Management, Sammy Wilson, described the closure as "not something to feel upset about but a cause for celebration."

## Photography and sacred sites

Photography is welcome in most of the park, with some clear limits. Parks Australia says the rock details at culturally sensitive sites "are equivalent to sacred scripture for Aṉangu". Photographing these sites is prohibited, and they are marked by signs and on the park's maps.

- **The north-east face** runs from Kaṉtju Gorge to Kuniya Piti. It is one of the most sensitive areas, and it is also the side you see from Ayers Rock Resort and from commercial flights. Aṉangu ask that you take only wide shots from a distance. The park suggests shooting 1 to 2 hours before sunset, when natural shadows hide the sacred details.
- **People:** always ask before photographing Aṉangu.
- **Drones** are prohibited in the park under the EPBC Act.
- **Commercial use:** influencers, bloggers, artists and other content creators need a media permit.

## Everyday courtesy

Parks Australia's guide to respecting culture suggests a few simple habits:

- Greet people with "palya". The word can mean hello, welcome, thank you, OK and goodbye.
- Don't read silence as being ignored. In Aṉangu culture it is important to listen closely and think carefully before answering.
- Some people may find constant eye contact uncomfortable.
- Stay on marked tracks, and don't climb onto rock formations or boulders. This ensures you don't wander into sacred areas.
- Don't enter or photograph restricted areas, including sacred and ceremonial sites, burial grounds and people's homes.

A good place to start is the Cultural Centre, on the main road to Uluṟu, 10 to 15 minutes' drive from the entry station. Parks Australia recommends allowing at least two hours there. The centre has exhibits and presentations with Aṉangu and rangers, the Tjukurpa Tunnel and two galleries of Aṉangu art, Maruku Arts and Walkatjara Art. It is open daily from 7am to 5.45pm.

## Before you go

- Buy your park pass online and save it to your phone or print it.
- Plan around the heat. The park says May to September is the most comfortable time to visit. From October to March, daytime temperatures regularly exceed 35°C, and it advises not walking after 11am in summer.
- Make the Cultural Centre your first stop, and pick up a visitor guide at the information desk.
- Check the signs and maps for culturally sensitive sites before you take photos, and leave the drone at home.
- If you plan to share images commercially, apply for a media permit first.
