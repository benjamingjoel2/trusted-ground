Most of Nepal's popular trekking routes need several pieces of paperwork. These are a TIMS card, an entry permit for the national park or conservation area you walk through and, in restricted zones, a special permit from the Department of Immigration. Since 31 March 2023, trekkers on the main routes in protected areas must be accompanied by a licensed guide, arranged through a government-registered trekking agency. In practice, a trek in Nepal now starts with choosing that agency.

## The guide rule

According to the Nepal Tourism Board (NTB), under the "Revised TIMS Provision effective from March 31, 2023", trekkers entering specific protected areas must be accompanied by a licensed trekking guide. The NTB lists the affected regions and treks. They include:

- Everest, Rolwaling and Makalu Barun
- Annapurna, Mustang, Dhaulagiri and Manaslu
- Langtang, Helambu, Ganesh Himal-Ruby Valley and Panchpokhari Bhairabkunda
- Kanchenjunga, Dolpo and Humla

The NTB advises that "the first thing an aspiring trekker must do" is find out whether their route requires a guide and TIMS card. If it does, the next step is to contact a government-registered trekking agency to arrange both. The full list of named treks is on the [NTB's TIMS page](https://ntb.gov.np/en/plan-your-trip/before-you-come/tims-card).

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) gives the same message plainly: "Do not trek alone." It also says breaking permit requirements can lead to fines or removal from protected areas.

## The TIMS card

TIMS stands for Trekking Information Management System. Under the NTB's system:

- The agency, not the trekker, completes a registration form with details of the trekkers, the guide and the route.
- The agency pays online, and an e-card with a QR code is generated for each trekker.
- At the time of writing (November 2026), the NTB gives the fee as NPR 2,000 per trekker, or NPR 1,000 for citizens of SAARC (South Asian) countries.

Ask your agency for a copy of each e-card before you set off.

## National park and conservation area permits

Most trekking routes pass through a national park or conservation area, and each needs its own entry permit. The NTB lists entry fees for foreign visitors per person, per entry. At the time of writing, these include NPR 3,000 each for Sagarmatha (Everest) National Park, the Annapurna Conservation Area, Langtang National Park, Makalu-Barun National Park and the Manaslu Conservation Area, and NPR 2,000 for the Kanchenjunga Conservation Area. According to the NTB, fees can be paid at the Tourist Service Center at Bhrikutimandap in Kathmandu or at the park entry points.

These fees change, so check the NTB's current table and confirm with your agency which permits your route needs.

## Restricted area permits

Some remote areas need a special trekking permit, issued by the Department of Immigration in Kathmandu, as well as the permits above. The NTB lists 15 restricted regions, including Upper Mustang, Upper and Lower Dolpa, the Manaslu area, the Tsum Valley, Humla and parts of Solukhumbu, Manang and Rasuwa. Each entry names the specific municipalities and wards covered.

These permits are charged per person, per week or per day, and some cost more in the September to November season. For example, the NTB lists Upper Mustang at USD 50 per person per day at the time of writing. If your route crosses a restricted zone, your agency should explain which permits you need and include them in the quote.

## Safety, insurance and choosing an agency

The FCDO lists the hazards of trekking, "especially above 3,000m": altitude sickness, sudden changes in weather, avalanches and sun exposure. It also warns about cheap trekking deals. It says some guides have taken "inexperienced trekkers to high altitudes too quickly" and then arranged costly helicopter evacuations. Its advice:

- Make sure your insurance covers mountain rescue and helicopter costs. See our [insurance guide](/guide/insurance/).
- Carry enough cash for flight delays, as ATMs and card payments may be unavailable in remote areas.
- Mobile coverage is limited in places, including long stretches of the Annapurna Circuit. The FCDO suggests you consider a satellite phone.

A realistic itinerary, with time to acclimatise, matters as much as the paperwork. Our guide to [operator licences](/guide/licences/) explains how to check registration, and our [Nepal travel guide](/countries/nepal/) covers the wider trip.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Is your agency registered with the government, and can you show us your registration?
- Will our guide hold a current trekking guide licence, and will the same guide stay with us for the whole trek?
- Which permits does our route need (TIMS, park or conservation area, restricted area), and are they all included in your price?
- How many acclimatisation days are in the itinerary, and what happens if someone shows signs of altitude sickness?
- What is your procedure for helicopter evacuation, and who pays at the time?
- Will you send copies of our TIMS e-cards and permits before we leave Kathmandu?
