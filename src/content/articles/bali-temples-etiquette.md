Bali's nickname, the Island of a Thousand Temples, undersells it: shrines stand in family compounds, rice fields, markets and on clifftops above the Indian Ocean, and small offerings appear on pavements and doorsteps every day. Visitors are welcome at many temples, but they are places of worship first. Cover your shoulders and knees, wear the sarong and sash usually provided at the entrance, stay out of the inner sanctums unless you are there to pray, and never step on an offering.

## Why temples matter

According to Indonesia's Ministry of Tourism, Balinese social and cultural life is closely bound to Hinduism, and temples serve both as communal places of worship and as part of residential compounds. The guiding philosophy is Tri Hita Karana: harmony between people and God, between people, and between people and nature. It shapes everything from ceremonies to how water sources are protected.

The ritual calendar is busy. Ngaben is the cremation ceremony; Melasti is a purification ritual held before Nyepi; and Galungan and Kuningan honour ancestors and celebrate the victory of dharma over adharma. You may well arrive at a temple during a ceremony, which is part of the reason for the etiquette below.

## What to wear

The official tourism site advises modest clothing that covers the shoulders and knees, and says sarongs and sashes are usually provided at temple entrances. Bali's provincial government also makes modest, appropriate dress an obligation for foreign visitors, especially at holy sites, under a circular on behaviour for foreign tourists.

In practice:

- Wear a top with sleeves or carry a scarf or shawl for your shoulders.
- Wear the sarong and sash offered at the gate, or bring your own.
- Dress the same way when you visit temples during a ceremony or festival.

## How to behave inside

The ministry's temple guidance is short and clear: move quietly, speak softly, follow the directions of temple officials, and do not interrupt rituals in progress.

Bali's provincial rules go further. Circular No. 7 of 2025, launched by Governor Wayan Koster on 24 March 2025 and updating a similar 2023 circular, requires foreign tourists to respect the sanctity of temples, sacred images and religious symbols. According to Antara, Indonesia's state news agency, it forbids visitors to:

- enter the main or central areas of a sacred site, except to pray;
- climb sacred trees;
- behave in ways that defile holy places;
- litter, pollute water sources or use single-use plastic.

Antara reports that breaking these rules can lead to sanctions or legal proceedings under Indonesian law. The circular also asks visitors to be accompanied by a licensed guide who understands Balinese customs, to stay in licensed accommodation and to follow the rules of each site.

## Offerings and ceremonies

The small woven trays of flowers you see on the ground are canang sari, daily offerings. The ministry's advice is simple: do not step on them. Walk around them, keep children and bags clear, and leave them where they are.

At Tirta Empul, near Tampaksiring, holy springs are used for melukat, a cleansing ritual, and visitors can watch or take part. Follow the temple staff's instructions on dress and order if you join in.

Nyepi, the Balinese day of silence, is the biggest date in the calendar for travellers to know. The FCDO says that on Nyepi everyone in Bali must stay indoors, turn off lights and make no noise, and that Ngurah Rai International Airport closes for the whole day. In 2026 it fell on 19 March; check the date for the year you travel.

## Temples visitors often see

Indonesia Travel lists several temples that welcome visitors, with entry fees that change, so check on the day:

- **Besakih**, the Mother Temple, Bali's largest and holiest complex, on the slopes of Mount Agung. Visit in the morning and avoid large ceremonies unless invited.
- **Tanah Lot**, a sea temple on a rocky outcrop in Tabanan, popular at sunset.
- **Uluwatu**, on a limestone cliff in the south, known for Kecak dance performances at sunset.
- **Ulun Danu Beratan**, a water temple on Lake Beratan.
- **Goa Gajah**, the Elephant Cave, a 9th-century archaeological site near Ubud.

Bali also charges foreign tourists a levy. At the time of writing (November 2026) the FCDO puts it at 150,000 rupiah, payable online or on arrival through the provincial government's Love Bali website.

## Before you go

- Pack clothes that cover shoulders and knees, and use the sarong and sash at the gate.
- Stay in the outer courtyards unless you are there to pray.
- Never step on or move offerings, and do not interrupt ceremonies.
- Do not climb sacred trees, walls or shrines for photographs.
- Check the Nyepi date before booking flights, as the airport closes for the day.
- Pay the Bali tourist levy only through the official provincial website or on arrival.
