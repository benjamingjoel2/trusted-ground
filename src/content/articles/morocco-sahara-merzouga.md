Merzouga, the village at the edge of the Erg Chebbi dunes, is the most common way into the Moroccan Sahara, but it is a long way from Marrakech and Fez. Morocco's national tourist office suggests allowing three days, recommends October to April as the best time for the desert, and warns that summers are extremely hot and winter nights are cold.

## Where Merzouga is and what you go for

The Moroccan National Tourist Office (ONMT), which runs Visit Morocco, describes Merzouga as a small village in the middle of the sands and "one of the gates of the Sahara". It sits in Erg Chebbi, a dune area with palm groves, tracks and walking routes.

The region is the Tafilalet, which ONMT describes as a transitional landscape of oases where green vegetation meets ochre sand. Rissani, nearby, is described as the cradle of the Alaouite dynasty. Erfoud and the larger town of Errachidia are the other main stops on the way in.

Typical activities listed by ONMT include:

- a camel caravan into the dunes and a night in a bivouac (a desert tent camp)
- watching sunrise over the dunes after a night on site
- hiking on foot or by camel
- Dayet Srij, a lake among the dunes that attracts stilts, wagtails and storks
- quad biking
- side trips to the Todra (Tudgha) gorges

## Distances and how long to allow

This is the part people most often underestimate. ONMT's suggested "desert break" itinerary from Marrakech gives driving times in two stages:

- **Marrakech to the Ouarzazate-Zagora-Tinghir area:** 193.7 km, about 3 hours 47 minutes.
- **On to the Errachidia-Midelt-Merzouga area:** 303.0 km, about 4 hours 20 minutes.

That is around eight hours at the wheel before you add stops. From the north, ONMT gives about 341.8 km and 5 hours 21 minutes from Fez to the Errachidia-Midelt-Merzouga area, with the route crossing the Talghemt pass. It also puts Merzouga about 2 hours 30 minutes from Errachidia.

Breaking the journey with a night on the way makes the drive far easier, and ONMT's own recommendation is three days for the region. One night in the dunes after a single long drive from Marrakech leaves very little time actually in the desert.

## Heat, cold and when to go

ONMT recommends October to April for the desert, and is clear that summer is extremely hot and winter nights are cold. Pack for both: sun protection and light clothing for the day, and warm layers for a camp night in the cooler months.

If you do travel in the heat, the NHS advice on heat illness applies:

- Drink more cold fluids, especially when active.
- Wear light, loose clothing and avoid the sun between 11am and 3pm.
- Limit alcohol and avoid extreme exercise.
- Know the signs of heat exhaustion: tiredness, dizziness, headache, nausea, heavy sweating, cramps and strong thirst.

The NHS describes heatstroke, with symptoms such as very high temperature, hot skin that is not sweating, confusion or loss of consciousness, as a medical emergency. In Morocco, the FCDO says to dial 150 and ask for an ambulance.

ONMT also notes that the dunes are shaped by the Chergui, a Saharan wind. Ask what happens to camp plans if a strong wind is forecast.

## Safety on the road and in the desert

- **Night driving.** The FCDO warns that poor lighting makes driving at night dangerous, with vehicles, pedestrians and animals on the road. Secondary and mountain roads can be especially hazardous in bad weather. Plan long drives for daylight.
- **Self-driving.** The FCDO says UK drivers need a 1968 international driving permit alongside their licence, plus an insurer's green card.
- **The Algerian border.** Merzouga lies on Morocco's eastern side, near Algeria. The FCDO states that the land border between Morocco and Algeria is closed and patrolled, and that crossing it without authorisation is illegal.
- **Activity standards.** The FCDO notes that adventure providers may not meet UK safety standards. It reports that quad biking accidents involving British nationals are frequent, and advises always wearing a helmet. Check that your insurance covers the activities you plan.
- **Medical care.** The FCDO says public health facilities provide limited care and recommends insurance that covers local treatment and medical evacuation. See our [insurance guide](/guide/insurance/).

## Planning desert nights

Before booking a night in a bivouac, find out:

- how you reach the camp (by camel, on foot or by 4x4) and how long that takes
- what the sleeping, washing and toilet arrangements are
- how warm the bedding is for the time of year
- whether the price includes dinner and breakfast, and the transfer back to your vehicle in the morning

## Questions to ask your operator

- How many hours of driving are there each day, and is any of it planned after dark?
- Where exactly is the camp, how do we reach it, and what facilities and bedding does it have in the month we are travelling?
- What do you do if a sandstorm or strong wind is forecast, or if someone in the group becomes unwell from the heat?
- Are quad bike or 4x4 activities included, are helmets provided, and does your insurance cover them?
- How far is the nearest medical facility from the camp, and how would an evacuation work?
- What is your cancellation and refund policy if we have to change dates because of weather?
