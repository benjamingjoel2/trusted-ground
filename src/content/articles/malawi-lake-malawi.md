Lake Malawi runs down the eastern side of the country like an inland sea: golden sand, clear water and, after dark, the lights of fishermen's lanterns scattered across the surface. Malawi's tourism board calls it the "calendar lake", 365 miles long and 52 miles wide, and its southern end holds the country's only UNESCO World Heritage Site, a national park created to protect the lake's fish. The short version: go in the dry months, treat the water with care because of schistosomiasis, and plan around fuel and malaria.

## A lake the size of a sea

According to Visit Malawi, the official tourism site, the lake covers 29,600 square kilometres, about 20 per cent of Malawi's surface area, and is Africa's third-largest and second-deepest lake. Its other nickname, "lake of stars", comes from the lanterns of night fishermen, which look like stars from a distance. The tourism board also highlights the chambo, a fish it describes as found only in Lake Malawi.

The beaches are the main draw. Visit Malawi lists snorkelling, scuba diving, kayaking, sailing, boat trips and beach volleyball among the activities on offer, along with festivals held on the sand.

## Lake Malawi National Park

Malawi's Department of National Parks and Wildlife (DNPW) manages five national parks, and Lake Malawi National Park is the one built around water rather than land. Visit Malawi says the park was created to protect the lake's endemic fish species and, citing UNESCO, that the lake is home to more than 350 species of endemic cichlid fish. These small, brightly coloured fish are what many snorkellers and divers come to see.

A few points worth knowing:

- The park is protected under Malawi's National Parks and Wildlife Act, and the DNPW's stated purposes include preserving endemic species, "including fish". Ask locally about park fees and any rules on boats, fishing and collecting before you head out.
- It is illegal to buy, sell, kill or capture any protected wild animal or trade its parts without a licence, according to the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO). That covers anything taken from the park.
- Visit Malawi notes that this is Malawi's only World Heritage Site, so expect it to be the busiest part of the lakeshore.

## The islands

Malawi has two islands that sit within Mozambican territorial waters: Likoma, the larger, and Chizumulu. Visit Malawi says Likoma is best known for St Peter's Cathedral, one of the largest churches in Africa, which looks oversized for so small an island. It also mentions gardens and craft galleries on Likoma, and lists Maleli, Mumbo and Domwe among Malawi's other islands. Check transport and timings well ahead if you plan to visit.

## When to go: rainy season and dry season

Malawi's Department of Climate Change and Meteorological Services (DCCMS) says the country's main rainfall season runs from October to April. The main rains usually begin in November, starting in the south and moving north, and pre-season rains, locally called *chizimalupsya*, often arrive earlier. DCCMS forecasts stress that onset and end dates shift from year to year and between districts.

In practice, that makes roughly May to September the drier window for a lake trip. If you travel in or near the rains:

- Check the DCCMS seasonal outlook, which is published each year, before fixing dates.
- The FCDO notes that Malawi had the largest cholera outbreak in its history in late 2022 and early 2023, and that cases are likely to rise again during the rainy season.

## Swimming, health and getting around

The biggest health question at the lake is the water itself. TravelHealthPro, the UK's National Travel Health Network and Centre service, says there is a risk of schistosomiasis (bilharzia) in Malawi. The parasite is released from freshwater snails and can pass through intact skin. Its advice is that all travellers should avoid wading, swimming or bathing in fresh water, that insect repellent and towelling dry do not reliably prevent infection, and that anyone who may have been exposed should have a health check to test for it. Factor that into any snorkelling or beach plans and talk to a travel health professional before you go.

TravelHealthPro also rates the malaria risk in Malawi as high, and the FCDO warns that tap water may not be safe to drink, especially in rural areas.

On the road, the FCDO warns that fuel shortages are frequent and can cause long queues, so do not assume fuel will be available on your route. Carry your driving licence, which police may ask to see at checkpoints, and a copy of your passport and visa. Outside the main tourist areas, the FCDO advises dressing conservatively.

You must have a visa to enter Malawi. At the time of writing (November 2026), the FCDO says single and multiple-entry visas can be bought on arrival, with a single-entry visa costing 50 US dollars, and that e-visas are available through the Malawi Immigration website. Check the current rules before you travel. For more on the country, see our [Malawi travel guide](/countries/malawi/).

## Before you go

- Speak to a travel health clinic about malaria prevention and the schistosomiasis risk, and arrange a post-trip check if you swim in the lake.
- Check the latest DCCMS seasonal outlook if you plan to travel between October and April.
- Confirm visa rules and fees with the FCDO and Malawi Immigration shortly before departure.
- Plan fuel stops in advance, as the FCDO says shortages are frequent.
- Ask your lodge or guide about national park fees and rules before snorkelling, boating or fishing in the park.
- Never buy shells, fish or other wildlife products from the park.
