At the turn of October into November, Mexican homes and cemeteries fill with marigold petals, candlelight, copal smoke and the favourite foods of people who have died. Día de Muertos, the Day of the Dead, is a family occasion for welcoming the souls of the dead home for a short visit. It is not Mexican Halloween. Visitors are welcome in many places, but you are a guest at something private and often sacred, and how you behave matters.

## What the festival is

UNESCO added the Day of the Dead, as practised by Mexico's indigenous communities, to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008. It had first been proclaimed in 2003. UNESCO describes the festivities as commemorating the temporary return to Earth of deceased relatives and loved ones, at the end of October and the beginning of November. That period also marks the end of the annual maize-growing cycle.

The main days follow the Catholic calendar. According to INAFED, a Mexican federal government institute, 1 November (All Saints) is for the *muertos chiquitos*, children who have died, and 2 November (All Souls) is for adults. Practices vary a great deal by region. In the Huasteca region the Xantolo celebrations begin on 29 October, according to the Secretaría de Cultura, Mexico's culture ministry.

Where the tradition comes from is debated. UNESCO and INAFED describe a fusion of pre-Hispanic rites with Catholic feasts brought by the Spanish. An article from INAH, Mexico's national institute of anthropology and history, argues that the 1 and 2 November feasts themselves began in medieval Europe, and that they are separate from the funeral practices of ancient Mesoamerica, which took place on different dates.

## The ofrenda

The heart of the celebration is the ofrenda, the offering set out at home and at the grave. UNESCO describes families laying flower petals, candles and offerings along the path from the cemetery to the house. They place the dead person's favourite dishes around the home altar and the tomb, along with flowers and crafts such as paper cut-outs.

INPI, Mexico's national institute for indigenous peoples, explains the meaning of the main elements:

- **Water**, to quench the thirst of souls after their long journey.
- **Salt**, for purification.
- **Candles**, whose light guides the souls home. In several indigenous communities each candle stands for one person who has died.
- **Copal and incense**, to cleanse the space.
- **Flowers**, especially the yellow cempasúchil (marigold). Its scattered petals mark a path of colour and scent for the souls. INPI notes that the name means "twenty flower" in Náhuatl.
- **Bread**, in many shapes, and the foods the person loved in life.
- **A toy xoloitzcuintle dog** on children's altars. In tradition, this dog helps souls cross the river on the way to Mictlán, the underworld.

INAFED adds papel picado (cut tissue paper), sugar skulls, pan de muerto and mole to the familiar picture.

## Places known for their celebrations

The Secretaría de Cultura names a number of communities where the tradition of the living keeping company with the dead in homes, streets and cemeteries has been strongly kept. They include Pátzcuaro in Michoacán, San Andrés Mixquic in Mexico City, the Huasteca Potosina, Huaquechula in Puebla and Ocotepec in Morelos.

- **Mixquic**, in Tláhuac in the south of Mexico City. The culture ministry describes the *alumbrada*, the night of candles, as the high point. From 18:00 on 2 November residents walk to the cemetery with candles, incense and flowers and stay by the graves through the night.
- **Lake Pátzcuaro and Janitzio**, Michoacán. The ministry describes women and children going silently to the island cemetery before midnight on 1 November with offerings, as candles are lit on the graves and chants in Purépecha are heard across the island.
- **Cuanajo**, near Pátzcuaro. The ministry describes a local custom of offerings placed on small wooden horses.

Programmes, access and cemetery opening arrangements change from year to year, sometimes because of restoration work. Check the state tourism or municipal announcements for the year you travel. For broader trip planning, see our [Mexico guide](/countries/mexico/).

## Visiting respectfully

These are working rituals, not performances. UNESCO notes that great care goes into the preparations, because the dead are believed to be able to bring prosperity or misfortune depending on how well the rituals are carried out. With that in mind:

- **Treat cemeteries as you would any graveyard at a funeral.** Keep your voice down, keep to the paths and never step on or sit on graves.
- **Ask before photographing people**, and accept a no. Avoid flash at candlelit vigils, and do not put your camera close to a family's altar.
- **Do not touch or take anything from an ofrenda.** The food, drink and objects are offered to particular people who have died.
- **Dress modestly** for church and cemetery visits. Face paint and costumes are part of some public events, but they are out of place beside a family keeping watch at a grave.
- **Support the community.** Buying flowers, bread or crafts from local sellers, or making a small offering where it is invited, is a more fitting way to take part than simply watching.
- **Go with someone local if you can.** A community guide can explain what you are seeing and tell you where visitors are welcome.

## Before you go

- Book accommodation early for late October and early November in well-known places such as Pátzcuaro.
- Check this year's official programme and any cemetery access restrictions before travelling.
- Bring warm layers and a torch if you plan to stay out for a night-long vigil.
- Learn a few phrases of Spanish to ask permission and say thank you.
- Remember the key dates: 1 November for children, 2 November for adults, with regional variations either side.
