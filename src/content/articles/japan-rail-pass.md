The nationwide Japan Rail Pass gives unlimited travel on JR trains, including most Shinkansen, for 7, 14 or 21 days. It is only for overseas visitors who enter Japan as a "Temporary Visitor". At the time of writing (November 2026), a 7-day standard pass bought online costs 50,000 yen. That only pays off if you cover long distances, so travellers staying in one region should compare it with the cheaper regional passes sold by individual JR companies.

## The network and the pass

The JR Group's six companies run trains from Hokkaido in the north to Kyushu in the south. The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) says timetables are "followed religiously". Shinkansen bullet trains connect the main cities, and slower local lines run through the countryside. The Japan Rail Pass is a joint product of all six companies. It covers Shinkansen and limited express trains, with the important exception of the fastest services on the main Tokyo–Osaka–Kyushu corridor (see below).

## Who can buy it

According to the official Japan Rail Pass site, you must be a foreign tourist visiting Japan for sightseeing, with "Temporary Visitor" stamped or stuck in your passport. Automated immigration gates at airports do not stamp passports, so use a staffed gate or ask an officer for the stamp. Visitors who enter on another status, such as "Trainee", cannot collect the pass. Japanese nationals living abroad may qualify under separate rules that need proof of long-term overseas residence, and they can only buy overseas. If you do not meet the conditions, you cannot use the pass, even if you have already paid for it online.

## Prices and how it works

The pass comes in two classes, Green Car (first class) and Ordinary, for 7, 14 or 21 days. Official adult prices for online purchase at the time of writing:

| Pass | 7 days | 14 days | 21 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary | 50,000 yen | 80,000 yen | 100,000 yen |
| Green | 70,000 yen | 110,000 yen | 140,000 yen |

Children aged 6 to 11 pay half. Buying through a JR-designated travel agency overseas costs more, for example 53,000 yen for a 7-day Ordinary pass, paid in local currency. The JR Group says prices may change, and the price on the day you buy applies.

Once you have the pass:

- **Collect it at a ticket office.** You exchange your purchase for the pass in person.
- **Carry your passport** every time you use it, as staff may ask to see it.
- **Use the gates.** You can go through automatic ticket gates or show the pass at a staffed gate.
- **Reserve seats free.** The pass does not guarantee a seat, but reserved seats cost nothing extra. If you buy on the official site, you can book seats before collecting the pass.
- **Mind the dates.** The pass only works within the period you chose. Refunds are generally only possible before the start date, and a lost or stolen pass cannot be reissued.

## Nozomi, Mizuho and other limits

The fastest trains on the Tokaido, Sanyo and Kyushu Shinkansen, Nozomi and Mizuho, are not covered by the pass alone. Pass holders can buy a special "Nozomi Mizuho Ticket" for them, valid for one day. Official prices at the time of writing include 4,960 yen for Tokyo to Kyoto or Shin-Osaka, 6,500 yen for Tokyo to Hiroshima, and 4,960 yen for Shin-Osaka to Hakata.

The pass does not cover JR Group highway buses or some local bus routes. Where a JR train runs onto another company's line, you pay a separate fare for that section, with some exceptions.

**Big luggage:** on the Tokaido, Sanyo, Kyushu and Nishi-Kyushu Shinkansen, bags whose length, width and height add up to between 160cm and 250cm need a reserved seat with an oversized-baggage area. JR Central says it costs nothing beyond a normal reserved seat, but travelling without one means a 1,000 yen fee. Anything larger cannot be taken on board.

## Regional passes and IC cards

JNTO notes that travellers focused on one area may get better value from a regional pass. Each is sold by one JR company, and JNTO lists passes including:

- **Hokkaido Rail Pass** (JR Hokkaido), which excludes the Hokkaido Shinkansen
- **JR East Pass** and **JR Tokyo Wide Pass** (JR East), the latter reaching Lake Kawaguchiko near Mount Fuji, Nikko and the Izu Peninsula
- **Hokuriku Arch Pass**, for travel between Tokyo and Osaka via Kanazawa
- **JR-Central tourist passes**, covering places such as Takayama and Shirakawa-go
- **JR-West passes**, from the Kansai Mini Pass for Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe and Nara up to the JR-West All Area Pass, which reaches from Hakata to Toyama
- **All Shikoku Rail Pass** and the **JR Kyushu Rail Pass**

Prices, durations and eligibility vary, so check each company's own site.

For city travel, rechargeable IC cards such as Suica and PASMO (Tokyo area) or ICOCA (Kansai) let you tap through gates. JNTO says most charge a refundable 500 yen deposit. Welcome Suica, a tourist card with no deposit, is valid for up to 28 days. ## Before you go

- Add up the full fares for your planned route before deciding whether a nationwide pass is worth it.
- Ask for a "Temporary Visitor" stamp at a staffed immigration gate.
- Buy on the official Japan Rail Pass site if you want to reserve seats before you arrive.
- Budget for Nozomi or Mizuho tickets if speed matters on the Tokyo–Osaka–Hakata line.
- Reserve an oversized-baggage seat if you are travelling with a large suitcase.
- Keep your passport with your pass at all times.
