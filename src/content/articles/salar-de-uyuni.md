For most of the year the Salar de Uyuni is a flat, white crust of salt that seems to run to the horizon. After heavy rain, a thin sheet of water can turn parts of it into a mirror that reflects the sky. The mirror depends on the rainy season, which also brings flooded tracks and blocked roads, and the salt flat is high enough for altitude sickness to be a real risk. Plan around all three.

## What the salar is

NASA's Earth Observatory describes Bolivia's Salar de Uyuni as the largest salt flat in the world. Its salt crust covers about 10,000 square kilometres (4,000 square miles). It is so flat that scientists use it to calibrate satellite instruments that measure the height of the land.

The salar is also rich in minerals. NASA notes that halite (common salt), ulexite and gypsum have been harvested there, some since at least the 1600s. Beneath the crust lies a lithium-rich brine. NASA cites a US Geological Survey estimate that Bolivia holds about 15 per cent of the world's lithium.

## When the mirror appears

According to NASA, during the rainy season water can fill part of the salt flat and give it its mirror-like look. The area is a closed basin, so rain on the flat and snow on the surrounding peaks all drain into it. NASA notes that visitors come to see the salt crust in the dry season and the mirror lakes in the wet season.

How much water there is varies from year to year. In early 2022 heavy rain in November, December and early January left the salar brimming almost to its edges. NASA reports that local newspapers covered flooding in some areas and temporary bans on travel across the salar during the busy tourist season.

That is the trade-off of a rainy-season visit. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) gives Bolivia's rainy season as **November to March**. In that period it warns of:

- landslides, with roads that can be blocked or impassable for days at a time
- travellers stranded in high, remote areas, so you should carry food, water and warm clothing in case of delays
- rough, mostly unpaved roads where four-wheel drive vehicles are often needed, especially in the rainy season

If seeing the mirror matters most to you, go in the rainy season and leave slack in your plans. If you want dependable access across the flat and onward routes, the drier months are the safer bet. The FCDO also notes that forest fires are common in parts of southern and eastern Bolivia in the dry season (May to October) and can affect air quality.

## Altitude

The FCDO lists the Salar de Uyuni, alongside La Paz, as a place in Bolivia where altitude sickness is a risk. TravelHealthPro, the UK's National Travel Health Network and Centre, says altitude illness usually occurs above 2,500 m, although some people get it lower down. It gives La Paz's height as 3,630 m.

Key points from TravelHealthPro:

- Physical fitness does not appear to protect you.
- Risk factors include a fast ascent, normally living below 900 m, exertion soon after arrival and a previous history of altitude illness.
- Acute mountain sickness typically starts 6 to 10 hours after you arrive above 2,500 m, with a headache plus symptoms such as nausea, dizziness or fatigue.
- The severe forms, high altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE) and high altitude cerebral oedema (HACE), are life-threatening and need urgent attention.
- The key to prevention is a gradual ascent with regular rest days.

In practice, avoid flying straight from sea level and heading out onto the salar the next day. Talk to a travel health professional before you go, particularly if you have a heart or lung condition. The FCDO also notes that some medicines are in short supply in Bolivia, so bring enough prescription medicine for your stay, with the prescription.

## Choosing a salt flat tour

Most visitors cross the salar on a four-wheel-drive tour. The FCDO lists salt flat tours among Bolivia's adventure activities and warns that **there are no official minimum standards for tour operators**. It advises getting local advice, using only reputable companies, and checking that your insurance covers the activities you plan.

The FCDO also warns that vehicles are often poorly maintained and that serious accidents happen on the main tourist routes. Protests and road blockades can start with little warning: it advises against trying to cross a blockade in a vehicle and suggests allowing extra time to reach your destination.

## Entry and border crossings

At the time of writing (November 2026), the FCDO says British visitors can stay in Bolivia for up to 90 days without a visa. Many travellers reach Uyuni overland. The FCDO notes that Bolivia no longer stamps passports on the border with Argentina, but you must register your exit with Argentina's immigration authorities. You need a yellow fever vaccination certificate if you arrive from a country listed as a transmission risk. Other nationalities should check their own government's advice.

## Questions to ask your operator

- What is the vehicle's maintenance record, and does it carry spare tyres, a first-aid kit and a satellite or radio link?
- What happens if the salar is flooded or a road is blocked: is there an alternative route, and what is the refund policy?
- How high are the overnight stops, and does the itinerary allow time to acclimatise?
- How many passengers share each vehicle, and how long are the driving days?
- What food, water and warm bedding are provided for cold nights?
- Is the driver also the guide, and what languages do they speak?
