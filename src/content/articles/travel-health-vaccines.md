The jabs are the easy part of a long-haul trip; the timing is what catches people out. UK health bodies advise getting travel health advice weeks before you go, because some vaccines need several doses and others take time to protect you. Leave it to the last week and you may travel without full protection, or without a certificate a border official asks to see.

## Start early

The NHS says you should try to get advice from a travel health professional at least four to six weeks before you travel. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) goes further in its country pages, advising travellers to check vaccine recommendations at least eight weeks before a trip. If your itinerary is still taking shape, book the appointment anyway: you can update it later.

The NHS lists a few practical points for the appointment:

- Tell the person vaccinating you about any health conditions, including a weakened immune system, or if you are pregnant, and bring any medicines you take.
- Check when you had previous travel vaccines; some protect for years, others only short term.
- Do not have a vaccine while you are unwell, including with a high temperature.
- Mild side effects such as a sore lump, a temperature or a headache are common.

All travellers, including children, should be up to date with standard UK vaccinations such as measles.

## What is free and what you pay for

According to the NHS, the following travel vaccines are available free at a GP surgery after a risk assessment:

- cholera
- polio (as part of the combined diphtheria, tetanus and polio vaccine)
- hepatitis A
- typhoid

Vaccines not routinely available on the NHS, which you pay for at a private clinic, pharmacy travel service or GP surgery that offers them, include hepatitis B, Japanese encephalitis, rabies, tick-borne encephalitis and yellow fever. Yellow fever vaccine is only given at registered yellow fever vaccination centres, which you can find through the National Travel Health Network and Centre (NaTHNaC).

## Yellow fever and certificates

Yellow fever is spread by mosquitoes. The World Health Organization (WHO) says a single dose of vaccine gives lifelong protection, with immunity developing in 80 to 100 per cent of people within ten days. Under the International Health Regulations, countries may ask travellers for proof of vaccination.

That proof is the International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis, which the NHS says you receive once you have had a vaccine such as yellow fever. TravelHealthPro notes that, since July 2016, a valid yellow fever certificate lasts for life and cannot be rejected because more than ten years have passed. Keep it with your passport.

## Example: planning for Tanzania

Tanzania shows how destination advice layers up. At the time of writing (November 2026), TravelHealthPro's Tanzania page says:

- **Most travellers:** hepatitis A, tetanus and typhoid are recommended.
- **Some travellers:** depending on plans, vaccines including cholera, hepatitis B and rabies may be advised. Pre-exposure rabies vaccine is recommended for people in higher-risk situations, including long stays of more than a month and travel to areas where medical care is limited.
- **Yellow fever:** there is a low potential for exposure and vaccination is generally not recommended for travel to Tanzania itself, but a certificate is required from travellers over one year old arriving from a country with yellow fever transmission risk, or after transiting for more than 12 hours through an airport in one. The FCDO confirms the certificate requirement.
- **Malaria:** there is a high risk in all areas below 1,800 metres, including Zanzibar, and antimalarial tablets are recommended for those areas. TravelHealthPro advises the "ABCD" approach: awareness of risk, bite prevention, chemoprophylaxis (tablets) and prompt diagnosis of any fever.
- **Altitude:** there is a risk of altitude illness above 2,500 metres, which includes Mount Kilimanjaro at 5,895 metres.

The FCDO adds that medical facilities are limited, especially outside Dar es Salaam, and that the legal status of some UK medicines can differ abroad. It also says all visitors to mainland Tanzania and Zanzibar must buy mandatory Tanzanian government inbound travel insurance, even if they already hold a personal policy, and should still have their own travel insurance.

## Before you go

- Book a travel health appointment six to eight weeks before departure.
- Check your destination's page on TravelHealthPro and the FCDO health section.
- Work out whether your route, including transits, triggers a yellow fever certificate requirement.
- Ask about antimalarials early: some courses start weeks before arrival.
- Pack your vaccination certificate, prescriptions and medicines in your hand luggage.
- Buy travel insurance that covers medical treatment and evacuation for every activity you plan.
