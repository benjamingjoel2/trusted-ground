Every July, Mongolia turns its attention to three contests that grew out of life on the steppe: wrestling, archery and horse racing. Naadam is the country's national festival, held each year from 11 to 13 July, and it is as much about music, costume, food and ritual as it is about sport. If you want to see it, plan around those dates, book early, and prepare for crowds in town and rough roads beyond it.

## What Naadam is

UNESCO added Naadam to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2010. Its description is a useful starting point for visitors. Naadam is "a national festival celebrated every year from 11 to 13 July across Mongolia", focused on "three traditional games: horseracing, wrestling and archery".

UNESCO ties the festival closely to "the nomadic civilization of the Mongols, who have long practiced pastoralism on Central Asia's vast steppe". The three sports, it notes, are "directly linked with the lifestyles and living conditions of the Mongols". They have traditionally been taught at home by family members, although formal training has recently developed for wrestling and archery.

## The three games, and everything around them

The sport is the centrepiece, but it is not the whole festival. According to UNESCO:

- **Competitors are honoured.** Spectators "revere the sportsmen, sportswomen, and children who compete", and winners receive titles for their achievements.
- **Words and song matter.** Ritual praise songs and poems are dedicated to the contestants.
- **Costume and kit are distinctive.** Mongolians wear "unique costumes" and use "distinctive tools and sporting items" during the festival.
- **The arts are part of it.** Oral traditions, performing arts, national cuisine and craftsmanship all feature. So do long song, Khöömei overtone singing, Bie biyelgee dance and the Morin khuur fiddle.
- **Everyone can join in.** "Everyone is allowed and encouraged to participate in Naadam", which UNESCO credits with building community involvement.

The festival's rituals and customs also "accentuate respect for nature and the environment".

## Listening for the long song

Of the music you may hear, the urtiin duu, or "long song", is one of the most distinctive. UNESCO, which also lists it as intangible heritage, describes it as one of the two major forms of Mongolian song, alongside the "short song". It is "a lyrical chant" with "an abundance of ornamentation, falsetto, an extremely wide vocal range and a free compositional form".

UNESCO notes that the long song "can also be heard at the naadam". It is also sung at weddings, the birth of a child, the branding of foals and other events in nomadic life. The form is widely believed to have originated 2,000 years ago, and it has appeared in literary works since the thirteenth century.

## Crowds, weather and getting around

Naadam is a peak moment in the Mongolian year, and that has practical consequences.

**Pickpockets.** The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that pickpockets operate in markets and crowded public places. It says the risk "increases during the festivals of Tsagaan Sar (New Year...) and Naadam (July)". Keep your phone, passport and money in a secured bag on your person, and leave valuables in a hotel safe if possible.

**Getting around safely in the capital.** The FCDO advises against being out alone at night in Ulaanbaatar. It suggests asking hotels, restaurants or clubs to order a car for you, or using licensed taxis or the trackable UBCab app.

**Weather.** Summer heat can reach +35°C, and the FCDO says "weather conditions often change without warning". Warnings are issued all year for flooding, dust storms, strong winds and heatwaves. The most extreme conditions occur in and around the Gobi Desert and the steppe. Pack for heat, sudden rain and cold nights.

**Rural travel.** If you plan to watch a local Naadam outside the capital, remember that the roads are hard going. The FCDO says only about 10% of roads outside Ulaanbaatar are tarmacked and signposting is minimal. Large rural areas also have no phone coverage. Its advice is to research your route and ask local drivers about conditions. Carry backup communications, plenty of water and supplies, and share your plans with someone.

## Paperwork and etiquette

- **Entry.** According to the FCDO at the time of writing (November 2026), British citizens can visit Mongolia for tourism for up to 30 days without a visa until 31 December 2026. Your passport needs to be valid for at least six months after you arrive. Make sure the border officer stamps it. Other nationalities should check with the Mongolian authorities.
- **Registration.** Hotels register guests using an app. If you are travelling independently or staying with friends, you must register yourself with the Mongolian Immigration Agency.
- **ID.** Always carry your passport. The FCDO says police will not accept a copy, and you could be fined.
- **Photography.** Ask before taking photographs in Buddhist monasteries, and do not touch sacred images or objects. It is illegal to photograph or film police or military personnel.

## Before you go

- Fix your dates around 11 to 13 July, and book accommodation and transport well ahead.
- Check current entry rules for your passport, as the FCDO's visa-free arrangement for British citizens runs until 31 December 2026.
- Carry your passport at all times, and keep valuables secure in festival crowds.
- If you are heading to a rural Naadam, ask your driver or operator about road conditions, communications and supplies.
- Pack for heat, sudden storms and cool nights.
- Ask before photographing people and religious sites, and leave police and military out of your pictures.
