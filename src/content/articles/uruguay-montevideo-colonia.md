Uruguay holds a striking range of experiences within a compact area. Colonia del Sacramento is a Portuguese-founded town of crooked lanes and low stone houses on the Río de la Plata. Montevideo is a capital where drum troupes still parade through the old neighbourhoods on Sundays. Along the Atlantic coast, sea lions haul out on rocks below shifting dunes. The main toll roads linking Colonia, Montevideo and Punta del Este make it easy to combine a historic town, a city break and time by the sea.

## Colonia del Sacramento: a contested colonial town

According to Uruguay's Ministry of Education and Culture, the Portuguese founded Colonia del Sacramento in 1680. The town became an object of dispute between the colonial powers of Spain and Portugal, and its changing occupation left a notable mix of Portuguese, Spanish and post-colonial architecture. Colonia remained the region's most important military stronghold and port until Montevideo was founded in 1726–29.

UNESCO added the Barrio Histórico (historic quarter) to the World Heritage List in December 1995. The ministry says it is the only example in the River Plate region of a town plan that does not follow the rigid grid Spain imposed on its colonies. Instead, its "spontaneous", typically Portuguese layout of streets follows the lie of the land. Uruguay protected the old town's street plan as a national historic monument in 1976, and more of its buildings were added in 1998.

When Uruguay marked 30 years of World Heritage status in 2025, the Ministry of Tourism called Colonia one of the country's main cultural destinations. It stressed that World Heritage status brings a lasting duty to protect the site and to encourage responsible tourism. In practice, that means walking rather than driving through the old quarter and treating the historic houses as people's homes.

## Montevideo: candombe and tango

Montevideo's old neighbourhoods are the birthplace of two art forms that UNESCO added to its list of intangible cultural heritage in 2009: candombe and tango. Uruguay's Ministry of Education and Culture traces them as follows:

- **Tango** was born in the late 19th century among the working-class people of Buenos Aires and Montevideo. It mixed African, Creole and European immigrant influences. It is still danced in *milongas*, traditional dance halls, in Montevideo and other cities of the River Plate.
- **Candombe** grew from the music and dance of the more than 20 African peoples brought to the region as slaves. It is played on three drums, the *piano*, *repique* and *chico*, by groups called *comparsas* that move through the streets. Today you can hear it on Sundays and public holidays in the capital's neighbourhoods and across the country.

The ministry links distinct drumming styles to particular Montevideo neighbourhoods. The Cuareim style was born in the Medio Mundo tenement on Calle Cuareim, in the heart of Barrio Sur. The Ansina style comes from a tenement in Palermo. Each February the Desfile de Llamadas takes candombe to its biggest stage. The ministry calls it the country's largest public spectacle, with more than 200,000 musicians and spectators.

## The Atlantic coast: dunes, sea lions and islands

East of Montevideo, the coast turns from river to ocean. The best-known protected stretch is Cabo Polonio National Park in Rocha department. Uruguay's Ministry of Environment says it still has the character of the Atlantic coast before mid-20th-century resort building. Its standout feature is a system of moving dunes, some more than 20 metres high, among the few left in the region.

Wildlife is the other draw. The park's islands hold almost half of Uruguay's populations of South American fur seals and sea lions. The rocky cape has the country's only fur seal colony on the mainland, where you can watch them up close. Southern right whales also pass through the area. The village of Cabo Polonio grew up in the 19th century around sealing, which ended in 1991. About 70 people live there today, joined in summer by many seasonal residents and visitors.

Further west, the Isla e Islote de Lobos lie 8.1 km off Punta del Este. In 2024 they became Uruguay's first fully marine protected area. The ministry says the islands are home to fur seals and sea lions, and their lighthouse dates from 1858 and was rebuilt in 1906.

## Safety and getting around

Uruguay feels relaxed, but the FCDO warns of street crime in Montevideo, including bag-snatching, pickpocketing and mugging. It advises against walking downtown or in the port area alone, especially at night and at weekends. Thieves regularly break into cars parked on the street. Crime rates are generally lower elsewhere in the country.

The FCDO says the main toll roads from Colonia to Montevideo and Punta del Este are in good condition, though serious accidents are common. Tolls are moving to electronic tags only, and hire cars already have them. Many beaches have lifeguards from December to March, and coloured flags show swimming conditions. Red means danger and black means a thunderstorm. Credit cards are not always accepted outside the main towns. For more on the country, see our [Uruguay travel guide](/countries/uruguay/). ## Before you go

- Allow at least a full day in Colonia's historic quarter, and explore it on foot.
- Check the date of the Desfile de Llamadas if you plan a February visit to Montevideo.
- Carry some cash for smaller towns and coastal villages where cards may not be accepted.
- On the coast, swim only at beaches with lifeguards and follow the flag colours.
- Keep your distance from fur seals and sea lions, and follow any park signs at Cabo Polonio.
- In Montevideo, use paid car parks and avoid walking alone in the port area at night.
