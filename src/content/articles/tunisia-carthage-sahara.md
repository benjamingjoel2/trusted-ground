Few countries pack so much into a single road trip. North of Tunis lie the remains of Carthage, once the centre of a Mediterranean trading network and later capital of Rome's province of Africa. Further south, a Roman amphitheatre rises above the town of El Jem, and beyond the salt flats of the Chott el-Jerid the dunes of the Grand Erg Oriental begin. The history is easy to reach. The desert needs more planning, because UK travel advice restricts travel near Tunisia's borders with Algeria and Libya.

## Carthage: Punic and Roman layers

According to the Tunisian National Tourist Office's Discover Tunisia site, legend holds that Carthage was founded by the Phoenician queen Elyssa, also known as Dido, in 814 BC. The Carthaginians, or Punics, ran a network of trading posts across the Mediterranean from Spain to Sicily. Rome destroyed the city in 146 BC, then rebuilt it as the capital of its wealthy province of Africa.

That history explains what you see today. The Punic city survives only in fragile traces, including the residential Magon and Hannibal quarters and the Punic ports. The Roman remains show the city's wealth:

- **The Antonine Baths**, whose great hall stood 30 metres high.
- **The cisterns**, with a capacity of 60,000 cubic metres.
- **Damous el-Karita**, once the largest church in Africa.

The tourist office notes that Carthage is one of Tunisia's sites on the UNESCO World Heritage List, along with Dougga, El Jem, Kerkouane and the medinas of Tunis, Kairouan and Sousse. If Carthage's Punic traces leave you wanting more, the tourist office points to Kerkouane on Cap Bon, a Punic town never rebuilt after it was abandoned in the 3rd century BC.

## El Jem: the amphitheatre

"Few ancient sites are as impressive," says Discover Tunisia of the Roman amphitheatre at El Jem. Modelled on the Colosseum in Rome and admired for its architectural precision, it hosted spectacles in which hunters fought wild animals. It is also on the UNESCO list.

## The Sahara edge: Douz, the Chott and the ksour

Discover Tunisia calls the Douz region "the gentlest approach to the desert": lines of dunes, palm groves and sand drifting over the roads. Douz is home to the Mrazig, a people of nomadic herders who still leave their houses at times to live under canvas. Nearby are the large palm groves of Kebili and Deggache, and the villages of Sabria, Nouil and El Faouar, between the sand and the vast salt flat of the Chott el-Jerid.

What the tourist office highlights around Douz:

- immediate access to the sand desert and many desert camps;
- a large weekly market and a strong nomadic culture;
- activities including microlight flights, quad biking, sand-boarding and land-sailing on the Chott el-Jerid.

Beyond, Tunisia has 40,000 square kilometres of sand desert, part of the Grand Erg Oriental. The tourist office says regional guides and camel drivers lead walks, camel treks and motorbike, quad and 4x4 trips. Some tarmac routes are passable in an ordinary car, and it describes routes as secured by military posts and regularly spaced refuelling points.

In the mountainous south-east are the ksour, fortified granaries where semi-nomadic people once stored harvests in stacked cells called *ghorfas*. Villages such as Chenini and Douiret perch on hilltops, and the tourist office says the Berber language is still spoken there. Some ksour and cave houses now take guests.

## What UK travel advice says

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) advises against all travel to some parts of Tunisia and against all but essential travel to others. At the time of writing (November 2026) these include:

- in the south, the militarised zone south of El Borma and Dhehiba, within 20km of the rest of the Libyan border north of Dhehiba, and the town of Ben Guerdane;
- all but essential travel within 75km of the Libyan border, including Remada and El Borma, but excluding Zarzis, the C118 road and areas of Medenine Governorate north of that road;
- in the west, the Chaambi Mountains National Park, designated military operations zones and several areas near the Algerian border, including Kasserine Governorate and the town of Sbeitla.

Check any desert or ksour itinerary against the FCDO map, because travelling against its advice can invalidate your insurance. The FCDO also says security forces are on high alert, that tourist sites may carry a higher risk of terrorist attack, and that a state of emergency has been in place since 2015.

Practical points from the same advice:

- Quad bikes are popular in desert resorts, but safety standards vary. Always wear a helmet.
- Check fuel availability with local drivers before long journeys in remote areas, and take care driving at night on unlit roads.
- Approach security checkpoints slowly and be ready to show ID.
- Do not photograph military sites or government buildings.
- You need permission from the Tunisian authorities to take antiquities out of the country.
- Summer heatwaves can cause power cuts, affecting air conditioning, phone networks and card payments.
- Dress modestly at religious sites and in remote areas. Ramadan next starts in February 2027.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Does any part of the route, including ksour visits, fall inside an area the FCDO advises against?
- Who leads desert excursions, and what vehicles, water and communications do they carry?
- Are helmets and safety briefings provided for quad and buggy trips?
- How is the itinerary adapted in summer heat or during Ramadan?
- What happens if a checkpoint or road closure changes the plan?
