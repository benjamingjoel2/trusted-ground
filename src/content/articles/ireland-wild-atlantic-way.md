Sea cliffs that drop straight into the Atlantic, white beaches between rocky headlands, Irish-speaking islands and pubs where traditional music still fills the evenings: the Wild Atlantic Way strings all of this along Ireland's western seaboard. It is a signposted touring route of about 2,500km, not a single road you must drive end to end. The trick is to choose one or two regions and slow down.

## What the route is

Fáilte Ireland's Discover Ireland site describes the Wild Atlantic Way as 2,500km of coastline running from the Inishowen Peninsula in Donegal to Kinsale in County Cork. Along the way it passes through nine counties and three provinces. The route marked its 10th anniversary in 2024. Discover Ireland calls it "the longest coastal touring route in the world".

Because it is so long, very few visitors drive the whole thing in one trip. A more realistic plan is to pick a stretch, such as Donegal's headlands, Mayo and Connemara, the Burren and Clare, or the Kerry and West Cork peninsulas, and leave time for walks, boat trips and bad-weather days. Cyclists have their own option: Discover Ireland describes a 1,600km Wild Atlantic Way cycle.

## Highlights from north to south

The tourism board's own list of highlights gives a sense of how varied the coast is:

- **Donegal.** Fanad Head Lighthouse is a popular spot for watching dolphins. The Sliabh Liag (Slieve League) cliffs are among the highest sea cliffs in Europe.
- **Sligo.** Mullaghmore Head is known for big-wave surfing, Strandhill for surf lessons, and Carrowmore Megalithic Cemetery for its ancient tombs.
- **Mayo.** Dún Briste, the sea stack at Downpatrick Head, rises 45m above the ocean. The Céide Fields visitor centre tells the story of field systems and tombs up to 5,000 years old. Clew Bay is said to have an island for every day of the year.
- **Galway.** Killary Harbour is one of Ireland's only fjords, and the 5km Derrigimlagh Loop leads to the spot where the first non-stop transatlantic flight ended.
- **Clare.** The Burren is a karst limestone landscape with its own unusual mix of native and alpine plants. The Cliffs of Moher face due west over Galway Bay towards the Aran Islands.
- **Kerry and Cork.** Dingle (Daingean Uí Chúis), Slea Head and the Blasket Islands are here. Further south come the Beara Peninsula and the town of Kinsale, with the 6km Old Head loop walk.

Kerry is also home to an International Dark Sky Reserve. In Mayo, the Mayo Dark Sky Park at Ballycroy covers 150 square kilometres.

## Islands along the way

Discover Ireland counts 30 islands along the route. A few are worth planning around:

- **Achill (Acaill)** is the largest of Ireland's offshore islands. There's no ferry: you can cross by road bridge or cycle over on the Great Western Greenway from Westport.
- **The Aran Islands (Árainn)** in Galway Bay are Inis Mór, the largest, Inis Meáin and Inis Oírr, the smallest. They are known for the Aran sweater and for writers such as Máirtín Ó Direáin. Off Donegal, islands such as Toraigh (Tory) are places where the Irish language thrives.
- **Dursey Island** off West Cork is reached by Ireland's only cable car, a crossing of about 10 minutes.
- **The Great Blasket (An Blascaod Mór)** has been uninhabited since its last residents left in 1953. The Blasket Centre on the mainland tells their story.

## Skellig Michael: book early, check the season

Skellig Michael (Sceilg Mhichíl) is one of only two UNESCO World Heritage Sites in the Republic of Ireland, according to the Office of Public Works (OPW), which manages it. Monks lived in stone beehive huts on its summit until they left in the thirteenth century. The key practical points from the OPW:

- **Access is by permitted boat only.** You can reach the island only with boat operators that hold a permit. The crossing is about 11.5km from Portmagee and other Kerry harbours.
- **The season is short.** The 2026 season ran from 12 May into September, and the island has now closed until 2027. Opening depends on the weather, and visits are prohibited outside the season.
- **It is a demanding climb.** Reaching the monastery means climbing 618 steep, uneven steps, rising more than 180 metres. Wear sturdy shoes with good grip, carry water and stay on the recognised paths.

## Safety and practicalities

The Cliffs of Moher site warns that the cliffs are "entirely vertical and the cliff edge is abrupt". The same applies to much of this coast: keep to paved or marked paths, and keep back from the edge in wind. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) advises drivers to check the RAC's Ireland guide for the rules of the road. It also recommends locking your car and parking in secure car parks where possible, as theft can happen. For more on travelling in the country, see our [Ireland guide](/countries/ireland/).

## Before you go

- Pick one or two regions rather than trying to drive all 2,500km in a single trip.
- If Skellig Michael is on your list, check the OPW's season dates for the coming year and travel only with a permitted boat operator.
- Check ferry timetables for the islands. Some, like the Great Blasket, are reached by ferry only in summer.
- Keep well back from cliff edges, especially in wind or rain, and stay on marked paths.
- Leave slack in your plans: boat trips and island crossings depend on the weather.
