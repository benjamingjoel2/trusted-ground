In the dry months, the long red-oat grass of the Maasai Mara fills with wildebeest and zebra moving north out of Tanzania's Serengeti. The Mara is really several areas side by side. There is the national reserve itself, managed in two parts, and around it a ring of community conservancies on land owned by Maasai families. The migration usually reaches the Mara around June or July and stays until about November, but the reserve and conservancies have resident wildlife all year.

## The reserve: two managers, one ecosystem

The Maasai Mara National Reserve is a single reserve run in two sections. According to the Mara Conservancy, management was divided in 1994. The eastern side went to Narok County Council and the north-western sector, the Mara Triangle, to Trans Mara County Council. The Triangle covers 510 square kilometres, about a third of the reserve.

Since 2001, the Mara Triangle has been run by the Mara Conservancy, a not-for-profit management company, under an agreement with the local council. When Kenya's new constitution merged the two county councils into the Narok County Government, the agreement passed to Narok, and a new ten-year agreement was signed in 2016. The Mara Conservancy describes the arrangement as the first public-private partnership of its kind in the region.

This matters for visitors because the two sections can have different ticketing and payment rules. Check which section your camp is in, and which section you will be driving in, before you go.

## The conservancies

Outside the reserve, much of the land belongs to Maasai landowners who have grouped their plots into conservancies and lease them for wildlife and tourism. The Maasai Mara Wildlife Conservancies Association (MMWCA), founded in 2013, coordinates them. According to the MMWCA, it has 24 member conservancies at different stages of development, covering about 450,000 acres and involving more than 15,000 landowners, who receive lease payments each year.

Each conservancy is different. The MMWCA's profiles give a sense of the range:

- **Mara North Conservancy** covers more than 29,170 hectares, with 783 Maasai landowners and 12 tourism partners.
- **Mara Naboisho Conservancy** covers more than 52,000 acres, with 636 landowners. The MMWCA says it strictly monitors tourist numbers and reduces vehicles.
- **Olare Motorogi Conservancy** covers about 33,386 acres, with 288 landowners.
- **Loita Hills Community and Wildlife Conservancy** offers nature hikes, walking safaris and cycling as well as game drives.

The conservancies also matter for wildlife. The Mara Conservancy notes that the Mara's own resident wildebeest, which once migrated in from the Loita Plains, now increasingly stay in the conservancies just outside the reserve, as farming and other human activity have cut their routes.

What a conservancy allows, such as walking, night drives or how many vehicles can gather at a sighting, is set by each conservancy and its tourism partners. Ask your camp directly.

## When to go

The Mara Conservancy says the migration "usually starts somewhere in June-July and goes on until November". It describes the true spectacle as the herds themselves, not just the river crossings: 1,245,000 wildebeest, 200,000 zebra, 18,000 eland and 500,000 Thomson's gazelle spread across the landscape.

River crossings happen often during the migration but are hard to predict, so build in several days if seeing one is a priority. Outside the migration months, you are trading the herds for quieter roads and the resident wildlife. For where the herds are in the rest of the year, read [the Great Migration in the Serengeti](/articles/serengeti-great-migration/).

## Rules and fees in the Mara Triangle

The Mara Conservancy publishes clear rules, enforced by rangers who can issue on-the-spot fines or remove vehicles from the park:

- **Crowding at sightings.** When more than five vehicles are waiting to see an animal, each one gets 10 minutes, and waiting vehicles must stay 100 metres back.
- **Inside the vehicle.** You may not get out except at designated points, stand on the roof or sit in the window, including at river crossings.
- **At crossings.** Keep quiet, keep back and never block moving wildebeest. The Conservancy says vehicles pushing herds into unsuitable crossing points have caused hundreds of animals to drown.

On fees, at the time of writing (November 2026), the Mara Conservancy says:

- The Triangle is cashless (M-Pesa and card).
- Tickets cover 12 hours, from 6am to 6pm, and do not carry over.
- Child rates apply from 9 to 17 years, and children aged 8 and under are free.
- You must show a passport or national ID to get a resident rate. Driving licences are not accepted.

Triangle fees are paid according to where you stay. The eastern side of the reserve is managed separately by Narok County Government, so check its fees and payment rules with your camp.

## Before you go

- **Apply for your eTA early.** The UK FCDO says you must get an electronic travel authorisation before travelling and recommends applying at least two weeks ahead.
- **Buy the mandatory insurance.** From 5 October 2026, most visitors need Kenyan government travel health insurance. Your own policy does not count. It is bought as part of the eTA application.
- **Ask which section and conservancy your camp is in.** This decides which fees, rules and activities apply.
- **Book through a properly established operator.** The FCDO advises using reputable operators, arriving in daylight, and never buying safaris from touts.
- **Check recent health advice.** At the time of writing, the FCDO notes confirmed Ebola cases in Kenya and screening at borders.
