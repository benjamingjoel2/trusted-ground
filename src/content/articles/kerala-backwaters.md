A Kerala houseboat moves at the pace of the water around it: past paddy fields lying below the level of the canals, past Chinese fishing nets on the approach to Kochi, past fields of red and white water lilies. The short answer for planners is that October to March is the cool, settled season for a cruise, the monsoon months bring heavy rain and a quieter, greener landscape, and booking through the district tourism council's official counter is the simplest way to know who you are dealing with.

## From rice barge to houseboat

Kerala Tourism explains that today's houseboats are reworked versions of the kettuvallam: "kettu" refers to a dwelling structure and "vallam" means boat. The originals were thatched boats on jackwood hulls, the planks joined with coir and coated with a black resin made from boiled cashew kernels. They carried rice and spices from Kuttanad to the port at Kochi, and a standard boat could hold up to 30 tonnes.

When lorries took over the cargo trade, many of these boats, most of them more than a century old, faced being abandoned. Converting them for travellers kept them on the water. A modern houseboat has furnished bedrooms with toilets, a living area, a kitchen and a deck, and is built and fitted with natural materials such as bamboo, areca nut wood, coir and coconut wood. Most are poled by local oarsmen, though some use an engine, and several boats can be joined into a "boat-train" for large groups.

Houseboats operate in Thiruvananthapuram, Kollam, Kottayam, Alappuzha, Ernakulam, Thrissur and Kasaragod districts. Alappuzha is the centre of it all, with about 500 boats according to Kerala Tourism.

## Kuttanad: farming below sea level

Much of the classic backwater scenery is Kuttanad, which spans parts of Kottayam and Alappuzha districts and is known as the rice bowl of Kerala. Kerala Tourism says farming here takes place up to 10 feet below sea level, the lowest farming altitude in India, on land reclaimed from the backwaters and protected by dikes. The largest reclaimed area, R Block, covers more than 850 hectares.

A popular cruise runs from Kottayam to Alappuzha through Kuttanad and along Vembanad lake, stopping at Pallom, where Colonel Munroe erected a lighthouse in 1813. A longer route from Alappuzha to Kochi passes through Kuttanad and stops at Kumarakom, known for its bird sanctuary, before the canals narrow towards Kochi. Kerala Tourism suggests early morning and late evening for birdwatching, and says these trips usually take one to two days.

## Seasons and the two monsoons

Kerala gets two monsoons. According to Kerala Tourism, the southwest monsoon, Edavappathy, arrives in June, and the northeast monsoon, Thulavarsham, arrives in mid-October, with clouds forming over the Bay of Bengal. Rain usually falls for a few hours at a time with sunny breaks, though it can occasionally last several days.

Kerala Tourism recommends October to March for backwater cruises, when the weather is cool and pleasant, and notes that heavy rain from June to September can affect travel plans. The monsoon is also the traditional season for Ayurvedic rejuvenation treatments.

The UK Foreign Office advises that India's monsoon usually runs from June to November and can bring flash flooding and landslides with little warning, closing roads and disrupting phone, internet and power. If you travel in the rains, build slack into your itinerary.

## Booking and safety on the water

The District Tourism Promotion Council (DTPC) in Alappuzha runs a houseboat pre-paid counter, which Kerala Tourism lists as the official booking point, with advance booking by deposit. Kerala Tourism's own cruise guidance is that a houseboat should be licensed and equipped with safety measures, and that you should book ahead in the busy season.

That advice matters. The FCDO warns that tourist boats and other small craft in India rarely carry life-saving equipment. Before you board, check for life jackets in sizes that fit everyone in your party, including children, and ask what the crew does in bad weather.

## Travelling responsibly

Kerala's Responsible Tourism Mission was launched on 20 October 2017 as the state government's agency for responsible tourism, with the stated aim of "making better places for people to live in and better places for people to visit". Kumarakom was an early responsible tourism destination; Kerala Tourism says the approach was introduced there in 2008, about six months before it reached Vythiri in Wayanad.

The mission promotes village life experiences and native experiences that introduce visitors to local crafts and community life. In Kumarakom, that can mean walking through paddy fields or trying toddy, the mild drink made from fermented coconut palm sap.

Simple ways to keep your footprint light on the water:

- Choose a boat that uses solar power for lighting, which Kerala Tourism says is preferred.
- Ask how the boat handles waste and sewage, and keep plastic out of the water.
- Spend money locally: village walks, crafts and food bought from residents keep income in backwater communities.
- Respect privacy; the canal banks are people's homes and workplaces.

## Before you go

- Plan for October to March for the most settled weather, or accept heavy rain and possible disruption between June and November.
- Book through the DTPC pre-paid counter in Alappuzha or ask your operator for the boat's licence.
- Check life jackets for every passenger, including children, before departure.
- Confirm the route, the number of hours under way and what meals are included.
- Ask whether the boat uses solar lighting and how waste is handled.
- Read the FCDO's India advice for monsoon and flood warnings.
