Senegal's two great heritage landscapes could hardly be more different. In the north, Saint-Louis is a historic island city reached across an Eiffel-style bridge over the Senegal River. South of Dakar, the Saloum Delta is a maze of mangroves, tidal channels and islands, best explored slowly by pirogue. Both are UNESCO World Heritage sites, and together they show the country's history, cuisine and wildlife at their most memorable.

## Saint-Louis: an island city on the river

Senegal's tourism agency, the Agence Sénégalaise de Promotion Touristique (ASPT), says that crossing the Pont Faidherbe into Saint-Louis often feels like stepping into another era. The city's cultural and architectural heritage has been on UNESCO's World Heritage List since 2000.

The bridge is the single crossing point onto the historic island, and the way most visitors first see the city. ASPT notes that it is named after General Louis Faidherbe, colonial administrator of Senegal from 1854 to 1861 and again from 1863 to 1865.

Saint-Louis also has a place in aviation history. ASPT describes it as a legendary stop on the Aéropostale airmail route and the departure point for the first crossing of the South Atlantic in 1930, associated with the Latécoère line and pioneering aviators such as Jean Mermoz.

Things to look out for:

- **Walking the island.** The streets are best seen on foot, at an unhurried pace.
- **Food.** ASPT says the cooking of Saint-Louis is renowned in Senegal and beyond, shaped by mixed influences and fresh fish and farm produce. It credits the region as the home of thieboudienne (ceebu jën), the rice-and-fish dish recognised by UNESCO.
- **The river.** ASPT highlights cruises on the Senegal River aboard the Bou el Mogdad, a boat that has sailed since the colonial era, with stops at bird reserves and riverside villages.

## The Senegal River delta and its birds

Saint-Louis sits where the river meets the sea, and ASPT describes the surrounding delta as a "gigantic oasis" where water and land intertwine, and the last stop for thousands of migratory birds. If birds are a priority, ask locally about visiting the nearby reserves.

## The Saloum Delta: mangroves, islands and shell mounds

The Saloum Delta is formed by the seasonal Sine and Saloum rivers, about 150km south-east of Dakar according to UNESCO. It is both a national park and a UNESCO biosphere reserve. UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere Programme describes 72,000 hectares of marine areas, 23,000 hectares of flooded land and 85,000 hectares of island terrain. Mangroves dominate, alongside sand dunes, open forest, coastal waters and sand islands.

What makes the delta a cultural as well as a natural site is the long human presence. UNESCO notes that sustainable shellfish gathering and fishing in brackish water, along with the processing of the catch for preservation and export, developed here, and that the shell mounds and burial mounds form "specific and exceptional cultural landscapes". ASPT says the delta is on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

**Wildlife.** UNESCO describes the delta as an important habitat for birds and a breeding site for migratory waterbirds, including more than 30,000 pairs of royal terns. It says the region holds a quarter of the world's royal terns. ASPT adds that around 400 bird species and 114 fish species have been recorded, and that the mangroves shelter mammals including hyenas, jackals, monkeys, warthogs, dolphins and manatees, as well as turtles, monitor lizards and crocodiles.

**Exploring by pirogue.** ASPT recommends pirogue trips through the delta's bolongs (tidal channels) and islands, visiting Niominka and Serer fishing villages, the Île aux Coquillages (Shell Island) and bird islands. Villages and bases it names include Toubacouta, Foundiougne, Palmarin, Dionewar, Niodor, Djiffer and Ndangane.

**Culture.** ASPT lists events including the International Festival of Sine Saloum and Serer Culture, traditional wrestling (lambi) after the harvest, and museums at Foundiougne and Kaolack.

## Protecting a fragile place

UNESCO warns that the mangroves are under permanent threat from the spread of rice cultivation and forest exploitation, along with forest fires, overfishing and the destruction of bird colonies. Village associations play a key role in mangrove restoration, running nurseries and reforestation plots, and community groups help set up eco-guards. As a visitor, keep your distance from nesting colonies, take rubbish away with you, and ask whether the excursion you are joining works with local communities.

## Practicalities and safety

- **Money.** The FCDO notes that there are few ATMs or banks outside Dakar and that ATMs often run out of cash, so carry enough for your trip into the delta. Mobile payment apps such as Orange Money and Wave are widely used.
- **Health.** The FCDO says medical facilities in Dakar are reasonable but limited elsewhere, so have travel insurance that covers medical treatment and repatriation.
- **Roads.** Main roads are generally good, but minor roads can be in very poor repair, and heavy rains between June and September can damage roads.
- **Customs.** Senegal is a predominantly Muslim country. The FCDO advises dressing and behaving modestly in public, especially in religious areas and during Ramadan.

## Before you go

- Allow at least a couple of days in each place: Saint-Louis rewards slow walking, and the delta is best explored by boat.
- Bring enough cash from Dakar; ATMs are scarce elsewhere.
- Ask your pirogue guide how they keep a respectful distance from bird colonies.
- Check road conditions if travelling between June and September.
