Steam drifts off an open-air pool edged with rock, a small folded towel rests on a bather's head, and the only sound is water spilling over stone. The onsen, Japan's natural hot spring bath, is one of the country's great pleasures, and it runs on a set of simple, firmly kept rules. Wash before you get in, bathe naked, keep your towel out of the water and stay quiet. If you have a tattoo, check the facility's policy before you go.

## What makes an onsen

The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) says Japan has more than 2,300 hot spring facilities, often clustered in hot spring villages. It distinguishes the onsen, whose water must largely come from a natural, mineral-rich source, from the sento, a public bath using ordinary heated water. You may see the word "tennen" (natural) added to signal the real thing.

Baths come in several forms. JNTO describes:

- **Rotenburo:** open-air baths.
- **Yukimi baths:** open-air baths with views of falling snow.
- **Ashinoyu:** geothermal footbaths, often found near stations or town centres.

Most public onsen are split into men's and women's sections, which JNTO notes are usually marked in blue and red. Mixed bathing exists but is hard to find.

## How to bathe, step by step

JNTO's bathing guidance is consistent across its pages. In order:

1. **Shoes off** before the changing room. Leave clothes and valuables in the lockers or baskets provided. JNTO warns that jewellery can discolour in some mineral waters.
2. **Undress completely.** Onsen bathing "is typically enjoyed in the nude", so swimwear stays in the changing area.
3. **Wash thoroughly** at the seated shower stations, rinsing off all soap. Then pour water over your body (kakeyu) to adjust to the temperature.
4. **Keep the small towel out of the bath.** Fold it on your head or rest it at the edge.
5. **Tie up long hair** so it doesn't touch the water.
6. **Keep it calm.** Speak softly, and don't swim or splash.

Phones and cameras belong in the changing room. JNTO says photography in the bathing area is "absolutely forbidden" to protect other bathers' privacy.

## Staying comfortable and safe

The water is hot, so pace yourself. JNTO suggests limiting a soak to about 30 minutes, taking breaks and drinking cool water if you feel overheated. It advises against drinking alcohol before bathing, and against taking food or drink into the bath. Kakeyu, the pre-bath pour, also helps you avoid a sudden jump in blood pressure as you enter.

## Tattoos

Tattoos are the question most visitors ask about. JNTO explains that tattoos were historically used by the authorities to mark criminals and became associated with organised crime. As a result, people with tattoos have often been refused entry to baths.

The picture is mixed and changing:

- JNTO cites a 2015 Japan Tourism Agency survey in which about 30 per cent of hotels and inns said they would let a guest with a tattoo use their communal baths.
- Rules are "very case-by-case", in JNTO's words. Some facilities post a sign saying tattoos are not allowed. Where there is no sign, ask the staff.
- Small tattoos can sometimes be covered with an adhesive, skin-coloured tattoo seal or a bandage. JNTO says these are "generally only suitable for very small tattoos".
- The most reliable option is a private bath, called a kashikiri-buro (or kashikiriburo), which many inns rent out.

JNTO suggests emailing a facility in advance to check its rules. Its tattoo guide also gives a useful phrase: "Tatu ga haitte iru no desu ga, daijobu deshou ka?" ("I have a tattoo, but is it all right for me to go in?").

## Onsen at a ryokan

A ryokan is a traditional Japanese inn, and JNTO says most have an onsen. Expect tatami floors, futon bedding and a cotton yukata robe to wear during your stay. JNTO notes that staff will explain the custom of removing your shoes indoors. Stays usually include a multi-course kaiseki dinner and a traditional Japanese breakfast, and are priced per person rather than per room.

Bathing arrangements at a ryokan follow a common pattern, according to JNTO:

- **Communal baths** are usually free for overnight guests. Many guests change into the yukata in their room, with underwear, and walk to the baths in it.
- **Private kashikiri baths** usually cost extra and need booking, either in advance or at the front desk.
- **En-suite baths:** some rooms have their own private bath, sometimes outdoors, included in the room price.

You don't have to stay overnight to bathe. JNTO points out that many onsen offer day-use or drop-in access, a gentle way to try it for the first time. It names towns such as Hakone, Kusatsu and Ginzan among the onsen destinations with traditional ryokan. ## Before you go

- Check each onsen's or ryokan's tattoo policy in advance, by email or on its booking page.
- If you have a tattoo, ask whether a private kashikiri bath is available and whether it needs booking.
- Pack a hair tie, and expect to bathe naked: swimwear is not used in the baths.
- Leave your phone in the locker; photography in the bathing area is not allowed.
- Keep soaks short, drink water and skip the alcohol beforehand.
- For a first visit, a day-use onsen is an easy way to learn the routine.
