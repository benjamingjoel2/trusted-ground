Sea lochs, empty beaches, mountain passes and island ferries crossing grey-green water: the Highlands and islands reward slow travel, but they also punish a rushed plan. Ferries need booking, many roads are single-track, and from late spring the midges arrive. Build in time, learn the passing-place etiquette, and pack repellent.

## The North Coast 500

The North Coast 500 (NC500) is a signed touring route that, according to VisitScotland, stretches for 516 miles around the north of Scotland. It starts and finishes in Inverness and can be driven in either direction. Highlights along the way include the Bealach na Bà pass to Applecross, Torridon, Ullapool, Smoo Cave near Durness, Thurso and John o' Groats.

VisitScotland says driving the loop non-stop would take around 11 to 13 hours, but most people spread it over five to seven days. That is the realistic minimum if you want to stop, walk and cope with slow roads. The route is open all year, but some attractions have shorter winter hours and the weather can be unpredictable, so check road conditions and opening times before setting off. For an overview of the wider country, see our [Scotland guide](/countries/scotland/).

## Single-track roads and passing places

Parts of the NC500, and many island roads, have narrow, single-track sections. VisitScotland advises checking your vehicle's size and planning your route before you leave, and urges care on the Bealach na Bà's hairpin bends.

The Highway Code (rule 155) sets out how single-track roads work. They are "only wide enough for one vehicle" and may have special passing places. In short:

- If a vehicle is coming towards you, or the driver behind wants to overtake, pull into a passing place on your left, or wait opposite one on your right.
- Give way to road users coming uphill whenever you can.
- If necessary, reverse until you reach a passing place to let the other vehicle pass.
- Slow down when passing pedestrians, cyclists and horse riders.

Because you may need to reverse some distance, be confident doing so in your vehicle, especially if you are driving a large motorhome.

## Island ferries with CalMac

CalMac Ferries sails to around 30 island and mainland destinations listed on its website, including Cowal and Dunoon, Kintyre and many of the islands. A few practical points from its booking pages:

- **Timetables are seasonal.** At the time of writing (November 2026), CalMac's winter timetable runs from 19 October 2026 to 25 March 2027. Tickets are valid only for the timetable season in which they were bought.
- **Book ahead, especially with a car.** You can book online and manage your journey through a personal account. Some routes have extra requirements: CalMac says vehicles travelling to Kerrera, Iona or the Small Isles need a permit before you buy tickets.
- **"Turn Up and Go" tickets** give you an open ticket for your chosen date without a specific sailing time. Unused journeys are non-refundable.
- **Foot passengers** with a bike, kayak or similar equipment should say so when booking, so CalMac can manage capacity.
- **Check service status before you travel.** CalMac publishes service status updates on its website and app, and asks passengers to check them before setting out.

Island weather can disrupt sailings, so avoid planning a tight connection between a ferry and a flight or a non-refundable booking.

## Midges and ticks

The Highland midge is a tiny biting fly, and on still evenings it can make camping or a lochside walk miserable. VisitScotland says midges are active from May to October, with peak activity in the middle of summer, and that they are "mainly found in the west Highlands, islands and damp rural areas", especially in woodland and near water. They are "often most active around dawn and dusk and in still, damp conditions".

What helps:

- Check a midge forecast before you set out, since activity changes with wind and humidity.
- Use a repellent made for midges, apply it to exposed skin and reapply as directed, especially after rain or sweating.
- Choose breezy spots for picnics and pitch tents with the wind in mind.

Ticks are the more serious concern. VisitScotland notes that "some ticks can transmit Lyme disease, a bacterial infection that requires medical treatment". Check yourself after walking through long grass or bracken, remove any tick promptly with a proper removal tool, clean the bite and watch it for a few weeks. Seek medical advice if the bite becomes infected or you feel unwell.

## When to go and where to stay

VisitScotland suggests visiting the NC500 between April and June or in September and October for milder weather, longer days and fewer people. Late spring has the added advantage of coming before the midges reach their peak.

Motorhomes and campervans are well suited to the route, and VisitScotland points drivers to campsites, holiday parks and motorhome stopovers. Use designated sites and overnight parking areas, and follow the Scottish Outdoor Access Code, which asks everyone to "enjoy Scotland's outdoors responsibly", use stoves rather than open fires when camping, and avoid disturbing wildlife.

## Before you go

- Book CalMac sailings early if you are taking a car, and check whether your route needs a vehicle permit.
- Check CalMac service status on the day and leave slack around ferry crossings.
- Read Highway Code rule 155 and practise using passing places before you hit the busiest roads.
- Allow five to seven days for the NC500 rather than trying to rush it.
- Pack midge repellent, a head net for still evenings and a tick removal tool.
- Use designated campsites and stopovers and follow the Scottish Outdoor Access Code.
