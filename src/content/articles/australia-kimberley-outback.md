Kakadu is open all year, but it is not the same park in every month. In the dry season most visitor sites are open and the 4WD tracks to the big waterfalls can be driven; in the tropical summer, floods close a number of areas while the landscape turns green and the storms roll in. When you go decides what you can reach, so plan the trip around the season rather than the other way round.

## Two seasons, or six

Parks Australia describes northern Australia's familiar pattern as a dry season and a wet tropical summer. Its own pages give slightly different boundaries: one puts the dry season at April to October and the tropical summer at November to March; its "When to come" page uses May to October and November to April. Treat the edges as soft.

Kakadu's Traditional Owners recognise six seasons, marked by changes in weather, flowering plants and bush foods. Parks Australia lists them as:

- **Kudjewk, monsoon season** (December to March, 24°C to 34°C): thunderstorms, heavy rain and flooding. Speargrass grows to over two metres and magpie geese nest in the wetlands.
- **Bangkerreng, "knock 'em down" storm season** (April, 23°C to 34°C): floodwaters recede and streams run clear, after violent, windy storms early in the season.
- **Yekke, cooler but still humid** (May to mid-June, 21°C to 33°C): morning mists over the plains and billabongs carpeted with water lilies. Patch burning begins.
- **Wurrkeng, cold weather season** (mid-June to mid-August, 17°C to 32°C): low humidity, most creeks stop flowing and waterbirds crowd the shrinking billabongs.
- **Kurrung, hot dry weather** (mid-August to mid-October, 23°C to 37°C): still "goose time"; sea turtles nest at Field Island and West Alligator Head.
- **Kunumeleng, pre-monsoon storm season** (mid-October to late December, 24°C to 37°C): increasingly humid, with afternoon thunderstorms. It can last from a few weeks to several months.

## The dry: open roads and busy lodges

Parks Australia calls the dry season the most popular time to visit, with most visitor sites open, and advises booking accommodation in advance because hotels get busy. It is the season for the waterfall country, including Jim Jim and Twin Falls.

Check before you build a trip around them, though. At the time of writing (November 2026), the Kakadu access report says the Jim Jim and Twin Falls area, including Twin Falls gorge, the escarpment walk and Karnamarr campground, is closed for 2026. The report is updated daily and lists target opening dates for each site, so read it again close to departure.

## The wet: storms, floods and fewer people

The tropical summer brings vivid green landscapes, spectacular flooding and afternoon storms, with fewer visitors. Flooding closes a number of attractions, but Parks Australia says popular areas such as Burrungkuy (Nourlangie) and Yellow Water are open all year. Waterfalls that are cut off by road can still be seen from the air on a scenic flight.

Park pass prices are lower in the tropical summer, which Parks Australia gives as November to May for pricing. Passes are valid for seven days, can be bought online before you arrive, and cover Kakadu only, not Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa or Northern Territory Government parks. Prices change, so check the current rate on the park's passes page. Extra permits are needed for activities such as overnight bushwalking or camping outside designated areas.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) notes that the tropical cyclone season, which affects the Northern Territory among other regions, normally runs from November to April, and that heavy rain can cause flash floods.

## Distances and roads

Kakadu covers almost 20,000 square kilometres. There are no taxis, buses or trains in the park: you need your own vehicle or a tour. Many sites can be reached in a 2WD, but West Alligator Head, Twin Falls, Jim Jim Falls, Maguk and Jarrangbarnmi are 4WD only. Parks Australia also warns that some hire companies do not allow 2WD cars on unsealed roads such as those to Gunlom and Yurmikmik, so read your rental terms.

On dirt roads, the park's advice is to drive slowly (80 km/h is too fast in most conditions), keep headlights on, watch for wildlife and obey road closures. Crossing flooded creeks and rivers can be extremely hazardous.

## Heat and crocodiles

Temperatures regularly reach 30°C or more and can exceed 40°C in summer. Parks Australia advises walking only in the cooler parts of the day, avoiding 2.30 pm to 6.30 pm, and in summer not walking after 11 am. Carry and drink at least one litre of water per person per hour.

Saltwater crocodiles live in most of Kakadu's waters, including floodplains, billabongs, creeks, rivers and the coast, and can attack in knee-deep water. Where there are no warning signs, assume crocodiles are present. The park says the safest place to swim is a public or hotel pool. Camp at least two metres above the high-water mark and at least 50 metres from the water's edge. Mobile coverage is absent in most of the park, so tell someone your plans for the day.

## Before you go

- Read the Kakadu access report in the week before you travel; site openings depend on the weather.
- Buy your park pass online and check whether the dry- or wet-season rate applies to your dates.
- Confirm your hire car may be driven on unsealed roads, and hire a 4WD if you want the southern falls.
- In the dry season, book accommodation well ahead.
- Plan walks for early morning and carry far more water than feels necessary.
- Never swim in rivers, billabongs or rock pools unless signs say it is safe.
