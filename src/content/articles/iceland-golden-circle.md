A rift valley where the old Icelandic parliament met in the open air, a hot spring that hurls boiling water into the sky every few minutes, and a two-step waterfall thundering into a canyon: the Golden Circle packs three of Iceland's best-known sights into a single loop in the south-west. All three are protected areas with their own rules. Drive it in a day, stay on the marked paths, and leave time for the weather.

## Þingvellir: where Iceland's parliament began

Þingvellir National Park sits beside the river Öxará, and the park describes it as the place that "epitomizes the history of Iceland". The name means "Assembly Plains". According to the park, the Alþing general assembly was established here around 930 and continued to meet at Þingvellir until 1798. For two weeks each summer, people from all over the country gathered to settle legal matters and to trade. The park calls it a "protected national shrine", and under a law passed in 1928 the protected area must always remain the property of the Icelandic nation.

The landscape is just as striking. The park explains that the area is part of the Atlantic Ocean ridge that runs through Iceland, and the movement of the earth's crust shows in the gorges and cracks that cut across it. Lake Þingvallavatn lies in a rift valley stretching south from the Langjökull glacier. Three of Iceland's five freshwater fish species live in the lake: brown trout, Arctic charr and three-spined stickleback. Birch woodland is typical of the area.

**Silfra**, a fissure in the rift valley separating the North American plate from the Hreppar microplate, is a popular diving site and one of the most important springs in the area, according to the park. Diving and snorkelling here are organised activities, so check the park's own diving page before you book.

The visitor centre at Hakið, by car park P1, houses an interactive exhibition, *Heart of Iceland*, which the park says takes about 40 minutes.

## Geysir and Strokkur

The Geysir hot spring area in Haukadalur gave the English language the word "geyser". The Environment Agency of Iceland, which manages the area, says the high-temperature field covers about 3 square kilometres and lies in Iceland's western volcanic belt. The protected area itself is 1.2 square kilometres and was declared on 17 June 2020.

Don't count on seeing Great Geysir erupt. The agency says it woke up for a while after earthquakes in 2000 but has been dormant since, and its last known eruption was in 2016. The show now belongs to **Strokkur**. Because it doesn't empty between eruptions, it refills quickly and erupts at regular intervals, reaching up to 30 metres.

The rules here are strict, and they exist for good reason:

- **Stay on the marked walkways.** This is required, both to protect the fragile hot-spring crust and for your own safety.
- **Throw nothing in.** Throwing stones, coins, soap or anything else into the springs is prohibited.
- **No bathing** in the hot springs, the pools or their run-off channels.
- **No cooking** in the springs or hot clay, apart from the area's traditional bread baking.

Rangers patrol the area all year round. Hotels, shops and restaurants sit just outside the protected area, and the agency notes there is a petrol station at Geysir, the last one before the Kjölur highland route.

## Gullfoss

Gullfoss on the river Hvítá was protected in 1979 to preserve the waterfall and the canyon below it, along with the vegetation around them. The Environment Agency says the falls drop in two steps: an upper fall of 11 metres and a lower fall of 20 metres. Average flow is 109 cubic metres per second, rising to about 130 in summer, and the largest floods on record reached 2,000 cubic metres per second.

Spray from the falls keeps heather, mosses and scrub growing. Because Gullfoss sits high above sea level, the growing season is short and damaged plants take a long time to recover. The agency points to unofficial paths that are slow to heal. Cutting vegetation, disturbing wildlife and damaging rock formations are all prohibited.

## Getting around and costs

Most visitors drive the loop themselves or join a coach tour. Þingvellir charges parking fees to cover the running of its car parks. At the time of writing (November 2026), the park lists 1,000 ISK for a passenger car with five or fewer seats, 1,200 ISK for a car with six to nine seats and 400 ISK for a motorcycle, with higher rates for buses. Check the park's parking page before you go, as rates are set by its board.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that the weather can change quickly and that distances between towns are greater than many visitors expect. It advises keeping dipped headlights on all year, fitting winter tyres when needed and checking the Icelandic Road Administration for road and weather conditions. It also lists going too close to cliff edges and hot springs among the common causes of accidents in Iceland. If you are travelling in the dark months, read our guide to [winter driving on the Ring Road](/articles/iceland-ring-road-winter-driving/). The Golden Circle can also be combined with a [northern lights](/articles/northern-lights-iceland-finland/) evening. For wider planning, see our [Iceland guide](/countries/iceland/).

## Before you go

- Check road and weather conditions with the Icelandic Road Administration on the morning you set out, especially between autumn and spring.
- Pay the Þingvellir parking fee for your vehicle category, and check current rates on the park's website.
- At Geysir, stay on the walkways and keep a respectful distance from Strokkur. The water is boiling.
- Don't throw coins or stones into any spring, and don't step off marked paths at Gullfoss.
- If you plan to dive or snorkel at Silfra, read the park's own guidance before booking.
- Leave your travel plans with your accommodation or with Safe Travel Iceland, as the FCDO recommends.
