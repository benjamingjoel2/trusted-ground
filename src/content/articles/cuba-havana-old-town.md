Old Havana's colonial streets and its music have drawn travellers for generations, but at the time of writing (November 2026) the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) advises against all but essential travel to Cuba. It cites severe shortages of fuel, electricity and water, flight suspensions and frequent protests. If you still need to travel, bring enough cash for your whole stay, because international bank cards are no longer accepted. You will also need a tourist visa and an online arrival form.

## Read this first: the current travel warning

The FCDO says Cuba's economic situation is getting worse. The national electricity grid has collapsed several times, and further collapses are likely. Low fuel reserves are disrupting transport, medical care, communications, water supply and street lighting. Cuban authorities have said that all of the country's international airports are without aviation fuel. Only Terminal 3 at Havana's José Martí International Airport is operating, and several airlines have suspended flights.

The FCDO also warns that travel insurance may be invalidated if you travel against its advice. If you are considering a trip, read the full FCDO guidance, talk to your insurer and decide whether the journey is essential before making plans. The rest of this article is for travellers who go ahead, and for anyone planning a future visit once conditions improve.

## The old city

Old Havana (La Habana Vieja) is the historic core of the Cuban capital. It is a district of plazas, churches, colonnades and wrought-iron balconies, best explored slowly on foot. Much of its pleasure lies in small details, such as a courtyard glimpsed through a doorway or a façade worn by sea air.

Under present conditions, explore in daylight. The FCDO reports that street lighting is affected by fuel shortages and advises travellers to pre-arrange transport and avoid walking at night. It specifically names the streets of Old Havana as a place where pickpockets and bag-snatchers operate.

## Music as living heritage

UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage lists recognise several of Cuba's musical traditions:

- **Rumba**, inscribed in 2016. UNESCO describes it as mainly associated with African culture, with elements from Antillean culture and Spanish flamenco. Rumba developed in the marginal neighbourhoods of cities such as Havana and Matanzas. It is performed with chants, gestures, handclapping and dance, sometimes using household or work utensils as percussion. The audience often joins in.
- **Son**, inscribed in 2025. UNESCO describes son as a music and dance practice that blends African and European influences, with lyrics drawn from daily life. The singer often improvises verses, and the *tres*, a stringed instrument, plays a key role.
- **Bolero**, inscribed in 2023 as a joint Cuban and Mexican element. It is a lyrical, sentimental song form performed everywhere from homes to concert halls and serenades.

UNESCO stresses that these traditions are passed on within families and neighbourhoods. When you hear them, you are hearing a living practice, not a museum piece. Ask before photographing or filming performers, and tip musicians in cash.

## Money, entry and getting around

**Cash only.** The FCDO says the Cuban Central Bank has announced that international cards such as Visa and Mastercard are no longer accepted, and you cannot withdraw cash from ATMs with a foreign card. Bring enough cash for your whole stay, plus a reserve for emergencies such as medical treatment or a delayed flight. US dollars and euros are widely accepted. Exchanging money anywhere other than an official Cadeca exchange house is illegal, and you could be fined or imprisoned. You must declare foreign currency worth more than USD 5,000. Ask your accommodation and tour providers whether you can pay them in advance by bank transfer. Our article on [paying a local operator abroad](/articles/paying-a-local-operator-abroad/) has general advice on payments.

**Entry.** Tourists need a tourist visa, also called a tourist card, before travelling. You can get one through the Cuban Embassy, a travel agent or the eVisa-Cuba site. You must also complete the D'Viajeros online arrival form within 72 hours before entering Cuba. Airlines check this at check-in. Download or print the QR code, because wifi may not be available when you arrive. Drones are not allowed, and some art and antiques need an export permit.

**Transport.** The FCDO says official yellow taxis are generally reliable. It advises avoiding private taxis and old private classic cars, which are unlikely to be properly licensed or to have modern safety features. Three-wheeled Coco-taxis may be unsafe, and tourists have been injured and killed in them. Criminals posing as drivers or tour operators operate at the airport and around Old Havana. Hurricane season runs from June to November.

## Before you go

- Read the latest FCDO travel advice for Cuba and confirm that your travel insurance covers the trip.
- Check with your airline that your flights are operating and that you have a realistic way to leave the country.
- Get your tourist visa and complete the D'Viajeros form within 72 hours before arrival. Save the QR code offline.
- Carry enough US dollars or euros in cash for the whole stay plus an emergency reserve, and exchange money only at Cadeca offices.
- Use official yellow taxis or pre-arranged transport, and avoid walking at night.
- Pack for power and water cuts: a torch, a power bank and water purification.
