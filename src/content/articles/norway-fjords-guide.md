Geirangerfjord and Nærøyfjord are the two fjords most people mean when they picture western Norway: sheer walls, waterfalls dropping straight into green water, and farms clinging to ledges hundreds of metres up. Both are on the UNESCO World Heritage list, but they sit in different parts of Fjord Norway and are reached in different ways. The short answer: base yourself near each in turn, use the train and boat network around Nærøyfjord, and plan the Geiranger side around roads that close in winter.

## Geirangerfjord: waterfalls and cliff farms

Visit Norway describes Geirangerfjord as a landscape carved by glaciers over a succession of ice ages. From the water you see the waterfalls that give the fjord its fame: the Seven Sisters (De syv søstrene), the Suitor (Friaren) and the Bridal Veil (Brudesløret).

High on the slopes are abandoned mountain farms. Skageflå can be reached by boat from Geiranger to Skagehola and then a steep path; Visit Norway says boats run hourly from 09:00 to 19:00 in the summer season. Knivsflå, several hundred metres up the mountainside, is a steep hike.

Above the village, the road to Dalsnibba climbs to a viewpoint about 1,500 metres above sea level, and Ørnevegen (the Eagle Road) twists north towards Trollstigen with the Ørnesvingen viewpoint looking down the fjord.

## Nærøyfjord: narrow, steep and quiet

Nærøyfjord is an arm of the Sognefjord in Aurland. According to the Visit Norway listing, it is 17 km long and only 250 metres wide at Bakka, its narrowest point, with mountains in the area rising to 1,761 metres. The same listing notes that the fjord can freeze over in cold winters.

The classic way to see it is by boat. A sightseeing route runs year-round between Gudvangen, Aurland and Flåm, taking you through the narrows past waterfalls and small farms.

## How to reach each fjord

**Geirangerfjord.** Visit Norway gives these options:

- **Train** from Oslo (about 5.5 hours) or Trondheim (about 4 to 5 hours) to Åndalsnes, the nearest station, then onward by bus.
- **Car** from Ålesund, about 3 hours. Ålesund Airport is the closest airport.
- **Ferry**: the Geiranger–Hellesylt car ferry runs all year, and fjord cruises run from Ålesund year-round.
- **Coastal ship**: in summer, the northbound Hurtigruten sails into Geiranger. In winter it calls at Ålesund instead, with a bus connection to Geiranger.

**Nærøyfjord.** The rail approach is the Flåm Railway. The rail operator Vy says the line is 20 km long, runs from Myrdal (a mountain station on the Bergen Line) down to Flåm at the head of the Aurlandsfjord, takes about an hour and runs every day, all year. From Flåm, the boat route continues through Nærøyfjord to Gudvangen.

## Travelling between the two

There is no single direct link between the fjords, so you will need to combine legs. One approach is to reach Myrdal on the Bergen Line, then take the Flåm Railway and the Nærøyfjord boat, then head north towards Ålesund or Åndalsnes for Geiranger, using the Hellesylt ferry to arrive by water. Check current timetables before committing to tight connections, as many services change between summer and winter.

If you are driving, the Geiranger–Trollstigen National Tourist Route is the scenic way in or out of Geiranger from the north. Norwegian Scenic Routes gives it as 104 km, including a ferry crossing between Eidsdal and Linge, with Trollstigen's eleven hairpin bends at one end.

## Seasons and road closures

The fjords are open all year, but the mountain roads above them are not. Norwegian Scenic Routes lists the Trollstigen stretch and the road from Langvatnet to Geiranger as winter-closed. Its past dates show how variable this is: Trollstigen has closed as early as 5 October and opened as late as 12 June. Even after opening, roads may close at night or in bad weather, so check status on the day.

The FCDO notes that you must use winter tyres when roads are covered in snow or ice, and may need studded tyres or snow chains. It also warns that police enforce campfire bans in many hiking areas in summer.

Boats are changing too. The Norwegian Maritime Authority says a zero-emission requirement for passenger ships in the World Heritage fjords applies from 1 January 2026 to ships under 10,000 gross tonnage, and from 1 January 2032 to larger ships. Expect the smaller sightseeing boats to be electric or otherwise zero-emission, and check what your cruise line plans if you are arriving by large ship.

## Before you go

- Check the live status of mountain roads on the day; past opening dates are only a guide.
- Book Flåm Railway and fjord boat tickets together if you need a tight connection.
- In winter, remember the Hurtigruten does not enter Geirangerfjord; plan for the bus from Ålesund.
- If driving in the cold months, confirm your hire car has winter tyres.
- Pack layers and waterproofs for boat decks, which are exposed even in summer.
