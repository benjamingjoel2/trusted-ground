From the airstrip at Lukla, the trail climbs for days through rhododendron and fir forest, past Namche Bazaar and on towards the foot of the world's highest mountain. The trek to Everest Base Camp, at 5,380 metres according to the Nepal Tourism Board, is one of the great walks on earth. It is also a serious high-altitude undertaking. You will need a licensed guide booked through a registered agency, the right permits, a slow itinerary with acclimatisation days, and some flexibility for weather.

## The route in outline

According to the Nepal Tourism Board (NTB), most trekkers fly from Kathmandu to Lukla, about 30 minutes by air. The alternative is to take a bus to Jiri and walk in. The NTB says it takes about nine days to reach Everest Base Camp from Lukla, and around five days to walk back down to Lukla.

The whole trek lies in Sagarmatha National Park, whose headquarters are at Namche Bazaar. The NTB lists Himalayan tahr, musk deer and snow leopard among the park's wildlife. Above about 3,500 metres the forests are dominated by silver fir, birch, rhododendron and juniper.

## Guides and permits

Since 31 March 2023, the NTB's revised TIMS rules require trekkers on listed routes to be "accompanied by licensed trekking guide". The Everest region is on the list, including the Everest Base Camp and Gokyo treks. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) says the same: "Do not trek alone."

Two documents are needed according to the NTB:

- **TIMS card.** The Trekking Information Management System card is arranged and paid for online by your registered agency. At the time of writing (November 2026), the NTB gives the fee as NPR 2,000, or NPR 1,000 for citizens of SAARC countries.
- **Sagarmatha National Park entry permit.** The NTB lists NPR 3,000 per person per entry for foreign visitors, and NPR 1,500 for SAARC nationals, at the time of writing. It says fees can be paid at the Tourist Service Center at Bhrikutimandap in Kathmandu or at the park entry points.

Fees change, so ask your agency for a full list of permits and any other charges in writing, and check the NTB's current figures. Our [guide to Nepal's trekking permits](/articles/nepal-trekking-permits/) explains the system in more detail.

## Altitude: going up slowly

Altitude sickness is the main health risk. The NHS says it usually occurs above 2,500 metres, and that the risk rises if you go up quickly. Its prevention advice is specific:

- Once above 3,000 metres, don't sleep more than 500 metres higher than the night before.
- Above 3,000 metres, take a rest day at the same altitude every three to four days.
- Don't drink alcohol, and keep hydrated.

The NHS lists the common symptoms as headache, nausea or vomiting, loss of appetite, exhaustion, dizziness and difficulty sleeping. They usually start six to ten hours after reaching altitude. If symptoms don't improve within a day, or get worse, it advises going down by about 300 to 1,000 metres. Confusion, problems with balance, breathlessness at rest or a cough with frothy or bloody spit are signs of a medical emergency.

This is why acclimatisation days matter. The FCDO warns of reports of guides taking "inexperienced trekkers to high altitudes too quickly", then calling in expensive helicopter evacuations. Be wary of itineraries or prices that look too good to be true. Our [altitude sickness guide](/articles/altitude-sickness-travel/) covers the subject in depth.

## The seasons

The NTB names October to November and March to May as the best seasons for Sagarmatha National Park. December to February brings snow, with daytime temperatures around 5°C, and June to September is the rainy season.

The FCDO describes the monsoon, roughly June to October, as a hazardous time, with flooding and landslides possible. It warns of hazards all year round, "especially above 3,000m", including sudden storms, avalanches and sun exposure. Pack warm clothes and waterproofs whatever the month.

## Flights, insurance and getting help

The flight to Lukla is the usual way in, and also a common source of delays. The FCDO says internal flights in high mountain areas "can be delayed by poor weather conditions and cancellations are common". It also notes that no Nepali airline currently meets international safety standards for inclusion on the UK Air Safety List. Build spare days into your plans at both ends of the trek.

The FCDO advises making sure your insurance "includes cover for mountain rescue services and helicopter costs". It also suggests carrying enough cash, as ATMs may not be available and cards may not be accepted in remote areas. ## Questions to ask your operator

- Is your agency registered with the government, and will our guide hold a current trekking guide licence?
- Which permits and local fees does the route need, and are they all included in the price?
- How many acclimatisation days are in the itinerary, and how high do we sleep each night above 3,000 metres?
- What happens if someone shows signs of altitude sickness, and who decides to descend?
- How many spare days do you build in for Lukla flight delays, and what does a delay cost us?
- What is your procedure for helicopter evacuation, and who pays at the time?
