El Chaltén sits at the foot of some of the most dramatic granite in the Andes, with Cerro Fitz Roy and Cerro Torre rising above beech forest, glacier lakes and clear rivers. Argentina's tourism board calls it the country's trekking capital, and most of its classic walks start on the edge of town. You can hike independently or with a guide, but you need a park ticket bought online for the northern trails, and in some cases you must register before you set out.

## Where El Chaltén is and when to go

El Chaltén is in Santa Cruz province, in the north of Los Glaciares National Park. According to Visit Argentina, the national tourism board, you reach it by road from El Calafate, a little over 200 km away, where the nearest airport is. The town sits at about 400 metres above sea level.

The board describes the climate as temperate and humid, with no clearly defined dry season, and generally cold for most of the year. Summer brings less rain but more wind. It recommends October to April as the main season, when the weather is milder and trails are in their best condition. Winter (May to September) brings snow, frost and far fewer visitors. It suggests spending three to seven days in the area.

The park's own trail leaflet makes a point worth remembering on every walk: the weather in town is not the weather in the mountains. Plan for layers, waterproofs, gloves and strong wind whatever the forecast in the village.

## The classic day hikes

Los Glaciares National Park (APN, Argentina's national parks administration) lists several walks that can be done in a day from town:

- **Laguna de los Tres (Fitz Roy).** The most popular hike, reaching the closest natural viewpoint to the Fitz Roy massif. The park rates it medium to high difficulty, 8 to 9 hours and about 25 km in total. The park leaflet warns that the final section is very steep and dangerous in wind and rain. Fitz Roy itself is 3,405 m.
- **Laguna Capri.** Shares the start of the Fitz Roy trail and is the park's suggestion if you only have one day.
- **Laguna Torre (Cerro Torre).** Three to four hours each way along the Fitz Roy river valley to the lake below Cerro Torre (3,102 m), about 18 km return.
- **Miradores de los Cóndores and de las Águilas.** Short walks near town with views over El Chaltén and Lago Viedma.
- **Chorrillo del Salto.** A waterfall, described by the leaflet as the easiest trail in the area.
- **Loma del Pliegue Tumbado.** About four hours each way with roughly 1,000 m of ascent, for wide views on a clear day.

Visit Argentina also lists Piedras Blancas, the Huemul glacier and Laguna Toro among the most popular routes. Longer routes are possible over several days, camping only in permitted sites.

## Tickets, portals and registration

The town is free to enter, but the trails into the park are not. Visit Argentina lists three access portals in the northern zone: Los Cóndores (before you reach town), Base Fitz Roy (for the Torre, Laguna de los Tres and Chorrillo del Salto trails) and Río Eléctrico, about 16 km along provincial route 41.

At the time of writing (November 2026), APN's published fees, valid from 1 June 2026 and the same for the northern and southern zones, are:

- General rate: ARS 50,000
- Argentine residents: ARS 25,000
- Santa Cruz province residents: ARS 8,000
- Students (with Argentine student credentials): ARS 15,000

In the northern zone, tickets are sold **online only**, either on the website or by scanning the QR code at the portal, and payment is by credit or debit card. APN says cash is not accepted. Fees change, so check the tarifas page before you go.

APN says registration at the park information centre is compulsory for climbing and for trekking in remote areas, and for walking the trails from May to September. There is also an online form, the Registro de Uso de Montaña en Áreas Remotas. The park leaflet marks Laguna Toro as an unmarked trail where you must register. Climbing and expeditions onto the Southern Patagonian Ice Field must be done with authorised providers or guides and reported to the park.

## Rules on the trail and wildlife

The park leaflet sets out what it expects from walkers:

- Walk only on marked trails, and camp only in permitted sites.
- No fires anywhere in the park. If you smoke, do it only at rest stops and carry the filter out.
- Carry all your rubbish home, and use the latrines only for their purpose.
- Do not wash or use soap in rivers and streams. The leaflet says the water is pure, and it asks you to keep it that way.
- No drones and no pets. Do not feed animals.
- Work out your walking time so you are back before dark.

Two animals get special mention. The huemul, an endangered deer found only in the forests of southern Argentina and Chile, is a protected Natural Monument. If you meet one, keep your distance, move slowly, stay quiet and tell a ranger. For a puma, the advice is not to run or turn your back. If it looks aggressive, raise your arms and shout. The leaflet also warns about invasive European wasps, which can sting more than once.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) advises that your travel insurance should cover all the activities you plan, including helicopter rescue and medical evacuation. It notes that you will have to pay for most helicopter rescues in Argentina.

## Before you go

- Buy your park ticket online before you walk to a northern-zone portal, and bring a card. APN says cash is not accepted there.
- Check whether your route or the season (May to September) needs registration, and use the online form or the park information centre.
- Stop at the Ceferino Fonzo visitor centre at the entrance to town for maps and current trail conditions.
- Pack for mountain weather, not town weather: waterproofs, warm layers, gloves, sun protection and enough food and water.
- Check that your insurance names hiking and covers helicopter rescue.
- Allow spare days: Visit Argentina suggests three to seven, and wind or rain can close the high viewpoints.
