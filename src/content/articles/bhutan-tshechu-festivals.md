Cymbals clash, drums and trumpets sound across a fortress courtyard, and dancers in silk and carved masks wheel across a crowded courtyard. A tshechu is Bhutan's great religious festival, held in the courtyards of its dzongs and temples. Each district has its own, and the dates follow the lunar calendar, so they change every year. Check the official calendar before you book flights.

## What a tshechu is

According to Bhutan's Department of Tourism, the word tshechu means "tenth". Guru Rinpoche (Padmasambhava) promised to return on the tenth day of each month of the Bhutanese lunar calendar, so that day is considered especially auspicious. The department describes tshechus as festivals that "often span several days and represent a significant part of the country's cultural heritage".

The setting matters. The department explains that the central tower of every dzong is usually surrounded by "a large and open courtyard where religious festivals are held". Tshechus are also where visitors can hear the religious music and chants of the monastic body, alongside regional folk music and dance.

## The masked dances

The mask dances, or cham, are the heart of a tshechu. They are sacred performances, not shows staged for visitors.

UNESCO has inscribed one of them, the mask dance of the drums from Drametse, on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (2008, first proclaimed in 2005). UNESCO's description gives a sense of what you'll see at any tshechu:

- It is performed at the Drametse festival in eastern Bhutan, which takes place twice a year, in honour of Padmasambhava.
- Sixteen masked male dancers in colourful costumes perform, accompanied by an orchestra of ten men.
- The instruments are cymbals, trumpets and drums, including the large cylindrical bang nga.
- A calm section represents peaceful deities, and a rapid, athletic section represents wrathful ones.
- People attend to receive blessings, and the dance has also come to represent Bhutanese national identity.

On the last day of some festivals a thongdrel (spelled thongdral in the official calendar) is displayed. The 2027 calendar marks this for Paro, Gangtey and Pemagatshel.

## When the festivals happen: the 2027 calendar

The Department of Tourism publishes a tentative festival calendar each year. Its 2027 list, at the time of writing (November 2026), includes:

- **Punakha Dromchoe:** 13 to 15 February, Punakha Dzong, followed by the **Punakha Tshechu**, 16 to 18 February.
- **Paro Tshechu:** 18 to 22 March, Rinpung Dzong, Paro, with the thongdrel on the last day.
- **Ura Yakchoe:** 18 to 22 April, Ura Lhakhang, Bumthang.
- **Nimalung Tshechu:** 12 to 14 June, Bumthang, and **Kurjey Tshechu**, 14 June, Bumthang.
- **Wangdue Tshechu:** 8 to 10 October, Wangduephodrang.
- **Thimphu Tshechu:** 10 to 12 October, Tashi Chhodzong, Thimphu.
- **Gangtey Tshechu:** 13 to 15 October, Gangtey Gonpa, with the thongdrel on the last day.
- **Jakar Tshechu:** 6 to 9 November, Jakar Dzong, Bumthang.
- **Black-necked Crane Festival:** 11 November, Gangtey Gonpa, Phobjikha.
- **Jambay Lhakhang Drup:** 14 to 17 November, Bumthang.

The calendar is labelled tentative, and dates can shift. Confirm them nearer the time.

The department also describes the Punakha Dromche as commemorating a 17th-century victory over Tibetan forces, and the Black-necked Crane Festival as a welcome for the cranes that winter in the Phobjikha valley, with children's crane dances in the courtyard of Gangtey Monastery.

## Attending respectfully

There is no formal festival dress code for visitors. But a tshechu is a religious event in a sacred building, and the Department of Tourism says that at places of religious significance "respectful smart-casual clothing that covers your body from shoulders to knees is appropriate and appreciated". It also says guides are required to enter dzongs and other monuments, and some charge an entry fee.

Sensible courtesies follow from that:

- Dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees.
- Stay out of the dancers' space in the courtyard.
- Follow your guide's lead on where you may stand and photograph. Drones generally cannot be brought into Bhutan, according to the department.

Popular festivals draw large crowds, so book accommodation early. Visitors pay Bhutan's Sustainable Development Fee for each night; see our [guide to the SDF](/articles/bhutan-sustainable-development-fee/). Our [Bhutan travel guide](/countries/bhutan/) covers the wider trip.

## Before you go

- Check the Department of Tourism's latest tentative festival calendar before booking flights.
- Allow a spare day either side in case dates move.
- Pack clothing that covers shoulders and knees for dzong courtyards.
- Ask your guide about photography rules at each festival.
- Plan for the Sustainable Development Fee on every night of your stay.
- If you want to see a thongdrel, check which day it is displayed. The 2027 calendar lists the last day at Paro, Gangtey and Pemagatshel.
