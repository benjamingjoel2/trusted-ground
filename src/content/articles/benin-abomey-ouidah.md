Abomey, capital of the old kingdom of Danxomè (Dahomey), and Ouidah, the coastal town from which enslaved people were shipped to the Americas, are the two places where Benin's history is easiest to see. Both are in the south of the country. The areas where the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) advises against travel are in the north. Abomey's palace site is in the middle of a major redevelopment, so check what is open before you set your itinerary.

## The Royal Palaces of Abomey

Abomey's palace site is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and was the seat of the kings of Danxomè. The Government of Benin describes a project to turn the town into a "mini cité muséale", a small museum city. It has three parts:

- **Restoring the palaces.** The government names five royal palaces for rehabilitation: those of kings Ghézo, Glèlè, Béhanzin, Agoli-Agbo and Akaba (with Queen Hangbé).
- **A new museum.** The Agence Française de Développement (AFD), which is co-financing the project, describes a 4,000 m² museum designed in the image of a royal palace. It will display the 26 works from Abomey's royal treasures that France has returned.
- **A heritage walk.** A landscaped walking route, plus restoration of the surrounding enclosure wall.

The government gave the total cost as 30 billion CFA francs, 23 billion of it from AFD. It is run by Benin's national heritage and tourism agency, ANPT. The government also announced a request to France for 35 further cultural objects and archives in 2026.

**What this means for a visit:** while the work continues, parts of the site may be closed, moved or shown differently from older descriptions. The government's own completion estimate dates from 2023. Ask your operator, close to your travel dates, which palaces and galleries are open.

## Ouidah and the Route of the Slave

Ouidah lies on the coast about 30 km from Cotonou. The Government of Benin describes it as the place from which thousands of enslaved people, captured inland in Dahomey or taken as prisoners of war, passed through the Door of No Return on their way to the Americas. King Agadja of Abomey made Ouidah his seaport in 1727.

The town's main memorial route is **La Route de l'Esclave**. The government describes it as a walk of about 3 km from the Place aux Enchères (the auction square) to the Door of No Return on the beach. On the way it passes:

- the Tree of Forgetting
- the Case Zomaï, where captives were held in darkness
- the Zoungbodji memorial
- the Memorial of Repentance

The government also lists the Ouidah History Museum in the former Portuguese fort, the sites of the other European forts, Afro-Brazilian houses, and the Temple of Pythons, which faces the Catholic basilica.

Ouidah is also an important centre of Vodun. The government notes that 10 January is a national Vodun day in Benin. The FCDO says that around the festival in early January accommodation is limited and pickpockets are active, so book well ahead if you travel then.

## Current travel advice

At the time of writing (November 2026), the FCDO's Benin advice was last updated on 4 November 2026. In summary:

- **Advises against all travel** to the Parc du W and the Mékrou and Djona hunting zones, the area between the Parc du W and the Niger border, Pendjari National Park and its hunting grounds, and anywhere else within 5 km of the Burkina Faso border.
- **Advises against all but essential travel** to the area between the RNIE 2 highway (Tchaourou to Malanville) and the Nigerian border, and to the rest of the Alibori and Atacora departments.
- **Terrorism and kidnap.** The FCDO says terrorists are very likely to try to carry out attacks in Benin and that the risk of kidnap is higher in the north.
- **Unrest.** The FCDO travel advice (updated 4 November 2026) refers to a recent attempted coup and advises avoiding demonstrations and complying with any curfews.
- **Crime and roads.** Street crime is common in Cotonou. The FCDO advises against driving outside towns after dark and calls the sea currents along the whole coast, including Ouidah's beaches, very strong.

## Entry and guides

According to the FCDO, British passport holders need a visa, applied for online at least four working days before arrival, and must show a yellow fever vaccination certificate on entry.

Benin is also regulating its guides. Under decree no. 2025-668, the national tourism agency, Bénin Tourisme, held a census and online registration of working tourism guides throughout October 2026. Guides already working could apply for a provisional authorisation to practise. It is reasonable to ask whether your guide has registered. For how licensing works more generally, see our licences guide and our [Benin guide](/countries/benin/).

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which parts of the Abomey palace site and museum are open on my dates, given the restoration work?
- Will my guide at Abomey and Ouidah be registered under Benin's 2025 guide regulations?
- Does any part of the itinerary go into or near the areas the FCDO advises against, and does my insurance cover every stop?
- How do you plan road journeys so we are not driving outside towns after dark?
- If I travel around the January Vodun celebrations, are accommodation and transport already confirmed in writing?
- What is your plan if unrest or a curfew affects the trip?
