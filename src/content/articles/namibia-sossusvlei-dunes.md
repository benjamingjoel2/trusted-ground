Sossusvlei is where the Namib Desert puts on its most famous show: star-shaped dunes in shades of apricot, red and maroon rising above bleached white clay pans, and in Deadvlei the blackened skeletons of camelthorn trees that died centuries ago. It sits inside the Namib-Naukluft Park, is reached through the Sesriem gate, and rewards an early start. The practical challenges are distance, heat and timing.

## The dunes and the pans

Namib-Naukluft is Namibia's largest conservation area. Namibia Wildlife Resorts (NWR), which runs accommodation in the park, describes almost 50,000 square kilometres of dunes, gravel plains and mountains, and says some of the dunes at Sossusvlei are among the highest in the world, reaching up to 325m measured from the base.

According to NWR, the star-shaped dunes are formed by strong winds blowing from several directions, and are at their tallest where the west-flowing Tsauchab River ends in the vlei. At their feet lie large clay pans. The one most visitors come to see, often called Deadvlei (NWR calls it "Dead Pan"), is an expanse of dried white clay punctuated by the skeletons of camelthorn trees that have been carbon-dated to between 500 and 600 years old. When enough rain falls inland for the Tsauchab to flow and fill the main pan, flamingos and other waterbirds arrive.

The dunes are part of the Namib Sand Sea World Heritage site. The IUCN, which assesses natural World Heritage sites, describes it as the only coastal desert in the world with extensive dune fields influenced by fog, covering more than three million hectares. Sand is carried from the interior of southern Africa by the Orange River, pushed north by ocean currents, and blown inland by the wind. The IUCN records sixteen distinct dune types, with star dunes in the east, where Sossusvlei lies.

## Getting in: Sesriem, the gate and the shuttle

Sesriem is the gateway. NWR's Sesriem Campsite sits beside Sesriem Canyon, a narrow gorge about 1km long and 30 to 40m deep, with pools at its foot that refill after good rains. The name comes from early settlers tying six lengths of rawhide thong (riem) together to draw water from those pools.

From Sesriem a road runs west towards Sossusvlei. Two things to know:

- **The last stretch.** The IUCN notes that a shuttle concession operates between the Sossusvlei 2x4 car park and the Deadvlei parking area. In an announcement effective from 15 October 2024, NWR said it would stop running its own shuttle from the 2x4 area and operate it only from Sesriem Campsite, where tickets are now sold. Check the current arrangement when you arrive.
- **Gate hours.** The park gate at Sesriem has set opening and closing times. We could not confirm the current hours from an official source, so check them with NWR or your accommodation before you plan a sunrise climb.

MEFT, Namibia's environment and tourism ministry, lists walking trails, 4x4 routes, photography and stargazing among activities in the park. NWR's Sesriem rates page lists guided morning drives, and MEFT notes that Sossus Dune Lodge offers sunset drives and walks to Sesriem Canyon.

## The heat, and when to go

The desert is at its most beautiful in soft early and late light, and at its most dangerous at midday. The Namibia Tourism Board says summer runs from October to April and that temperatures in the southern region, especially in the Namib Desert, can exceed 40°C. Winter days (May to September) are pleasant, but nights in the Namib can drop to freezing.

To manage the heat:

- **Climb early.** Plan dune walks for the first hours after the gate opens, and be off exposed sand before the hottest part of the day.
- **Carry more water than you think.** The IUCN describes the wider Sand Sea as having virtually no roads or facilities of any kind.
- **Pack for both extremes.** Bring sun protection for the day and warm layers for winter nights.

## Driving and staying on the track

Most visitors self-drive. The FCDO warns that accidents are common on Namibia's gravel and dirt roads, that many rental policies do not cover single-vehicle accidents, and that gravel roads can deteriorate in the rainy season from January to April. It advises carrying plenty of water, checking road conditions before setting off and avoiding driving at night outside towns because of wildlife and livestock. Our article on [self-driving Namibia and Etosha](/articles/namibia-self-drive-etosha/) covers this in more depth.

Inside the park, stay on designated routes. The IUCN identifies illegal off-road driving, sanitation and pollution as the key tourism impacts around Sossusvlei and Sesriem, where it says three-quarters of the park's visitors are concentrated, and notes heavy pressure from tourism there, including vehicle tracks.

## Before you go

- Confirm the current Sesriem gate opening and closing times with NWR or your accommodation.
- Buy shuttle tickets for the Deadvlei stretch at Sesriem Campsite, or check the current arrangement on arrival.
- Start early and carry plenty of water; desert temperatures can exceed 40°C in summer.
- Take warm layers in winter, when Namib nights can reach freezing.
- Check your hire car insurance for gravel-road and single-vehicle accidents.
