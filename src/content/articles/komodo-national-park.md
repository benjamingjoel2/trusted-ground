Dry hills fall into turquoise water, a crescent of pink sand curves around a bay, and on the trails a lizard up to three metres long lies motionless in the shade. Komodo National Park, off the west coast of Flores in Indonesia, is one of the few places on Earth to see Komodo dragons in the wild. The short answer: you visit by boat from Labuan Bajo, you walk only with a ranger or licensed guide, and since 1 April 2026 the park has capped visitor numbers at 1,000 a day.

## The park and its dragons

According to Indonesia's Ministry of Tourism, the park covers 219,322 hectares and is made up of three major islands, Komodo, Rinca and Padar, plus 26 smaller ones, all volcanic in origin. Komodo is the largest island and the busiest; Rinca has the largest dragon population; Padar is known for the viewpoint at its summit.

Komodo dragons are the largest lizards alive. The ministry describes adults reaching about three metres and more than 150kg, hunting deer, buffalo and wild boar from ambush. Their saliva carries harmful bacteria and an anticoagulant venom, so prey that escapes the first attack often dies later. The species is listed as vulnerable and lives only on five islands in and around the park: Komodo, Rinca, Padar, Gili Dasami and Gili Motang. Population estimates in official tourism material range from about 3,000 to 5,700.

## Park rules on land

Walking among dragons is tightly controlled, and for good reason. The ministry's guidance is that a local guide is mandatory for treks, mainly for safety. On Rinca, rangers meet visitors at the ticket checkpoint and lead the walk, and treks on Komodo Island are ranger-led too. Tourists cannot stay overnight on Rinca.

Indonesia Travel's safety rules for dragon areas are:

- go with a licensed guide or park ranger, because dragon behaviour can be unpredictable;
- keep at least five metres away from the animals;
- never approach or feed them;
- wear light clothing and sturdy walking shoes;
- follow park rules and do not litter.

According to Indonesia Travel, a permit is required and can be obtained at the park office in Labuan Bajo; the fee covers island access and conservation contributions. The page does not give an amount, and fees can change, so confirm it with the Komodo National Park Office (Balai Taman Nasional Komodo) rather than relying on figures quoted online.

## The daily visitor cap

The Komodo National Park Office has limited visits to 1,000 people a day from 1 April 2026, Antara reported in April 2026. The cap covers Komodo, Rinca and Padar islands and marine tourism at 23 dive sites.

The park's reasoning is ecological. In 2025 it recorded 429,509 visits, about 68 per cent of them from abroad, above a carrying capacity of 378,870 a year set by a 2022 study. The park office says heavy human activity can change dragon behaviour and reduce their wariness of people, and that coral reef condition has declined as visitor numbers grew without enough management. During the trial period, it suggested separating cruise ship allocations from regular visitor quotas.

Booking arrangements under the cap may change, so ask your operator, or the park office, how your visit is being booked against the quota before you pay.

## Diving and the seasons

The park's waters are as much of a draw as its dragons. The ministry names sites including Makassar Reef (Manta Point) for manta rays; Batu Bolong, with strong currents and pelagic fish, suited to experienced divers; Siaba Besar, a pinnacle for sharks and tuna; and the coral gardens off Pink Beach, whose colour comes from red-pigmented microorganisms called foraminifera.

According to Indonesia Travel:

- the dry season runs from about April to October;
- January to March is the rainy season, which often disrupts boat trips, though the south of the park can be good for diving in January and February;
- September to November is peak season for manta rays, whale sharks and dragons;
- July and August are the dragons' mating season, when they are harder to spot.

Seas are calmer early in the day. The ministry warns that afternoon water is choppy, and strong currents make boat travel on the Padar route after 11am very difficult, so morning starts are usual.

## Boat and dive safety

Labuan Bajo on Flores is the gateway, with direct flights from Bali and Jakarta. Day trips by boat take three to four hours each way depending on the vessel and sea, and liveaboards typically run three to seven days.

The UK Foreign Office advises that sea conditions in Indonesia can change rapidly and that safety standards vary between providers. Vessels can be crowded, overloaded or poorly maintained. Check life jackets and lifeboats before you sail, bring child-sized life jackets if you need them, and avoid travelling after dark unless the boat is properly equipped. For diving, the FCDO says standards are not always as high as in the UK: check the operator's credentials, make sure there is oxygen on board, and ask whether divers can be transferred to a hyperbaric chamber.

## Questions to ask your operator

- How is my visit being booked against the park's 1,000-a-day quota, and which islands and dive sites are included?
- Is the park entry fee included in the price, and will I get an official receipt?
- Will a park ranger lead every land visit?
- What safety equipment is on board, including life jackets in every size we need and oxygen for divers?
- What is the plan if afternoon seas or the rainy season make a crossing unsafe?
- For divers: what are the instructors' credentials, and where is the nearest hyperbaric chamber?
