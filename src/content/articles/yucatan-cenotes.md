Climb down a stone stairway into a cenote and the heat of the Yucatán falls away. Light drops through a hole in the rock roof, roots hang towards clear water, and the pool may run on into flooded caves. Cenotes are among the great swims of Mexico, but they are also windows onto the groundwater the region depends on. In Yucatán state, swimmers must follow legal rules: no sunscreen or soap in the hours before you swim, a shower first, and no food, alcohol or rubbish.

## What a cenote is

Yucatán state's cenote regulation (Decreto 193/2014, last amended in 2017) defines cenotes, caves and grottoes as underground hollows formed by limestone dissolving or collapsing, which may or may not expose groundwater or underground rivers. The regulation cites the state's census of cenotes and grottoes, which counted 2,241 in Yucatán: 1,014 open cenotes, 734 closed, 342 semi-open, and 151 grottoes.

The regulation explains why they need protecting. It cites the state environment ministry's finding that the features of cenotes make the aquifer vulnerable to damage from activity at the surface. The threats range from waste entering the water to clearing vegetation, installing lights and the impact of frequent recreational use.

## The rules for swimmers

Article 6 of the Yucatán regulation sets out what anyone entering a cenote, cave or grotto must do. In plain English:

- **No drugs or alcohol**, inside or before you go in.
- **No smoking, eating or drinking** inside.
- **Do not damage** the natural or built features, plants or animals.
- **Leave no rubbish**, inside or around the site.
- **No running or jumping** inside.
- **No sunscreen, tanning lotion, soap or similar products** for at least three hours before you enter. At cenotes run as bathing sites, organic biodegradable products are the exception.
- **Shower before you go in**, at sites run as bathing places.

The City of Mérida has its own regulation for cenotes within the municipality. It also bans tanning lotions or other chemical liquids that harm aquatic life, and food other than water. It forbids moving or removing pre-Hispanic objects or prehistoric fossils. Where artificial light is needed in enclosed or semi-enclosed cenotes, it requires visitors to use hand torches only. It also notes that activities in cenotes and caves with archaeological, historic or palaeontological remains need authorisation from INAH, Mexico's national institute of anthropology and history.

These are Yucatán state and Mérida rules. Quintana Roo, home to Tulum and Playa del Carmen, has its own laws, and individual sites post their own conditions. Follow the strictest rule you see.

## Sunscreen: what to use

Yucatán's exception for "organic biodegradable" products applies only at bathing sites, and the rule gives no test for the label. The safest way to follow Yucatán's rule is not to apply any sunscreen in the three hours before you swim. Cover up with clothing instead, and apply only after your last swim.

If you must protect your skin beforehand at a site that allows it, a UNAM (National Autonomous University of Mexico) biologist quoted in the university's Gaceta recommends physical blockers made from minerals such as zinc oxide or titanium dioxide. Chemical filters such as oxybenzone and octinoxate have been linked to damage on the reefs of the Mexican Caribbean. Check the cenote's own rules either way.

## Staying safe in the water

Yucatán's regulation requires every cenote run as a bathing site to have at least one lifeguard on duty whenever it is open, and life jackets to rent in sufficient numbers for its capacity. It bans tourist use of cenotes that are highly contaminated or dangerous to reach. Guides must be trained in first aid, tell visitors which areas are off limits, and show them how to use safety equipment.

Practical points:

- Wear the life jacket if one is offered, especially in deep water or if you are not a strong swimmer.
- Do not jump or dive from ledges. The state rules ban jumping, and depths and hidden rock are hard to judge.
- Never swim into caves or tunnels unless you are on a guided dive trained for it.
- Watch your footing on wet steps and ladders.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that adventure-sports equipment in Mexico, including for scuba diving, may not meet UK safety and insurance standards. British nationals have been injured and in some cases killed. If you plan cavern or cave diving, the FCDO advises checking that the company is reputable, takes safety precautions, uses up-to-date equipment and is fully licensed and insured, and that your travel insurance covers the activity.

## Getting there

Most cenotes are reached by road from Mérida, Valladolid or the Riviera Maya. If you cross into Quintana Roo, the FCDO notes that you must pay a state entry tax, which you can pay online, and show proof of payment at the airport before you leave. ## Before you go

- Skip sunscreen, repellent and lotion on cenote days until after your last swim, and pack a rash vest or long-sleeved swim top instead.
- Use the showers before you enter, even if nobody is checking.
- Leave food and alcohol outside, and take all your rubbish away.
- Ask whether a lifeguard is on duty and take a life jacket if you are unsure of the depth.
- Check the specific cenote's rules on arrival, especially in Quintana Roo, where state rules differ.
- For diving, use a properly licensed operator and confirm your insurance covers it.
