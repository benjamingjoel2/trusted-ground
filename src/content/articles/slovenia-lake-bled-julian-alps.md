A church on a tiny island in a green glacial lake, a castle on the cliff above, and behind them the limestone peaks of the Julian Alps: Bled is one of the most recognisable views in Europe. Just beyond it lies Triglav National Park, Slovenia's only national park, and on its western side the Soča, a river of startling blue-green. You can see a great deal in a few days. In return, the park asks you to keep to its rules.

## Lake Bled

According to the Bled tourist board, the lake basin was carved out by the Bohinj glacier. A large rock in its path was scraped down until only the present island was left, and when the ice melted the basin filled with water. The lake is up to 30 metres deep in places and home to 19 species of fish. Thermal springs on its eastern shore stay above 20°C. The lake's surface reaches about 25°C in summer and can drop to 4°C in winter. In very cold winters it freezes over.

A path of about 6 kilometres runs right round the shore. You can also go round in a horse-drawn carriage, swim and try water sports in summer, or simply watch the sun set behind the island, the castle and the swans.

## Bled Island and the pletna boats

The island's church is dedicated to the Mother of God. The tourist board says a church replaced an older pagan temple after Christianisation in 745, and the building took on its present Baroque look in the mid-17th century. That is also when the staircase of 99 stone steps was built. Local tradition says a groom must carry his bride up all 99 if they want to marry in the island church. Visitors ring the "wishing bell" inside.

The traditional way across is the **pletna**, a flat-bottomed wooden boat with a colourful awning. The tourist board traces its origins to 1590. The oarsman, the *pletnar*, rows standing up with two oars, and each boat carries up to 18 passengers. The right to run the boats was granted to local farming families under Maria Theresa, and the trade has passed down through those families ever since. There are 23 pletna boats on the lake, setting off from in front of the Park Hotel, from Mlino and from Velika Zaka. You can also hire a rowing boat and row yourself.

## Triglav National Park

Triglav National Park takes its name from Triglav, at 2,864 metres the highest peak in Slovenia. The park covers 840 square kilometres, about 4 per cent of the country, and almost completely overlaps the Eastern Julian Alps along the border with Italy. It is one of Europe's oldest parks: the first protection dates from 1924, when an Alpine Conservation Park was created in the valley of the Triglav Lakes. The Julian Alps were also included in UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere network in 2003.

The park's code of conduct is clear and worth reading before you set out:

- **No swimming** in high mountain lakes and streams, which are fragile habitats. Dogs aren't allowed to swim in them either.
- **Dogs on a lead** at all times in the natural environment.
- **No drones** without the park's prior consent.
- **No fires**, except in specially designated places.
- **No wild camping.** Tents and overnight stays are allowed only in designated areas, and the park points out there are mountain huts.
- **No driving or parking** off the roads in the natural environment, and **no cycling** except on permitted routes.

The park also has 134 quiet zones, where visits are restricted to protect raised bogs and species such as capercaillie, rock ptarmigan, black grouse and chamois.

## The Soča Valley

On the far side of the mountains, the Soča rises from a karst spring in the Trenta valley. The park describes water collecting beneath the peaks of Jalovec, Šit, Travnik and Mojstrovka and flowing out of a cave with an underground lake. A marked Soča Trail follows the river down towards Bovec. Near the village of Soča, the river has carved its best-known troughs, the Great Troughs. They run for 750 metres and are up to 15 metres deep, with banks sometimes only 2 metres apart. You can view them from a footbridge.

The park warns that walkers in the natural environment may meet falling rocks and trees, slippery ground, sudden weather changes, high water and wild animals. Visitors are responsible for choosing routes that suit their ability and for staying on marked trails.

## Getting over the Vršič Pass

The road over the Vršič Pass links Kranjska Gora with the Trenta and Soča valleys. The Soča Valley tourist board reports that a barrier system and new parking at the top of the pass came into use from 1 September 2026. Drivers who want to park take a ticket at the barrier, and they're let through only while spaces are free. Availability is shown on displays before the barriers. Through traffic uses a separate lane. Parking at the top is free until the end of 2026, so check current arrangements for later dates. For wider planning, see our [Slovenia guide](/countries/slovenia/).

## Before you go

- Walk the 6km lake path at Bled early or late in the day, and take a pletna to the island.
- Read Triglav National Park's code of conduct, and don't swim in mountain lakes or streams.
- Keep dogs on a lead, leave the drone at home unless you have the park's consent, and camp only in designated sites.
- Check weather and trail conditions before any mountain walk, and stay on marked trails.
- If you are driving the Vršič Pass, expect the barrier system at the top and check the latest parking rules.
