Beyond the Llogara Pass the Ionian coast opens up: mountains running down to the water, hidden bays and hillside villages above the sea. Inland, two old towns built in stone, Berat and Gjirokastër, keep the stone houses and castles of their Ottoman-era heyday. Southern Albania rewards a slow loop that combines coast and hill towns, and the main thing to plan around is the road: distances look short, but driving takes care.

## The Riviera: from Llogara to Ksamil

Albania's National Tourism Agency describes the Riviera as running from Vlorë to Sarandë and Ksamil, with the journey beginning at the Llogara Pass (Qafa e Llogarasë). Mountains slope down to the sea, creating beaches, hidden bays and Mediterranean-style villages.

Villages the agency highlights along the coast include Palasa, Dhërmi, Vuno, Himarë, Qeparo, Borsh and Lukovë. Among the beaches and bays it lists:

- **Palasa**, at the entrance to the Riviera near the Llogara Pass.
- **Gjipe Bay**, a hidden bay between cliffs and mountains in the Himarë area.
- **Himarë and Jala**, among the best-known beaches around Himarë.
- **Borsh**, south of Himarë, the longest beach the agency lists.
- **Gramë Bay**, at the edge of Çika Mountain.
- **Ksamil**, near Sarandë, known for its Three Islands.

## Sarandë and Butrint

Sarandë sits at the southern end of the Riviera, facing the Greek island of Corfu across the Ionian Sea. According to the National Tourism Agency, it was known in antiquity as Onhezmi and takes its modern name from the nearby 40 Saints Monastery.

The main cultural draw nearby is Butrint, a national park near Sarandë that the agency describes as part of the UNESCO World Heritage List. Other sights within reach of Sarandë include the Blue Eye (Syri i Kaltër), a natural spring, and the medieval Lëkurësi Castle above the town.

## Berat: the old town on the Osum

Berat lies on both banks of the Osum River, beneath its castle hill. The National Tourism Agency describes it as one of Albania's oldest settlements, known in the Illyrian and Roman periods as Antipatrea, and later an important centre for trade and crafts between the 16th and 18th centuries. Its old town is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and its nickname translates as the "city of one thousand windows".

The old town has three historic quarters, all continuously inhabited for centuries:

- **Kalaja (the castle)**, built on a rocky hill in the 13th century and still lived in. Within its walls are cobbled lanes, houses and a cluster of Byzantine churches, including St Nicholas (built 1591) and the 14th-century St Michael.
- **The Onufri National Iconographic Museum**, in the heart of the castle, holds works by masters of Albanian iconography, including the 16th-century painter Onufri, known for his distinctive red.
- **Mangalem**, a historic quarter of traditional white-fronted houses.
- **Gorica**, across the river, with the churches of St Spyridon and St Thomas.

Down in town, the King's Mosque, the Lead Mosque and the Helveti Tekke reflect what the agency calls centuries of religious coexistence.

## Gjirokastër: the stone city

Gjirokastër is built on the slopes of Mount Gjerë above the Drino valley. The National Tourism Agency explains that it flourished most between the 17th and 19th centuries under the Ottoman Empire, when many of its multi-storey stone tower-houses were built, with stone-slab roofs and small windows reflecting the defensive needs of the time. It is known as the City of Stone because nearly everything, from houses to streets, is built of it, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Highlights the agency lists:

- **The castle**, at the top of the city, one of Albania's largest fortresses, with museums inside and views over the Drino valley.
- **The Old Bazaar**, the traditional heart of the city, with craft workshops and cobbled streets.
- **House-museums** including the Skenduli House and the Zekate House.
- **Ismail Kadare's house**, where the writer grew up.
- **Antigonea**, an archaeological site nearby.

Local dishes to look for include qifqi, oshaf and pasha qofte.

## Driving and staying safe

Most visitors link these places by car or bus. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) is blunt about the risks: driving is often aggressive and road deaths are among the highest in Europe. It also advises:

- Carry a green card as proof of insurance, and get a 1968 international driving permit before you travel, as you cannot buy one abroad.
- After any accident, even a minor one, stay at the scene until police arrive.
- Rural and minor roads often have poor surfaces, and heavy snow can disrupt mountain transport, mainly from December to February.
- At the beach, heed lifeguards and flags, and use only licensed water sports centres; some rented boats and jet skis may lack proper safety equipment.

## Before you go

- Check opening hours and ticket arrangements for Butrint and the castle museums locally, as they are not published in one place.
- Get an international driving permit and green card before you leave if you plan to drive.
- Allow more time than the map suggests for the Llogara Pass and inland roads.
- Wear shoes with grip: the cobbled lanes of Berat and Gjirokastër are steep and polished.
- Dress modestly for churches and mosques.
- Read the latest FCDO advice, including entry requirements, before you travel.
