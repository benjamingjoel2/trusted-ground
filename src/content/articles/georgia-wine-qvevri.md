In Georgia, wine is still made in egg-shaped clay jars buried in the ground, a method UNESCO recognises as living cultural heritage. The eastern region of Kakheti is the country's main winegrowing area, and the autumn harvest, rtveli, is the most rewarding time to visit. Go in September or October for the harvest, hire a driver rather than drive after tastings, and expect to eat as well as you drink.

## The qvevri method

The qvevri is "an egg-shaped earthenware vessel used for making, ageing and storing the wine", according to UNESCO. It added the method to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013.

UNESCO describes the process in a few steps:

1. The grapes are pressed.
2. The juice, "grape skins, stalks and pips" all go into the qvevri together.
3. The qvevri is sealed and buried in the ground.
4. The wine ferments for five to six months before it is drunk.

The method is not just for professional wineries. UNESCO says that "most farmers and city dwellers use this method of making wine". Knowledge is passed down through families and neighbours, who harvest and make wine together. Children learn by watching their elders tend vines, press grapes, collect clay and fire the qvevris. Wine cellars "are still considered the holiest place in the family home".

## How old is Georgian wine?

The Georgian National Tourism Administration (GNTA) calls Georgia "the cradle of wine", with an 8,000-year winemaking tradition. It cites research at Gadachrili Gora in Marneuli Municipality, studied by Georgian and international scientists. That work found evidence of winemaking around 6000 BC. The GNTA adds that grape pips and potsherds have been found at more than 50 sites across the country.

Wine also runs through Georgian music. According to the GNTA, a significant portion of Georgian polyphonic singing is devoted to vines and wine. Some ritual songs belong to particular tasks, such as tending the vineyard, pressing the grapes or opening a qvevri.

## Kakheti: the heart of the vineyards

The GNTA describes Kakheti as "Georgia's premiere winegrowing region". It lies about 1 hour 50 minutes by road from Tbilisi. Fertile soil and abundant sun make winemaking the main business for many farmers there. The GNTA names its most popular grape varieties as Kakhuri Mtsvane, Saperavi, Rkatsiteli and Khikhvi, and it also highlights Kisi.

There is plenty to see between tastings:

- **Monasteries and churches.** The Davitgareji Monastery Complex stretches across 25 kilometres. Other churches include Alaverdi, Ikalto, Shuamta, Bodbe and Nekresi.
- **House-museums.** Options include the palace of King Erekle II in Telavi, museums in Kvareli, and the house-museum of the painter Niko Pirosmanashvili in Mirzaani.
- **Food.** The GNTA singles out mtsvadi, chakapuli and khashlama. For dessert there is churchkhela and tatara, both made from grape juice.
- **Nature.** Lagodekhi Protected Areas are the oldest protected areas in Georgia, and the region also has Vashlovani National Park.

Across Georgia, look for road signs marked "Wine Route". The GNTA says these lead to wineries large and small, including small family cellars that serve local food. You will find them in nearly every region, though not high in the mountains where grapes cannot grow.

## Rtveli: the grape harvest

According to the GNTA, rtveli "starts in September with Rkatsiteli harvest in Kakheti and ends in December with Chkhaveri harvest in Guria and Ajara". Exact timing depends on the sugar in the grapes, so different areas harvest at different times.

Rtveli is a family affair as much as a commercial one. The GNTA describes relatives gathering to pick grapes from early morning. The grapes must be pressed the same day so they do not spoil. Pressing traditionally happens by foot in a satsnakheli, a press made of wood, clay or stone. Many farmers believe foot-pressing gives a better wine because it does not break the stems.

A supra, or feast, follows. Then the new wine, machari, has to be stirred several times a day while it ferments. The GNTA says the most emotional moment comes in spring, when winemakers finally taste the previous year's wine.

## Getting around safely

- **Do not drink and drive.** The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that Georgia "has a lower blood alcohol content limit than the UK" and that punishments for drink-driving can be severe. A driver or organised transport is the simpler choice for a tasting day.
- **Road conditions.** The FCDO notes that roads outside city centres are often badly lit and poor quality. Stray livestock can be a hazard.
- **Taxis.** The FCDO advises not flagging taxis in the street. Book through an app and check the car has working seatbelts.
- **Entry.** At the time of writing (November 2026), the FCDO says British citizens can visit Georgia without a visa for up to one year. Other nationalities should check with Georgian authorities.

## Before you go

- For the harvest, aim for September or October in Kakheti, and confirm timings with your hosts, as they vary year to year.
- Arrange a driver or transport for tasting days rather than driving yourself.
- Ask whether a cellar makes wine in qvevri, and whether you can see where they are buried.
- Leave time for food: a supra is part of the experience.
- If you are bringing wine home, check your own country's customs allowances before you buy.
