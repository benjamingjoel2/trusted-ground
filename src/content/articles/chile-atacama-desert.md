In northern Chile, steam rises from the Tatio geysers in the freezing dawn, flamingos feed on high salt lakes, and the night sky is dark enough that the country has built much of its astronomy around it. Most visitors base themselves in San Pedro de Atacama. The essentials are simple: buy reserve tickets in advance, dress for extreme cold before sunrise, and give your body time at altitude before the highest excursions.

## Why the skies are so dark

Chile's official tourism board, Chile Travel, says the country has "more than 300 clear nights a year". It credits the Atacama's dry air and low light pollution for making stargazing here exceptional. The European Southern Observatory (ESO) notes that all of its observatories are in the northern regions of Antofagasta, Atacama and Coquimbo. A national lighting standard already protected the skies there.

In October 2023, ESO reported that Chile had published a new decree extending those light-pollution controls to the whole country. Public lighting is to favour amber tones and limit blue light, and advertising and sports lighting are subject to time controls.

For visitors, this means:

- **Night tours around San Pedro** are the easiest way to see the sky. Chile Travel points to the area as one where light pollution is minimal.
- **Observatory visits** are possible but need planning. The ALMA radio observatory announced weekend morning visits to its Operations Support Facility, at 2,900 metres, and said that reservations are mandatory. Its antennas stand at 5,000 metres on the Chajnantor plateau. That announcement dates from 2015, so check ALMA's website for the current schedule.
- **Further south**, Chile Travel says ESO's La Silla Observatory offers visits on Saturdays, and names Paranal, home of the Very Large Telescope, among the major sites.

## The Tatio geysers at dawn

According to Chile Travel, the Tatio is the third-largest geothermal field in the world, at more than 4,300 metres above sea level. The drive from San Pedro takes about 1 hour 30 minutes (80 km) on Route B-245, a gravel road. You need to arrive at dawn, between 6:00 and 7:00, when the cold makes the steam columns stand out.

Chile Travel warns that early-morning temperatures can fall as low as -20°C. It says warm layers, gloves and a hat are "mandatory". If you drive yourself, bring water and snacks, and expect temperatures below 0°C.

This is also the highest point on most Atacama itineraries, so it is not a trip for your first morning (see the altitude section below).

## Los Flamencos National Reserve

Many of the classic Atacama sights sit inside the Reserva Nacional Los Flamencos, run by CONAF, Chile's national forest and parks service. CONAF says the reserve was created in 1990, covers 73,986.5 hectares, and is co-managed with Lickanantay Indigenous communities. Visitors should follow those communities' guidance in each sector.

The reserve's sectors include:

- **Valle de la Luna**, which includes the Aldea de Túlor archaeological site
- **Laguna Chaxas** on the Salar de Atacama, run by the Atacameño community of Toconao
- **Lagunas Miscanti and Miñiques**
- the **Salar de Pujsa**, Aguas de Quelana and Tambillo

CONAF notes that three of the world's six flamingo species nest in Chile: the Andean, James's and Chilean flamingos. Vicuñas live in the reserve too.

Each sector handles its own tickets. At the time of writing (November 2026):

- **Valle de la Luna:** buy online at least a day in advance. CONAF gives the general entry price as CLP 10,800, with half price for seniors and students. You need a vehicle, because you cannot enter on foot, and cyclists are admitted only until 11:00.
- **Laguna Chaxas:** tickets are sold through an app named on CONAF's page.
- **Miscanti and Miñiques:** book online, then check in at the village of Socaire. CONAF lists the foreign adult fare as CLP 8,000.
- **Aldea de Túlor:** reserve by email.

CONAF's rules apply across the reserve. There are no fires, pets or drones (unless CONAF authorises them), no feeding or disturbing wildlife, and no removing plants, seeds or rocks. Stay on marked trails, and carry your rubbish out or use the closed bins provided. There is no overnight accommodation, so all visits are by day.

Weather can close trails: sandstorms in winter and rain in summer, plus snow and storms on the high Miscanti route.

## Altitude and health

TravelHealthPro, the UK's national travel health service, says altitude illness "usually occurs at altitudes over 2,500m". Its typical first sign is a headache, often with nausea, dizziness or fatigue. Above 3,000 metres, it advises not raising your sleeping altitude by more than 500 metres a day. It also says to descend if symptoms get worse or become severe.

Day trips to the Tatio and the high lagoons take you well above San Pedro and back the same day. Schedule them after a few days in the area. CONAF specifically warns of exposure to altitude on the Miscanti trails.

The UK FCDO says altitude sickness is "a risk in parts of Chile". Its emergency number for an ambulance is 131. Private clinics and hospitals in major cities are good but expensive, so check your insurance. There is more in our guide to [altitude sickness](/articles/altitude-sickness-travel/). ## Before you go

- Book Valle de la Luna and other reserve tickets in advance, and check opening hours with CONAF.
- Plan the Tatio geysers and high lagoons for later in your stay, not your first day.
- Pack serious cold-weather layers, gloves and a hat for dawn trips.
- Reserve observatory visits directly with the observatory, and check the current schedule.
- Carry water, sun protection and snacks, and take all your rubbish out of the reserve.
- Check that your travel insurance covers medical treatment and evacuation at altitude.
