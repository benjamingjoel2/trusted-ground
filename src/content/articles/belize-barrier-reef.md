The Belize Barrier Reef Reserve System is a World Heritage site made up of seven protected areas strung along Belize's Caribbean coast and out to its offshore atolls. Its best-known sight is the Great Blue Hole, a circular sinkhole on Lighthouse Reef Atoll, usually visited on a long day trip together with the bird colony and reef walls of Half Moon Caye. Both charge park fees, most visitors arrive with a licensed tour or dive boat, and you should check the weather and the UK government's advice before you go.

## A reef of cays, atolls and mangroves

From the air, the reef is a ribbon of white surf with turquoise shallows inside it and deep blue water beyond. The IUCN describes the coastal area of Belize as "the largest barrier reef in the northern hemisphere", together with offshore atolls, several hundred sand cays, mangrove forests, coastal lagoons and estuaries. UNESCO inscribed the reserve system in 1996.

Its seven component areas protect a remarkable range of wildlife. According to the IUCN, the site has recorded more than 500 species of fish and 65 species of stony coral. It also protects the West Indian manatee, the American crocodile and three species of sea turtle, and the jaguars of Bacalar Chico and the ospreys of Glover's Reef are part of the same system.

The reef has had a troubled recent history. UNESCO put the site on its List of World Heritage in Danger in 2009 because of unsustainable coastal development, including the clearing of mangroves. In 2018 it came off the list after Belize banned oil drilling in its marine territory and strengthened mangrove protection. Even so, at the time of writing (November 2026) the IUCN's 2025 assessment still rates the site's conservation outlook as a "significant concern". It names coastal development, tourism growth, overfishing, invasive lionfish and climate change as threats, along with coral disease.

## The Great Blue Hole

The Blue Hole lies near Half Moon Caye on Lighthouse Reef Atoll. The Belize Audubon Society, which co-manages it with the government, describes a circular sinkhole some 1,000 feet (about 300 metres) across and 412 feet (about 125 metres) deep. Its vertical walls and overhanging shelves hold large stalactites. Jacques Cousteau explored it in 1971 aboard the *Calypso*.

What to know before you go:

- **It is mainly a dive site.** There is no land and there are no visitor facilities, only mooring buoys for dive boats. Divers descend to see the stalactites, while snorkellers on the rim see corals, turtles and fish.
- **Sharks are part of the draw.** The Audubon Society lists great hammerheads as occasional visitors, as well as lemon, bull and blacktip sharks. Divers and snorkellers also report green, hawksbill and loggerhead turtles.
- **There is a fee.** At the time of writing, the Audubon Society lists the daily entrance fee for non-nationals as BZ$60, with opening hours of 8am to 4:30pm.

## Half Moon Caye

Most Blue Hole trips also stop at Half Moon Caye, which the Audubon Society describes as the base for managing both sites. It has rangers' quarters, a visitors' centre, picnic areas, toilets and a campground.

The island was first protected as a crown reserve in 1928 for its nesting red-footed boobies and magnificent frigatebirds. It became a Natural Monument in 1982. The 41.5-acre caye supports what the Audubon Society calls possibly the only viable breeding colony of white-phase red-footed boobies in the western Caribbean. Loggerhead, green and hawksbill turtles have been recorded nesting on its south-east beach. Offshore, the Half Moon Caye wall drops to more than 3,000 feet and is one of Belize's most valued dive sites.

The daily fee for non-nationals is listed at BZ$20 at the time of writing. The caye lies about 55 miles (89 km) east of Belize City.

## How trips work

The Audubon Society says the most common way to reach both monuments is with a tour operator. Boats leave from San Pedro, Caye Caulker, Hopkins and Placencia, and from resorts on Turneffe and Lighthouse Reef atolls. The Society does not run guided tours itself, but it can recommend licensed local guides. You can contact its Belize City office by phone or through its website.

You do not have to go as far as the atolls to see the reef. The Belize Tourism Board says there are hundreds of snorkelling sites of varying depths along the barrier reef, and local guides take first-time snorkellers out too. When you book any trip, ask whether park fees are included in the price or paid on the day.

## Safety on and off the water

The FCDO reports injuries and deaths from adventure sports in Belize, including snorkelling and diving. It says the main causes have been inadequate safety precautions and severe weather. It advises checking local forecasts and using only registered and licensed operators.

The hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November. Monitor the National Meteorological Service of Belize and the US National Hurricane Center during those months.

On land, the FCDO warns that tourists are vulnerable to theft in destinations including San Pedro, Caye Caulker and Placencia. Keep valuables out of sight and use ATMs inside banks or businesses. ## Before you go

- Check that your boat operator and guide are registered and licensed, and ask what safety equipment is carried on the long crossing to Lighthouse Reef.
- Ask whether the Blue Hole and Half Moon Caye park fees are included, and confirm current fees with the Belize Audubon Society.
- Be honest about your diving experience. The Blue Hole is a deep dive, so discuss depth limits with the dive leader beforehand.
- Check the weather forecast the day before, and be flexible during the 1 June to 30 November hurricane season.
- Make sure your travel insurance covers scuba diving to the depths you plan to dive.
- Do not touch coral, chase turtles or disturb nesting birds on Half Moon Caye.
