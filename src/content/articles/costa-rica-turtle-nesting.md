On a dark Caribbean beach in north-eastern Costa Rica, a green turtle hauls herself out of the surf to dig a nest, watched by a small group of visitors who stand still and silent behind their guide. Tortuguero National Park is, according to its public-use regulation, one of the most important sites in the world for green turtle nesting. Seeing it is tightly controlled. At night you can only reach the beach on a guided tour, in a small group and without lights or flash.

## Why Tortuguero matters

Costa Rica's regulation for the park, Decree No. 35848, explains why it exists. As well as the green turtle (*Chelonia mydas*), leatherback, loggerhead and hawksbill turtles also come ashore to nest here. The park protects other threatened species too, including the manatee, the jaguar, the American crocodile, the tropical gar and the great curassow.

There are no roads. The Costa Rica Tourism Board (ICT) says the park can only be reached by air or water. Small planes fly to the area. Most people arrive by boat, either from La Pavona (reached by bus from San José via Cariari) or from Caño Blanco near Siquirres. The ICT allows more than five hours for the public-transport route, and the boat trip from La Pavona along the La Suerte River takes about an hour and a half.

## When to go: nesting and hatching

The official dates and the practical advice are slightly different, so it helps to know both:

- **Nesting-watching season:** the park regulation sets the season for watching leatherbacks and green turtles nest as 1 March to 31 October. The Área de Conservación Tortuguero (ACTo), the arm of Costa Rica's national conservation system (SINAC) that runs the park, gives the same dates.
- **Hatching-watching season:** the regulation allows hatchlings to be watched from 1 April to 15 December.
- **Peak months:** the ICT's visitor leaflet says turtles are seen nesting in June and July and hatching from late July to November. It suggests September and October for seeing hatchlings head to the sea.

The regulation also lets ACTo change the observation period if scientific studies show the turtles are being harmed. Any change is published on ACTo's website, so check it before you book.

## How the night tours work

During the nesting season, ACTo says the beach is closed to the public from 18:00 to 06:00. This applies from the Tortuguero river mouth to the park boundary. The only way onto the beach at night is with a guide who holds a turtle-observation licence. The rules are as follows:

- **Two time slots:** tours run from 20:00 to 22:00 and from 22:00 to midnight.
- **Small groups:** a maximum of 11 people, including the guide.
- **Limits per turtle:** no more than 20 people may watch any single turtle.
- **Spotters guide you:** under the regulation, the beach is divided into numbered sectors and each guide is assigned one. Trained turtle trackers tell guides where a turtle is nesting, so groups wait until they are called rather than searching the sand.
- **No light:** the regulation bans using camera or phone flash, or any other light, on Tortuguero beach at night.

The ICT leaflet adds practical points. Wear dark clothing, do not use white light, do not bring cameras of any kind, and never touch the turtles.

## Responsible viewing, day and night

The same regulation sets wider park rules. It bans radios and sound systems, alcohol and drugs, fast sport boats and jet skis, and handling or feeding wild animals. Night walks on the park's trails and waterways are not allowed, except for the official turtle tours on the beach. Stay on public-use routes.

By day, the park's canals and trails are explored by boat and on foot. At the time of writing (November 2026), ACTo lists the entry fee for foreign adults as US$16.95, VAT included. The park is open from 06:00 to 15:00, and tickets are sold until 15:30. ACTo notes that tickets are not sold at the Cuatro Esquinas ranger post: for that entrance you must buy online through SINAC's services portal.

## Safety on the Caribbean coast

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) says safety standards for adventure activities and on small boats are variable, and notes several small-boat incidents in the Tortuguero area. It also warns that rip currents are very common in Costa Rica and there are often no lifeguards, so take local advice before swimming. For jungle visits, it advises going with an experienced local guide.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Does your guide hold a current ACTo turtle-observation licence for Tortuguero?
- Which time slot is the tour, and how many people are in the group?
- What happens if no turtle is found in our sector that night?
- Is the park entry fee included, and which entrance will we use?
- What safety equipment is on the boat, and is there a life jacket for everyone?
