Rows of vines run towards a wall of snow-capped Andes, and the air is dry and bright. Mendoza is the heart of Argentine wine. According to Argentina's national tourism board, the province produces more than 70% of the country's wine, and Malbec is its signature grape. Most visitors split their time between three wine areas within reach of Mendoza city. Many time their trip for the harvest festival, the Vendimia, and some go on to the high mountains around Aconcagua, the highest peak in the Americas.

## How Malbec came to Mendoza

Malbec originally comes from south-west France, around Cahors, where it is known as Côt. A paper published by the Argentine government tells how it reached Argentina. On 17 April 1853, a project was put to Mendoza's provincial legislature to found an agricultural school and experimental farm, the Quinta Normal. The French agronomist Michel Aimé Pouget was put in charge and brought in European vines, including Malbec, Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot. That date is why 17 April is now celebrated as World Malbec Day.

The grape adapted especially well to Mendoza, but its path was not smooth. By 1962 Argentina had 58,577 hectares of Malbec. Over the following decades about 83% of it was pulled up and replaced by higher-yielding varieties for everyday wine. The low point came in 1995, with 9,746 hectares. Demand for quality red wine then brought Malbec back, and by 2018 Argentina had 42,999 hectares, almost 20% of all its vineyards.

## The three main wine areas

Argentina's national tourism board (INPROTUR) describes three main areas around the city. Each has a different altitude and style:

- **Luján de Cuyo.** About 20 km south of the city, at 900 to 1,100 metres. INPROTUR calls it the cradle of Argentine Malbec. It has more than 300 sunny days a year, gravelly, well-drained soils and big swings between day and night temperatures, which give structured wines with dark fruit and spice.
- **Maipú.** About 15 km east of the city and lower, at 650 to 850 metres. It is one of the province's most traditional areas, with century-old wineries. The warmer climate ripens grapes earlier, giving fruitier, rounder wines: Malbec, but also Bonarda and Cabernet Sauvignon.
- **Uco Valley (Valle de Uco).** Between 80 and 130 km south of the city, with vineyards at 1,000 to 1,500 metres. Its stony, calcareous soils and cool nights give fresh, aromatic wines. It is known for innovative, high-end wineries making Malbec, Cabernet Franc and Chardonnay.

INPROTUR's practical advice is to book winery visits in advance and to stick to one area per day to cut down on driving. If you plan to taste, hire transport or stay at or near a winery. Many wineries also run restaurants with tasting menus paired with their wines.

## The harvest and the Vendimia

INPROTUR says Mendoza is good to visit year-round, but October to March is ideal because the vineyards are at their best, with March the harvest month. February to April, during the Vendimia, is high season, when you should book visits well ahead.

The Fiesta Nacional de la Vendimia is the province's great harvest celebration. Its main event is the Acto Central, a stage show at which the harvest queen is crowned. In the days before it, two parades fill the streets of Mendoza city:

- **The Vía Blanca.** A night parade of the harvest queens. According to the Mendoza government, it began by chance in 1940, when a storm forced the cancellation of the main show and the queen was chosen indoors instead. The parade organised afterwards became a permanent part of the festival.
- **The Carrusel.** A daytime procession of floats from each of Mendoza's departments, showing off their traditions, joined by folk groups and community associations. It began at the festival's second edition and moved from Parque General San Martín onto the city streets in 1939.

Dates and routes change every year, so check the provincial government's announcements before you travel.

## Into the high Andes: Aconcagua

West of the city, the road climbs into what the province's tourism body calls the Alta Montaña. This area includes the Potrerillos reservoir, the Uspallata valley, the Cristo Redentor monument, Puente del Inca and Aconcagua itself. INPROTUR gives Aconcagua's height as 6,962 metres and puts its base about 185 km from Mendoza city.

Anyone going beyond the viewpoints into Aconcagua Provincial Park needs a permit from Mendoza's Dirección de Áreas Protegidas. For the 2025–26 season, the provincial government set separate fees for summit climbs and for long and short treks. Fees differ by route (the Horcones or Vacas valleys) and by whether you are from Mendoza, elsewhere in Argentina, Latin America or further afield. Advance permits could only be bought through service companies registered to work in the park. Climbers and long-distance trekkers needed evacuation and medical insurance. Rules and prices are set each season, so check the current ones with the provincial authority.

## Safety and practicalities

The FCDO advises being particularly cautious in downtown Mendoza, as in other Argentine cities. It notes that Mendoza is one of the provinces with a risk of earthquakes. It also warns that you will have to pay for most helicopter rescues, including on Aconcagua, so make sure your insurance covers the activities you plan, including helicopter rescue and medical evacuation. ## Before you go

- Book winery visits in advance, especially between February and April.
- Plan one wine area per day, and arrange a driver or transport if you will be tasting.
- Pack warm layers: evenings in the Uco Valley and the mountains can be cold even after hot days.
- Check the year's Vendimia programme for parade dates and routes.
- If you are trekking or climbing on Aconcagua, confirm the current permit rules and buy your permit through a registered provider.
- Check that your insurance covers high-altitude trekking, helicopter rescue and medical evacuation.
