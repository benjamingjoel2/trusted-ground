An hour's drive from Kingston, the road climbs out of the heat into cool, misty forest where coffee bushes cling to the slopes. Jamaica's Blue and John Crow Mountains National Park is the country's only national park and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It is also home to one of the world's best-known coffees. Most visitors come to walk the trails at Holywell, to climb Blue Mountain Peak before dawn, or to learn how the region's coffee is grown and protected.

## The park and its people

The park is managed by the Jamaica Conservation and Development Trust (JCDT). It covers 41,198 hectares and includes Jamaica's highest point, Blue Mountain Peak, at 2,256 metres (7,401 feet). The JCDT says the park has more than 15 kilometres of hiking trails, hundreds of flowering plants and birds, and countless waterfalls. It is also home to the largest butterfly in the western hemisphere, the giant swallowtail. In 2015 UNESCO inscribed the mountains as a World Heritage Site for both their natural and cultural heritage.

That cultural heritage belongs to the Windward Maroons. These were Africans who refused forced labour on the plantations and defended their freedom from the mountains between the 17th and 19th centuries. The JCDT describes them as the first "keepers of the forest", with their own language, food, laws and way of life. Moore Town, formerly New Nanny Town, is home to the Moore Town Maroons. It is associated with Nanny of the Maroons, Jamaica's only female national hero. The JCDT arranges tours to Maroon communities at Scott's Hall, Moore Town, Charles Town and Bowden Pen.

## Blue Mountain coffee

Coffee is woven into the landscape. The JCDT notes that Irish Town, on the main road up to Holywell, takes its name from Irish settlers, many of whom came to Jamaica as overseers on English-owned coffee plantations. Higher up, the old Land Rovers that carry hikers to the Peak trailhead belong to local people, who also use them to carry coffee and other produce.

The name on the bag is closely guarded. According to the Jamaica Information Service (JIS), the Jamaica Blue Mountain and Jamaica High Mountain coffee brands are owned by the Jamaica Agricultural Commodities Regulatory Authority (JACRA). JACRA's director general told JIS that "every ounce of coffee that leaves our shores must go through a rigorous testing exercise". He also said JACRA cup-tests coffee before it can be distributed or exported.

Japan is the biggest buyer, according to Jamaica's commerce minister quoted by JIS. Jamaica and Japan celebrate Jamaica Blue Mountain Coffee Day every year on 9 January. It marks the first large shipment of coffee beans to Japan on that date in 1967.

If you want to visit a coffee farm or factory, ask what the tour covers before you book. Some only show the processing, while others include a walk through the plantation and a tasting.

## Holywell

Holywell sits more than 900 metres (3,000 feet) above sea level in mist forest. The JCDT calls it the largest recreation area in the park and the closest to the capital, about an hour's drive from New Kingston. It has five walking trails among tropical plants, including Jamaica's national tree, the blue mahoe. The JCDT says these walks suit children and older visitors. There are also lawns for picnics, a visitor centre covering the region's geography, biology and culture, and campsites with water, showers and toilets.

The mountains are also a destination for birdwatchers. According to the JCDT, migrants from the north arrive from October and leave by April. These include warblers, falcons and kingfishers, among some 120 migratory species. They join Jamaica's 29 endemic bird species, and the park is the only place where all of them can be seen.

## Climbing Blue Mountain Peak

The classic way to climb the peak is to start just after midnight and reach the summit for dawn. The JCDT gives these key figures:

- **The trail.** The Blue Mountain Peak Trail officially starts at Lookout, reached via Jacob's Ladder. The JCDT gives the trail as 9.3 km (5.8 miles) over steep terrain, about four hours to the summit.
- **Portland Gap.** This is the last rest stop, with rustic cabins. From there the final ascent is 5.6 km (3.5 miles), taking about two to three hours. Staying overnight at Portland Gap allows a later start, around 3:30am.
- **Day trips.** These are possible if you leave Kingston at about 6am.
- **The landscape.** Above about 2,000 metres the canopy gives way to elfin forest, with stunted, gnarled trees. The park says there are no large or poisonous animals.
- **The fee.** The JCDT's trail page lists a visitor fee of US$20 per person. The page was last updated in 2023, so confirm the current fee. You pay it at the JCDT office, through participating guesthouses or at Portland Gap.

Bring trail shoes, water, snacks, a warm jacket and a raincoat. Transport, accommodation, meals and a trail guide all cost extra.

## Safety and conditions

At the time of writing (November 2026), the FCDO notes that Hurricane Melissa made landfall in Jamaica as a major hurricane in October 2025. It caused significant damage to the western parishes. The Blue Mountains are in the east, but storms can still damage mountain roads and trails. Ask the JCDT whether trails, cabins and access roads are open before you set out.

The FCDO says many rural roads are narrow, winding and poorly lit at night, and a four-wheel-drive vehicle is recommended in some places. It advises caution in and around Kingston and says you should not drive in the city if you do not know it. For more on the island, see our [Jamaica travel guide](/countries/jamaica/). ## Before you go

- Contact the JCDT to check trail conditions, cabin availability and current fees.
- Plan a night ascent with a guide, and carry a head torch, warm layers and rain gear.
- Arrange 4WD transport to the trailhead in advance rather than driving yourself on mountain roads.
- Ask any coffee farm what its tour includes, and whether tastings use Jamaica Blue Mountain coffee.
- Read the FCDO's latest Jamaica advice, including updates on recovery from Hurricane Melissa.
