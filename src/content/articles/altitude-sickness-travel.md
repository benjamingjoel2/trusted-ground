Altitude sickness can affect anyone who goes high too quickly, usually above 2,500 metres. Fitness does not protect you. The best prevention is a slow ascent with rest days. The rule if you get symptoms is simple: don't go any higher, and go down if you get worse.

## Where and why it happens

At altitude, the air still contains 21% oxygen, but the air pressure is lower. TravelHealthPro, the UK's National Travel Health Network and Centre (NaTHNaC), explains that this means each breath takes in fewer oxygen molecules. If you climb gradually, your body usually adjusts.

The NHS says altitude sickness usually occurs above about 2,500 metres. TravelHealthPro lists several high-altitude cities:

- Cusco, Peru (3,399m)
- La Paz, Bolivia (3,630m)
- Quito, Ecuador (2,819m)
- Lhasa, Tibet (3,658m)

Popular treks reach much higher. Everest Base Camp and the Annapurna Circuit in Nepal reach 5,380m, Kilimanjaro reaches 5,895m and the Inca Trail tops out at about 4,200m. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) calls altitude sickness "a serious risk in parts of Peru, including Cusco, Puno, the Colca Canyon and Kuelap".

According to TravelHealthPro's summary of research, roughly 9 to 25% of unacclimatised people going to 2,000 to 3,000m develop acute mountain sickness (AMS). For those going to 3,500 to 4,500m, the figure is 35 to 50%.

## Who is at risk

TravelHealthPro separates risk factors linked to the trip from those linked to the individual.

**Trip factors:**

- how fast you ascend;
- the total gain in altitude;
- the altitude you sleep at.

**Personal factors:**

- having had altitude illness before;
- normally living below 900m;
- exerting yourself on arrival;
- some heart conditions.

It adds that "physical fitness does not appear to protect against altitude illness."

## Recognising the symptoms

The NHS says symptoms usually start 6 to 10 hours after reaching high altitude. They include:

- headache;
- loss of appetite;
- feeling or being sick;
- tiredness;
- dizziness;
- difficulty sleeping.

These are signs of AMS, the mild form of altitude sickness. TravelHealthPro describes two more serious forms:

- **High altitude cerebral oedema (HACE)** is swelling of the brain. It causes confusion, changes in consciousness and loss of coordination, and is often preceded by AMS.
- **High altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE)** affects the lungs. It begins with breathlessness on exertion and a dry cough, and can progress to breathlessness at rest, a wet cough and blood-stained sputum. HAPE can occur without AMS.

The NHS says to get medical help immediately if you or someone else at altitude:

- feels very unwell;
- has problems with balance or coordination;
- sees or hears things that are not there;
- is short of breath even at rest;
- has a cough with frothy or bloody spit;
- has blue or grey skin, lips or nails;
- is very sleepy or hard to wake.

In these cases, go to a lower altitude straight away if you can, around 300 to 1,000 metres lower. TravelHealthPro puts it bluntly: "Symptoms at altitude are caused by altitude illness until proven otherwise." Never leave a person with altitude illness alone.

## Preventing it: ascend slowly

The NHS and TravelHealthPro give consistent advice:

- If you can, spend a few days below 2,500m before going higher.
- Try not to go from below 1,200m to above 3,500m in a single day.
- Above 3,000m, don't increase your sleeping altitude by more than 500m a day, and take a rest day at the same altitude every three to four days.
- Drink enough water and avoid alcohol.
- If you have to fly straight to a high destination, rest for a day before going any higher.

That last point matters in Peru, where many travellers fly directly to Cusco. TravelHealthPro recognises that flying or driving straight to altitude can't always be avoided. In that case it advises building rest days into the rest of the itinerary.

**Medication.** The NHS suggests speaking to a GP or travel clinic if you've had altitude sickness before or will be ascending quickly, as they may prescribe medicine to help prevent it. TravelHealthPro notes that acetazolamide (Diamox) is the preferred drug, but stresses that preventive medicines "are not a substitute for gradual ascent". It says they are unnecessary for low-risk trips. It also says evidence for ginkgo biloba and coca is inconclusive or lacking, and neither is recommended to prevent AMS. Discuss any medication with a health professional before you travel. This is especially important if you are pregnant or have a pre-existing condition.

## If symptoms start

The NHS advises:

- Tell someone you're travelling with.
- Rest at the same altitude until you feel better.
- Take painkillers such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, or anti-sickness medicine, if needed.

Symptoms usually improve within one to three days, and you can then carry on climbing. If they get worse or don't improve after a day, go down 300 to 1,000 metres if you can. Never climb higher to sleep while you still have symptoms.

## Questions to ask your operator

- How fast does the itinerary climb, and what is the highest sleeping altitude each night?
- Are there acclimatisation and rest days built in, especially after flying in to a high city?
- Are guides trained to recognise altitude sickness, and what is the plan for descent or evacuation?
- What oxygen or emergency equipment is carried?
- Does my travel insurance cover the planned maximum altitude, including helicopter evacuation, as TravelHealthPro advises?
