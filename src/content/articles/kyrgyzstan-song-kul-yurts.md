A ring of bare mountains, a wide lake under a huge sky, and on the grassy shore a scatter of white felt yurts with horses grazing beside them. Song-Kul (also written Son-Kol or Son-Kul), in Kyrgyzstan's Naryn region, is where many travellers first see the country's summer pasture life up close. The short answer: Song-Kul is a protected wetland high in the mountains, the yurt camps around it follow the herders' seasonal move to upland pastures, and the conditions are those of high, remote country: cold nights, fast-changing weather and limited rescue. Plan for altitude and weather first, comfort second.

## The lake

The Ramsar Convention, the international treaty on wetlands, lists Son-Kol Lake as a Wetland of International Importance, designated on 23 January 2011. Its summary describes it as the largest high-altitude freshwater lake in central Kyrgyzstan, covering a Ramsar site of 36,869 hectares in Naryn Oblast that includes the Karatal-Japyryk State Nature Reserve.

According to the Ramsar summary:

- The lake is an important stopover for migratory birds, including the black stork, and supports breeding gulls, terns, bar-headed geese and grebes.
- It offers refuge to threatened species such as the critically endangered great bustard and the vulnerable saker falcon.
- It matters to the livelihoods of pastoralists and is a popular camping site for tourists.
- The basin is archaeologically significant for its burial mounds, stone monuments and stone hearths, and local people consider the lake and its basin sacred; many visit to pray.
- The site is jointly managed by the nature reserve and the regional administration.

That combination of wildlife, grazing, heritage and tourism is why your behaviour here matters: keep to existing tracks, leave the stone monuments and burial mounds undisturbed, and take your rubbish away with you.

## How the summer pastures work

Song-Kul's yurt camps grow out of an old system. A case study for the International Partnership for the Satoyama Initiative, hosted by United Nations University, describes the traditional pattern of Kyrgyz transhumance as highlands in summer and lowlands in winter. Livestock are taken up to lush alpine grasslands in summer, above the limit of crop farming, to fatten.

The same study explains:

- Kyrgyz pastures fall into three types by season and distance from the village: spring-autumn, summer and winter pastures. Summer pastures are the furthest from the villages.
- Pastures are state-owned and leased to livestock farmers. A 2009 Law on Pastures made local government responsible for managing all three types.
- Herders keep five kinds of livestock: sheep, goats, cattle, yaks and horses.
- Naryn province has the highest minimum altitude of any Kyrgyz province, at 2,040 metres, and in the highlands the rainy season falls in summer.

So when you stay in a yurt at Song-Kul you are, in effect, a guest on someone's working summer pasture. Expect animals around camp, early mornings and a household that is busy with more than tourism.

## The yurt

UNESCO has inscribed the traditional knowledge and skills of making Kyrgyz, Kazakh and Karakalpak yurts on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. It describes the yurt as a nomadic dwelling with a collapsible wooden circular frame covered with felt and bound with ropes, which can be put up and taken down quickly and is made from natural, renewable materials.

According to UNESCO, men and their apprentices make the wooden frame and its fittings by hand, while women make the interior decorations and outer coverings, ornamented with organic or geometric patterns, using weaving, felting, embroidery and other traditional techniques. Festivities, ceremonies, weddings and funerals are held in yurts, which are passed from parents to children as a family relic. Treat the inside of a family yurt as you would a home.

## Altitude, weather and horses

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) is direct about the mountains. It says trekking in Kyrgyzstan often involves very remote areas, with a high risk of avalanches, landslides and rockfalls, and that weather can change very quickly. At higher altitudes snow is possible even in summer, along with sunstroke, serious sunburn and altitude sickness. Heavy snowfall can block roads and trekking routes, particularly in autumn and winter.

The FCDO advises getting insurance that covers activity at high altitude, using a reputable trekking agency and not trekking alone. It notes that mountain rescue is a basic, volunteer-staffed service with limited equipment, and gives its 24-hour emergency number as +996 312 651 404. Our guides to [altitude sickness](/articles/altitude-sickness-travel/) and [adventure travel insurance](/articles/adventure-travel-insurance/) go into more detail.

If you plan to ride to or around Song-Kul, note that we could not find an official source setting standards for horse trekking in Kyrgyzstan, so the checks are up to you: ask about the guide's experience and the horses' condition, request a helmet, say honestly how much you have ridden, and confirm your insurance covers riding at altitude.

## Before you go

- According to the FCDO at the time of writing (November 2026), UK visitors can stay in Kyrgyzstan without a visa for up to 30 calendar days in each 60-day period; longer stays need registration or a visa. Check the rules for your passport.
- Pack for cold nights and summer snow as well as strong sun.
- Save the mountain rescue number and tell someone your route and return date.
- Ask your camp what water, power and toilet facilities there are, and bring what you need.
- Check that your insurance names high-altitude trekking and horse riding.
