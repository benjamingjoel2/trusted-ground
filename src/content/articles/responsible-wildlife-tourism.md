A lion seen from a vehicle at a respectful distance, or an elephant crossing a track at dusk, is what most travellers come to East Africa for. The encounters that cause harm tend to look different: a cub passed round for photos, an animal that can be ridden, a souvenir carved from horn or shell. Official guidance is consistent on where the line falls. Watch wild animals, do not handle them, and do not buy them.

## What travel-industry guidance calls unacceptable

ABTA, the UK travel trade association, publishes animal welfare guidance for its members and advice for travellers. It gives holding a lion cub and riding or bathing elephants as examples of unacceptable practices, and says that touching, riding or feeding dangerous wild animals poses significant risks.

Its advice to travellers includes:

- Never feed, touch, tease or provoke wild animals.
- Be wary of having a photo taken with a captive wild animal.
- Sanctuaries should not breed animals, and you should not be allowed to touch them.
- Do not encourage guides to pursue wildlife that is showing avoidance behaviour.
- Do not approach or interfere with breeding sites.
- Never ride donkeys, horses, mules or camels that are too young, too old, pregnant or nursing.
- Do not buy souvenirs made from wildlife products or other threatened natural materials.

If something worries you, ABTA suggests raising it with the attraction or your travel company. If a tour operator declines to sell an animal experience on welfare grounds, it says it can help to ask why.

## On safari: park rules come first

In Kenya, the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) advises visitors to game reserves and national parks to always follow park regulations and advice from wardens, to arrive in daylight and not to buy safari tours from touts. It warns that viewing wildlife on foot or at close range carries risks, and that swimming in rivers and lakes is illegal in national parks. On hikes, do not approach wild animals.

Park regulations vary by park and reserve and are set by the authority that manages each one, so read the rules you are given at the gate. Good conduct on a game drive is mostly about restraint: staying in the vehicle unless you are told otherwise, keeping noise down, and accepting that some sightings end when the animal moves away. Pushing a driver to get closer is exactly the "pursuit" ABTA asks travellers not to encourage.

## Hands off: the health reason

There is a medical reason to avoid touching animals too. TravelHealthPro, the UK's National Travel Health Network and Centre, says rabies is considered a risk in Tanzania and advises travellers to avoid contact with all animals. Rabies is usually transmitted by a bite, scratch or lick to an open wound, and once symptoms develop it is almost always fatal. After any possible exposure, wash the wound thoroughly and get urgent local medical assessment, even if the wound looks trivial. Children are at higher risk because they are less likely to avoid animals or report a bite.

## Souvenirs: what the law says

Buying wildlife products can break the law at both ends of the trip.

- **Kenya:** the FCDO says it is illegal to buy, sell, kill or capture any protected wild animal, or trade any of its parts, without a licence. Buying or trafficking banned goods can lead to a fine or prison.
- **Tanzania:** the FCDO says border officials have arrested foreigners trying to take wildlife items, including horns and seashells, out of the country. Exporting them requires a certified permit from the Ministry of Natural Resources and Tourism. It also warns against bringing animal-skin accessories into Tanzania, as you risk questioning or detention when you leave.
- **Back in Great Britain:** GOV.UK explains that moving specimens of species listed under CITES, the international convention on trade in endangered species, generally needs a permit. A specimen includes parts of animals and items made from them. Limited exemptions exist for some personal effects, but they do not apply to live animals or to items under stricter controls, such as rhino horn. Without a valid permit you could face up to seven years in prison, an unlimited fine, or both.

The simplest rule is not to buy anything made from an animal, shell or coral unless you are certain it is legal to export and import.

## The UK law on selling activities abroad

The Animals (Low-Welfare Activities Abroad) Act 2023 makes it an offence, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, to sell or offer to sell the right to observe or take part in certain animal activities that take place outside the UK. The offence applies only to activities described in regulations made under the Act. Check legislation.gov.uk for whether any have been made before relying on the Act as a filter.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Does any activity on this trip involve touching, riding, feeding or being photographed with an animal?
- What are the park rules on viewing distances and leaving the vehicle, and how do your guides apply them?
- What do your guides do when an animal shows signs of avoiding the vehicle?
- If the itinerary includes a sanctuary or orphanage, does it breed animals or allow handling?
- Will any shop or market stop on the trip sell wildlife products, shells or coral?
- If you have declined to sell certain animal experiences, which ones and why?
