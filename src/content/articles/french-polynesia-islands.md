Most visitors to French Polynesia stay in the Society Islands: Tahiti, Moorea and Bora Bora. Further afield are the Tuamotu atolls for diving, the Marquesas for mountains and culture, and the remote Austral and Gambier Islands. Island-hopping depends on domestic flights and ferries, so how far you can travel shapes your trip as much as which islands you choose.

## The Society Islands: Tahiti and Moorea

The Society Islands are split into the Windward and Leeward Islands. Tahiti Tourisme says the trade winds that give the groups their names keep temperatures pleasant all year. The archipelago has eight islands and the largest choice of places to stay and things to do in French Polynesia. Its people have kept up an ancestral culture of legends, sacred sites and marae, the stone platforms where ancient Polynesians held ceremonies.

**Tahiti**, the largest island in French Polynesia, is where most trips begin. International flights land at Faa'a International Airport, and domestic flights with Air Tahiti and Air Moana leave from there too. You can get around the island by taxi, bus, hire car or scooter. Tahiti Tourisme recommends arranging airport transfers with your accommodation or travel agent when you book.

**Moorea** is the easiest island to add to a stay on Tahiti. According to Tahiti Tourisme, ferries to Moorea leave the terminal in Papeete several times a day, and there are also ferries from Tahiti to the other Society Islands.

## Bora Bora and the Leeward Islands

**Bora Bora** is about 50 minutes by air from Tahiti. It is famous for its turquoise lagoon and the small palm-covered islets called motu. Matira Beach lies along the shore, with Mount Otemanu rising behind. Tahiti Tourisme lists boat tours, snorkelling over coral gardens, and kayaking or paddleboarding across the lagoon. Many hotels lend kayaks, paddleboards and snorkelling gear for free.

Bora Bora has a history beyond the resorts. There are ancient marae, sacred sites once used for religious rituals, and remains from the Second World War American presence known as Operation Bobcat.

In the south-east of **Raiatea**, the Taputapuātea marae was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2017. Tahiti Tourisme describes it as "the birthplace of Polynesian culture" and one of the most important ceremonial complexes in the Polynesian Triangle. Chiefs from different islands once gathered there for religious ceremonies and to make political decisions.

## The Tuamotus: atolls and diving

The Tuamotu Islands are 76 atolls spread across a vast area of ocean. The atolls are low coral rings around turquoise lagoons. Tahiti Tourisme says Air Tahiti and Air Moana fly there from Papeete in about an hour and a half, and suggests touring the atolls by bike. The real draw is underwater, with sharks, turtles, manta rays, dolphins and coral reefs. On Rangiroa there are churches built of coral and a vineyard growing grapes on a motu in the lagoon. Fakarava and the neighbouring atolls of Aratika, Kauehi, Niau, Raraka, Taiaro and Toau form a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, designated in 2006.

If you dive, the UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) warns that diving schools may have limited resources and don't always meet safety and maintenance standards. It advises asking about safety precautions before you dive, including whether the school can get you to the only hyperbaric chamber in French Polynesia, which is in Papeete.

## The Marquesas, Australs and Gambiers

**The Marquesas**, north-east of Tahiti, were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2024. Tahiti Tourisme describes jagged ridges, high peaks and cliffs rising steeply from the ocean. The islands are known for wood carving and tattooing, and their traditions are celebrated at the Marquesas Festival, the Matava'a o te Henua Enana.

**The Austral Islands** lie south-west of Tahiti. Of the seven islands, five are inhabited, and Air Tahiti flies to four of them. Tahiti Tourisme says humpback whales come to breed and raise their calves from August to October, especially around Rurutu and Tubuai. On Tubuai, Raivavae and Rimatara there are pre-European archaeological sites, and the islands are known for weaving dried pandanus leaves into hats, baskets and mats.

**The Gambier Islands** are the most remote group, more than 1,600 km from Tahiti. Their main island, Mangareva, is known for pearl farming and its missionary-era churches. The best known is St Michael's Cathedral in Rikitea, built from blocks of coral in 1848. Tahiti Tourisme says Air Tahiti flies there twice a week. The flight takes 3 hours 40 minutes and lands on a motu, 40 minutes by boat from Rikitea. A cargo ship that carries passengers also calls once a month.

## Entry rules and the cyclone season

According to the FCDO, at the time of writing (November 2026), British citizens can visit French Polynesia without a visa for up to 90 days for tourism or business. Your passport must be valid for at least three months after the date you plan to leave. French Polynesia is an overseas collectivity of France and sets its own entry rules. Travellers of other nationalities should check with the French authorities.

The FCDO warns that tropical storms, including cyclones, can happen from November to April, and that earthquakes are also a risk. If you plan to drive, British licence holders need a 1968 International Driving Permit as well as their UK licence. You cannot buy one outside the UK.

## Before you go

- Check the entry requirements for your nationality, and make sure your passport is valid for at least three months beyond your departure date.
- Plan your route around flight schedules. Remote groups such as the Gambiers have only a few flights a week.
- Arrange airport transfers with your accommodation when you book.
- If you travel between November and April, follow local weather news and check that your insurance covers cyclone disruption.
- If you dive, check your insurance conditions and ask about emergency transfer to the hyperbaric chamber in Papeete.
