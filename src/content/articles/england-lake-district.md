Mist lifting off Wastwater, sheep scattered across a green fellside, a lake steamer nosing into a stone jetty: the Lake District packs a remarkable amount of landscape into a small corner of northwest England. It is England's largest national park and, since 2017, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The keys to a good visit are simple: respect the weather on the fells, leave the car behind where you can, and follow the park's rules on camping and the water.

## The park in numbers

According to the Lake District National Park Authority, the park covers 2,362 square kilometres, roughly 58km wide and 64km long. It has 16 bodies of water and more than 350 fells. UNESCO designated it as a World Heritage Site in 2017, recognising it as a "cultural landscape".

A few superlatives from the park authority:

- **Scafell Pike**, at 978 metres, is England's highest mountain. Scafell (964m), Helvellyn (950m) and Skiddaw (931m) follow.
- **Windermere** is England's longest lake, at about 17km, and the largest in the park by area.
- **Wastwater** is England's deepest lake, at 74 metres.
- **Bassenthwaite Lake** is the only body of water officially called a "lake"; the rest are meres and waters.

The park is busy. The authority, citing Cumbria Tourism, reports 17.45 million visitors in 2025.

## Walking the fells safely

The park authority says there are more than 200 fell tops, and Alfred Wainwright wrote about 214 of them. Routes range from lakeside paths to serious mountain days, and the authority's Miles Without Stiles routes are graded for accessibility.

Weather is the main hazard. The park authority stresses that checking the latest forecast is essential, because conditions can change within a single day. Its advice:

- Use the Met Office Lake District mountain forecast for fell-top conditions, warnings and hazards.
- In winter (December to April), check Weatherline, the park's daily report on conditions on top of Helvellyn, compiled by its Fell Top Assessors.
- "Pack for the weather you'll get, not the weather you want."
- Know when darkness falls, and remember deep valleys can turn dark before sunset.
- Carry a paper map as a backup: phones lose signal and battery.

If you want to walk in snow and ice, the park lists winter skills day courses.

## Getting around without a car

Traffic and parking are real pressures, and the park authority asks visitors to leave the car at home where they can. It notes that Wasdale gets very busy with limited parking, and that parking in the Buttermere Valley can be difficult.

The alternatives are good:

- **Rail:** Oxenholme, Penrith and Carlisle are on the West Coast Main Line from London and Glasgow. A branch line runs to Windermere, and there is a direct Northern train from Manchester.
- **Buses:** At the time of writing (November 2026), the park authority says bus fares are capped at £3 per single journey. Core routes include the 555 through the heart of the park and the X4/5 from Penrith to Keswick.
- **Boats:** Cruises and launches run on Windermere, Ullswater, Coniston and Derwentwater, and day tickets combine bus and boat on several lakes. A car and passenger ferry crosses Windermere towards Hawkshead.
- **Seasonal shuttles** serve Wasdale and Buttermere in summer.

## Wild camping: the rules

Wild camping is a frequent source of confusion. The park authority says it is "technically not permitted" without the landowner's permission, and camping in car parks or on roadside verges is not allowed at any time. It is, however, normally tolerated if you:

- camp above the highest fell wall, well away from towns, villages and lake shores
- arrive late, leave early and stay only one night
- keep to one or two tents
- light no fires at all
- carry out all litter, and go to the toilet at least 30 metres from water, burying waste

The park's #LakeDistrictKind message also warns that wildfires and barbecues can destroy landscapes and harm wildlife.

## On the water

Swimming, paddling and boating are part of the appeal, but the lakes are cold. The park authority's Swim Safe Code includes avoiding cold water shock, and its website lists where you can swim safely. Any powered boat on Windermere, including one with an outboard or electric motor, must have a current Windermere registration. The park also asks visitors to help stop invasive non-native species spreading between rivers, tarns and lakes; its website explains how to avoid carrying them on your kit.

## Before you go

- Check the Met Office mountain forecast, and Weatherline in winter, on the morning of your walk.
- Pack waterproofs, warm layers, a paper map and a charged phone.
- Plan to arrive by train or bus, especially for Wasdale and Buttermere.
- If wild camping, follow the park's conditions and leave no trace.
- Register any powered boat before using it on Windermere.
