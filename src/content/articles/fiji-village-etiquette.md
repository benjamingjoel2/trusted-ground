Fijian villages are private, communal places, and visitors are expected to ask permission before entering. In practice, that means presenting a sevusevu, a gift of yaqona (kava) root, to the village chief. You should also dress modestly, take off your hat and shoes where it's expected, and follow your hosts' lead.

## Why village protocol matters

Village life in Fiji is built on a strong traditional structure. According to the Pacific Community (SPC), the regional intergovernmental organisation, in its guide *Cultural Etiquette in the Pacific*:

- Fiji is divided into 14 provinces, each aligned to one of three traditional confederacies: Kubuna, Burebasaga and Tovata.
- Provinces are made up of districts and officially recognised villages, which are formed of clans with distinct traditional roles.
- Each confederacy, village and clan has its own head. These traditional leaders "retain significant power and importance".

The SPC guide is blunt about the starting point: "Fijian villages are private property, so do not enter one unless you have proper permission." For most travellers, that permission comes through a guide, a host or an organised village visit, rather than by simply walking in.

## The sevusevu

The SPC guide says it is customary in rural areas for visitors to call on the village chief when they arrive and present a sevusevu, "seeking permission and approval". The sevusevu is a gift of dried or powdered yaqona root. The guide notes that presenting the whole plant is the preferred protocol, but powdered yaqona is an acceptable substitute today.

Welcomes vary. A simple one may just be a visit to the chief, where you hand over the sevusevu and explain why you have come. A more formal welcome includes a full yaqona ceremony. In that case a matanivanua, or orator, accompanies you, announces your arrival to the chief and performs the presentation on your behalf.

The SPC guide describes a few gestures that go with the welcome:

- Shake the chief's hand using both of your hands.
- After shaking hands, sit down and *cobo*: clap with cupped hands at least three times.
- When you leave, it is courteous to visit the chief again, thank the village for its hospitality and present another gift of yaqona, known as the *itatau*.

The guide also suggests asking, during the welcome, whether the village has any special protocols. Customs vary from place to place.

## The yaqona ceremony

The SPC describes yaqona as "central to Fijian culture", served ritually on important occasions and today also drunk socially among family and friends. If you are invited to join, keep these points from the SPC guide in mind:

- Seating follows seniority and rank, so sit where you are asked.
- Do not sit in front of the *tanoa*, the yaqona bowl, unless you are invited to.
- Fijian protocol is "learned by observation", so follow your guide and copy the people around you.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) adds a health note. If you take part in a kava ceremony, be aware of the potential risk of liver toxicity.

## Dress and behaviour

**Dress.** The FCDO says visitors should cover their shoulders and knees in rural villages and during kava ceremonies. It also notes that topless bathing and public nudity are illegal in Fiji. The SPC guide adds that women wearing trousers should cover them with a *sulu*, the Fijian wrap, when in a village.

**Hats.** Take off your hat while walking through a village. The SPC guide explains that only the chief is permitted to wear one.

**Inside a home.** According to the SPC:

- Take off your shoes before entering a *bure* (traditional house), and stoop as you move around inside.
- Men sit cross-legged and women with their legs to the side. Sitting with your legs stretched out is unacceptable.
- Never walk in front of someone seated on the floor. Pass behind them and say "tilou" (excuse me).
- Never touch anyone's head, which is considered the most respected part of the body.
- Don't sit or stand in doorways.
- Hold out gifts with both hands.

**Meals.** An invitation to eat is often simply hospitality. The SPC guide suggests politely declining the first offer, but not refusing if your host insists. It is considered rude to eat while standing or walking around the house.

**Sensitivities.** The FCDO notes that homosexuality was decriminalised in Fiji in 2010. However, there can be local sensitivities, especially in rural communities.

## Useful words

The SPC guide lists a few Fijian words worth learning before a visit:

- **Bula:** hello
- **Vinaka (vakalevu):** thank you (very much)
- **Moce:** goodbye
- **Tilou:** excuse me, said when passing someone or briefly standing above them

## Questions to ask your guide or host

- Will you present the sevusevu for us, or should we buy yaqona ourselves, and if so, where?
- Does this village have particular protocols we should know about, such as seating or where we can walk?
- What should we wear? Should we bring a sulu, or can we borrow one?
- Is photography welcome, and when should we ask first?
- Is a contribution to the village expected, and how is it handled?
