On Ghana's Central Region coast, two whitewashed fortresses look out over the Atlantic, not far apart. Cape Coast Castle and St George's Castle at Elmina are beautiful buildings with a terrible history: for centuries they were trading posts for gold and then holding places for enslaved Africans before they were shipped across the ocean. Today both are museums run by the Ghana Museums and Monuments Board, and a guided tour of their dungeons is one of the most powerful experiences in West Africa.

## Forts, castles and a World Heritage listing

The Gold Coast was lined with European trading posts. The Ghana Museums and Monuments Board (GMMB), the legal custodian of Ghana's material cultural heritage, describes them as forts and castles built and occupied by the Portuguese, Dutch, Danes, Swedes, French, British and Brandenburg-Prussians between the fifteenth and twentieth centuries.

According to the Zamani Project at the University of Cape Town, which documented Elmina Castle in 2006, the castle is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List together with 17 other fortified trading posts in Ghana, under the listing "Forts and Castles, Volta, Greater Accra, Central and Western Regions". These posts first protected ports in the gold and ivory trade, and later became centres of the transatlantic slave trade.

## St George's Castle, Elmina

St George's Castle, usually called Elmina Castle, is the older of the two. The Ghana Tourism Authority (GTA) says it was founded by the Portuguese in 1482 and describes it as the first major European construction in tropical Africa. The Portuguese called it São Jorge da Mina, after their patron saint, and it gave them a trade monopoly with unrivalled access to the region's gold.

The castle changed hands several times:

- **1637:** the Dutch ousted the Portuguese. Their main trades were gold and enslaved people, and they reconstructed the castle between 1770 and 1775.
- **6 April 1872:** the castle was ceded to the British.
- **Later uses:** it served as a police recruit training centre and a secondary school before becoming a museum.

The GTA says that as slaves replaced gold as the main commodity, storerooms were converted into dungeons. The tour, which is included in the entry fee, takes you through the dungeons, punishment cells and the Door of No Return, and to the room where the British imprisoned the Asante king Prempeh I. From the battlements you can see the fishing harbour and Fort St Jago (Fort Coenraadsburg) across the lagoon. The St George's Castle Museum inside, established in 1996, has displays on the Asante of Elmina, local trade with Europeans, gold weights, shackles, textiles and beads.

## Cape Coast Castle

Cape Coast Castle is, according to the GTA, the largest of the buildings that hold the legacy of the transatlantic slave trade. The town's name comes from the Portuguese "Cabo Corso" (short cape). A permanent fort was built here by the Swedes in 1653, and it then passed between the Danes, the local Fetu chief, the Dutch and finally the British, who captured it in the 1660s. The GTA says that by 1700 the British had upgraded it into a castle and, after French bombardment during the Seven Years' War, rebuilt it entirely after 1760.

The castle was the seat of the British administration of the Gold Coast until 19 March 1877, when it moved to Christiansborg Castle in Accra. It also housed the first formal schools in the country, known as Castle Schools.

The guided tour covers the slave dungeons, Dalzel Tower, and the cannons and mortars of the castle's defences. Inside is the West African Historical Museum, with ceremonial drums, muskets, shackles from the slave trade and pottery. The castle is also the regional headquarters of the GMMB.

The GMMB says the museum here covers the transatlantic slave trade from beginning to end: how people were captured and held in dungeons, sold in markets and halls of trade, forced through the "doors of no return" and onto the Middle Passage, and the life of the African diaspora. It also does not gloss over the African practices and institutions that supported the trade.

## Visiting respectfully

These are places of memory and grief. A few points of etiquette help:

- **Take the guided tour.** Much of the meaning lies in the explanations given inside the dungeons.
- **Allow time.** The tours are emotionally heavy, and you may want space afterwards.
- **Photograph with care.** Be mindful of others, especially in the dungeons.
- **Be prepared.** Some exhibits, including shackles, branding sticks and images of captives, are deliberately confronting.

## Practicalities

The GTA lists opening hours of 9am to 4:30pm daily for both castles. Entry fees are charged, and the Elmina tour is included in the fee; check current prices on arrival, as we could not confirm them from an official source.

The castles sit on the coast, but the beaches are not for swimming. The FCDO warns that swimming is dangerous along Ghana's southern coast because of rip tides and undertows, with occasional deaths of adults and children. It also says theft is the main problem on beaches, and advises against visiting beaches alone.

## Before you go

- Plan to visit both castles: they are close together and tell connected stories.
- Arrive well before the 4:30pm closing time to fit in a full guided tour.
- Budget for entry fees and confirm current prices locally.
- Do not swim off the southern coast; rip tides and undertows are dangerous.
