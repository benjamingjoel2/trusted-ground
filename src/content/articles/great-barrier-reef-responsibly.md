The Great Barrier Reef is a protected marine park, not open water. It is divided into zones that set what you may do where, commercial reef trips run under permissions granted under Australian federal law, and most visitors on those trips pay a small environmental management charge collected by the operator. In tropical Queensland, the November to May marine stinger season also shapes how and where you swim.

## How the marine park is managed

The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park is established under the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Act 1975, a Commonwealth law. The Act sets up the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority, provides for zoning plans that divide the park into zones, and makes it an offence to break the conditions of a permission granted under it.

For travellers, the practical effect is that the rules depend on where your boat goes, and that the company running your trip operates under conditions set by the Authority. Before you travel, check the current zoning maps and visitor rules on the Authority's website, gbrmpa.gov.au.

## Marine park zoning: what the colours mean

Zoning maps use colours that tell you what each area allows. The Queensland Government describes the four main zone types used in marine park zoning:

- **General Use (light blue):** allows a range of activities, including trawling.
- **Habitat Protection (dark blue):** protects sensitive habitats; no trawling.
- **Conservation Park (yellow):** fishing and crabbing allowed only to a limited extent.
- **Marine National Park (green):** areas of high conservation value, described as "look but no take".

Green zones matter most to visitors. Snorkelling and diving are about looking, and that is what these areas are for. Taking anything, from fish to shells, is a different matter. If your trip includes fishing, ask the operator which zones you will be in and what is allowed there. Do not assume a quiet bay or island beach is open to fishing or collecting.

## Permitted operators

Under the Act, many commercial activities in the marine park run under a permission granted under its regulations. Tourism permissions carry conditions, and the Act makes breaching those conditions an offence. An operator taking you to the reef should be able to tell you which permission it holds and what it allows: the areas it may visit, for example, and the activities it may offer there.

Asking about the permit is a reasonable check, and a straight answer is a good sign. It is also worth reading our guides to operator licences and to red flags when booking.

## The environmental management charge

The environmental management charge (EMC) is a charge set under the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Act and its regulations. The Act provides that:

- the amount of the charge is worked out under the regulations;
- where a visitor is liable, the charge is payable to the holder of the permission, in other words your tour operator;
- the operator must collect the charge from visitors and pass the money to the Authority on time, and failing to collect it is an offence.

The rate is set in regulations and can change, so we have not quoted a figure here. Check the current amount on the Authority's website, and ask your operator whether it is included in the price you have been quoted.

## Stinger season and other water safety

The Queensland Government says marine stingers are present in tropical Queensland waters all year, but the risk is higher during the marine stinger season, from November to May. The dangerous species named include the box jellyfish and the Irukandji. Its advice is to:

- swim inside stinger nets where they are provided;
- swim at patrolled beaches between the red-and-yellow flags and obey safety signs;
- stay out of the water when beaches are closed;
- consider wearing a full-body lycra suit to protect against stings;
- not touch stingers washed up on the beach, as they can still sting.

If someone is stung, call Triple Zero (000) and ask for an ambulance, pour vinegar on the sting, and give oxygen or CPR if needed while waiting. Irukandji symptoms can take 20 to 40 minutes to develop, so the Queensland Government advises seeking medical help if you are unsure.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) adds two points. You are required by law to complete a medical declaration for diving or snorkelling at a resort. And tropical cyclones mainly affect Queensland, the Northern Territory and Western Australia, with a season that normally runs from November to April. That overlaps with stinger season, so plan for the possibility of cancelled boat days and choose flexible bookings.

## Questions to ask your operator

- Which marine park permission do you hold, and which reef sites does it allow you to visit?
- Is the environmental management charge included in the price, and how much is it at the moment?
- Which zones will we be in, and if fishing is offered, where is it allowed?
- Do you supply full-body lycra suits during stinger season, and do you carry vinegar and first-aid equipment on board?
- What medical declaration will I need to complete before snorkelling or diving, and who supervises people in the water?
- If a cyclone or bad weather cancels the trip, do I get a refund or a new date?
