A bowl of noodles slurped at a counter, a covered arcade of pickle sellers and tea merchants, a salmon hot pot in Hokkaido: food is one of the best reasons to travel in Japan, and it changes noticeably from region to region. Eat the local speciality wherever you are, give the big markets a morning, and learn a handful of table manners. The most important are to say "itadakimasu" before you eat and never to stand your chopsticks upright in rice.

## Washoku: a food culture on UNESCO's list

In 2013 UNESCO added washoku, the traditional dietary culture of Japan, to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. UNESCO describes it as "a social practice based on a set of skills, knowledge, practice and traditions" covering how food is produced, prepared and eaten. It links washoku to respect for nature and the sustainable use of local resources. Its traditions are most visible at New Year, when families prepare rice cakes and symbolic dishes.

That emphasis on local, seasonal ingredients is why regional cooking matters so much.

## Regional dishes to look for

The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) lists specialities across the country. A selection, north to south:

- **Hokkaido:** ishikari-nabe, a salmon hot pot with vegetables, tofu and konnyaku; and "Genghis Khan", barbecued lamb.
- **Tohoku:** sasakamaboko, a steamed fish paste from Sendai; and wanko soba from Iwate, served in mouthful-sized portions in small bowls.
- **Kanto:** monja-yaki, a pan-fried batter dish, and fukagawa meshi, rice topped with clams, both from Tokyo.
- **Chubu and Tokai:** houtou, a noodle dish similar to udon; and misokatsu in Aichi, a deep-fried pork cutlet with a miso-based sauce.
- **Hokuriku:** hotaruika (firefly squid) in Toyama, and jibu-ni, a wild duck and vegetable soup, in Kanazawa.
- **Kansai:** yudofu (boiled tofu) in Kyoto, and takoyaki, octopus dumplings, in Osaka.
- **Chugoku:** oysters and okonomiyaki, a savoury pancake, in Hiroshima; and fugu (puffer fish) in Yamaguchi.
- **Shikoku:** bonito, and sanuki udon from Kagawa.
- **Kyushu:** mizutaki, a boiled chicken dish, in Fukuoka.
- **Okinawa:** goya champuru, bitter melon stir-fried with egg, tofu, vegetables and pork.

## Noodles by region

Noodles are a good way to taste regional difference. JNTO explains that Kanto-style udon broth uses bonito dashi and strong soy sauce, while Kansai-style uses kombu dashi and mild soy sauce. It names Sanuki udon (Kagawa), Inaniwa udon (Akita) and Gotou udon (Nagasaki) among the best known. For soba, usually made from buckwheat, JNTO highlights Togakushi soba (Nagano), Izumo soba (Shimane) and wanko soba (Iwate).

## Markets worth a morning

**Nishiki Market, Kyoto.** JNTO describes a narrow passageway about 400 metres long and 3.9 metres wide, with more than 100 vendors. It began as an open-air fish market some 400 years ago. Look for yuba (the film that forms on heated soy milk), mackerel sushi, dashimaki tamago (rolled omelette), Kyoto pickles and Uji green tea. Shops don't open especially early, and JNTO suggests going around late morning, as the market can get very crowded. There are noodle shops and other places to eat inside.

**Tsukiji Outer Market, Tokyo.** The wholesale inner market, known for its tuna auctions, moved to nearby Toyosu in October 2018. The outer market is still open, with seafood and produce sellers, kitchen equipment suppliers and restaurants. JNTO gives the nearest stations as Tsukiji-Shijo on the Toei Oedo Line and Tsukiji on the Hibiya Line.

## Table manners

JNTO's etiquette guidance is practical rather than fussy. The essentials:

- **Before and after:** say "itadakimasu" before eating and "gochisosama desu" afterwards, roughly "thanks for the food".
- **Wait for everyone:** in a group, people normally don't start until everyone has been served.
- **Finish your food:** leaving leftovers is frowned upon.
- **Slurp your noodles:** this is accepted.
- **Lift your bowl:** bring dishes up to your mouth rather than leaning down to them.
- **Sushi:** dip the fish, not the rice, in soy sauce.
- **Elbows** stay off the table, and take food from shared platters onto your own plate first.

Chopsticks have their own rules. The two biggest faux pas, JNTO says, are sticking them vertically into a bowl of rice and passing food from one pair of chopsticks to another. Also avoid stabbing food, pointing with them, or using them to drag dishes towards you.

When drinking, pour for others rather than yourself, and let someone pour for you. JNTO notes that "an empty glass is a glass that needs refilling", so leave yours partly full if you've had enough.

## Before you go

- Note one or two regional specialities for each place on your route.
- Visit Nishiki Market in the late morning and expect crowds.
- Head to Tsukiji's outer market for seafood, cookware and a meal; the tuna auction is now at Toyosu.
- Practise the two key chopstick rules: never upright in rice, never food from chopstick to chopstick.
- Learn "itadakimasu" and "gochisosama desu".
- Pour for your companions, and leave your glass partly full when you're done.
