The Great Migration is not an event with a start date but a loop that never stops: wildebeest, zebra and gazelle moving across the Serengeti ecosystem with the rains and the grass. Broadly, the herds spend roughly December to April on the short-grass plains of the southern Serengeti and Ndutu, where they give birth, then move north, reaching Kenya's Maasai Mara from around June or July before drifting south again late in the year. Exact timing shifts with the rain every year, so plan for a season, not a date.

## The scale of it

Tanzania National Parks (TANAPA), which manages Serengeti National Park, describes it as home to "the world's last remaining large mammal migration" and puts the herd at over 1.5 million wildebeest, plus zebras and the predators that follow them. Counts vary by source and by year: the Frankfurt Zoological Society, which works with TANAPA in the park, describes a migration of over two million wildebeest, zebra and gazelle, while the Mara Conservancy in Kenya gives figures of 1,245,000 wildebeest and 200,000 zebra.

The park itself was established in 1951 and has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1981, according to TANAPA. It covers about 15,000 square kilometres. Beyond the herds, TANAPA lists lions, leopards, elephants, buffalo and rhinos, and hundreds of bird species. The Frankfurt Zoological Society notes that the Serengeti's black rhinos are critically endangered and few in number.

## Month by month

These are typical patterns, drawn from the authorities that manage each part of the ecosystem. In any given year the herds can be weeks early or late.

- **December to March: the southern plains and Ndutu.** The Ngorongoro Conservation Area Authority (NCAA) says the herds begin arriving at the Ndutu plains, on the edge of the southern Serengeti, from the end of December. Ndutu is a calving ground, and "thousands of calves are born" there in season.
- **January and February: calving.** TANAPA names January to February as the calving season. The NCAA calls Ndutu one of the best places in the conservation area to spot predators.
- **April and May: moving north.** The NCAA says April is when "millions of wildebeest, gazelles, and zebras begin their journey northwards". Neither TANAPA nor the NCAA publishes a fixed route for these weeks, and the herds can be spread out and on the move.
- **June to October: the dry season and the north.** TANAPA recommends June to October, the dry season, for the migration. TANAPA describes the Grumeti River, the Seronera Valley and the plains as habitats that shape where animals move as the seasons change.
- **June or July to November: the Maasai Mara.** On the Kenyan side, the Mara Conservancy says the migration usually arrives somewhere in June or July and stays until November. This is the period of the famous Mara River crossings.
- **November and December: heading south.** As the herds leave the Mara, they make their way back towards the southern plains, where the NCAA expects them again from late December.

## River crossings: what to expect

Crossings are the image most people have in mind, but they cannot be scheduled. The Mara Conservancy says crossings "happen frequently during the migration but are difficult to predict", and that a build-up of agitated wildebeest along the riverbank is the usual sign one may happen.

The same authority is blunt about behaviour. Visitors must stay inside the vehicle, keep quiet, keep back from the crossing point and never block moving animals. Standing on the roof or sitting in the window is not allowed. It says it has seen wildebeest die "in their hundreds" when vehicles pushed them into crossing at unsuitable places where the banks were too steep to climb out.

## Ndutu and the Ngorongoro Conservation Area

Ndutu sits inside the Ngorongoro Conservation Area, not the national park. It is managed by a different authority, the NCAA, which sets its own fees. If your calving-season trip is based around Ndutu, check that your itinerary and quote include the correct conservation area fees as well as Serengeti park fees. The NCAA notes that Ndutu has resident wildlife year round, including elephant, lion, cheetah and giraffe, so it is not empty when the migration has moved on.

## Fees, access and park rules

At the time of writing (November 2026), TANAPA lists Serengeti entry fees of US$70 for international adults and US$20 for international children, and says fees vary by age, residency and type of vehicle, with additional charges for vehicles and camping. Check the current tariff with TANAPA before you pay.

- **Getting there.** TANAPA says Arusha is the most common entry point, with regular flights to Seronera airstrip. The nearest international airports are Kilimanjaro and Arusha. By road, the journey may take seven to eight hours.
- **Hours.** The park is open all year, and TANAPA says gates close at 6pm.
- **Rules.** Stay in designated areas, do not feed wildlife and keep to speed limits. TANAPA says guides must be certified and drones are prohibited without special permission.
- **Booking.** TANAPA recommends booking accommodation in advance, especially during peak migration seasons.

## Before you go

- **Pick a season, not a week.** Choose calving (roughly January and February) or the northern dry season (roughly June to October) and accept that the herds may be a little early or late.
- **Check entry rules.** The UK FCDO says you need a tourist visa for Tanzania. You can apply for an e-visa before you travel. All visitors also need Tanzanian government inbound travel insurance, even if they already have their own policy.
- **Confirm which authority you are paying.** A trip based at Ndutu involves NCAA fees as well as, or instead of, Serengeti National Park fees.
- **Ask how your guide handles crossings.** Ask if they follow the rules on distance and not blocking animals.
- **Allow for long drives.** If you are not flying in to Seronera, the road journey from Arusha can take a full day.
