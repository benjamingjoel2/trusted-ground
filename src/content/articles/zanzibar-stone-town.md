Stone Town is the old heart of Zanzibar City, on Unguja, the main island of the Zanzibar archipelago. It is a dense maze of coral-stone houses, heavy carved doors and stone benches where the street doubles as a meeting place. UNESCO, which lists it as a World Heritage Site, calls it "a fine example of the Swahili coastal trading towns of East Africa", with its urban fabric "virtually intact". It is also a living, largely Muslim town, and visitors are expected to respect local dress and customs, which Zanzibari authorities enforce with fines.

## A port shaped by the Indian Ocean

UNESCO describes Stone Town as a place that has "brought together and homogenized disparate elements of the cultures of Africa, the Arab region, India, and Europe over more than a millennium". The town was built on an ancient coral reef on the coast of Unguja and is the largest surviving Swahili town on the East African shore.

The more recently developed part of the city is called Ng'ambo, Swahili for "the other side". UNESCO notes that most of Stone Town's buildings are still used as homes and shops, which keeps it a working commercial and administrative centre rather than a museum.

## Reading the architecture

Much of the pleasure of Stone Town is in its details, and the UNESCO Urban Heritage Atlas explains where they come from:

- **Building materials.** Most buildings are made of coral rag masonry set in thick lime, earth and sand mortar, then covered with lime plaster and lime wash.
- **Omani houses.** Omani traders and plantation owners arrived in the early 19th century. They introduced massive, multi-storey square blocks of coral stone with flat roofs, crenellated parapets and inner courtyards for privacy. The carved square wooden doors are full of symbolic patterns.
- **Indian shopfronts.** The largest group of traditional buildings are narrow shop-houses along the bazaar streets, built by Indian traders. They have four-leaf Gujarat-style doors that open the whole front to customers, with living quarters at the back or upstairs. Many Omani houses were later bought by Indian merchants, who added verandas.
- **British-era additions.** In the early 20th century, under the British Protectorate, Saracenic or Moorish details and a few European-inspired churches were added.
- **The baraza.** With so little public space, streets are lined with barazas, low masonry benches at the base of the walls. They are part of a building's steps and also a place to trade and talk.

## Landmarks to look for

UNESCO lists a set of monuments, most from the 18th and 19th centuries, that show the town's many cultures and faiths:

- **The Old Fort**, built on the ruins of a Portuguese church and houses, and converted by the Arabs into a garrison fort.
- **The House of Wonders**, a ceremonial palace that Sultan Barghash built in 1883. It was among the first buildings in East Africa to have electricity and tap water.
- **The Old Dispensary**, built in 1887 by an Indian architect, with ornate Indian decoration.
- **Christ Church Anglican Cathedral**, which stands on the site of the last open slave market in Zanzibar and commemorates the abolition of the slave trade.
- **St Joseph's Roman Catholic Cathedral**, whose bell towers are part of the skyline.
- **Malindi Bamnara Mosque**, built in 1831 and one of the few mosques with a minaret.
- **The Hamamni Persian baths**, one of two Persian-style baths in the town.
- **The house of Tippu Tip**, the slave trader, which is a good example of a vernacular Arab townhouse.

UNESCO warns that the town's authenticity is vulnerable to new buildings of the wrong scale and design, to traffic, and to "pressures from the developing tourism industry". Choosing locally run guides and staying in restored historic buildings rather than new blocks is one way to tread lightly.

## Visiting respectfully: dress, Ramadan and the law

The UK FCDO notes that there is a high proportion of Muslims in Tanzania, particularly in Zanzibar and Pemba. It asks visitors to respect local traditions, especially during Ramadan and at religious sites.

- **Dress.** The FCDO advises dressing modestly throughout Tanzania. It says women wearing shorts or sleeveless tops away from tourist resorts in Zanzibar, "particularly in Stone Town", may cause offence. At the time of writing (November 2026), it says Zanzibari authorities can fine you US$700 or more for clothing they consider inappropriate in public, and guides or tour operators at least US$1,000.
- **Ramadan.** During the holy month, avoid eating or drinking in public outside hotels and restaurants. The dates change each year.
- **Same-sex relationships.** These are illegal in Tanzania, including Zanzibar, and the FCDO warns that showing affection in public could lead to arrest.
- **Plastic bags.** These are banned. If you arrive by air, you will be asked to hand them over, although ziplock bags for airline security are allowed.
- **Drugs.** Penalties are severe. Some CBD products are illegal.

## Staying safe

The FCDO reports bag snatching and other crime incidents in Stone Town, at hotels and on popular beaches across the island. It advises walking towards the traffic and keeping well away from the roadside. It also notes that previous elections in Zanzibar have turned violent, and that further demonstrations are possible after the October 2025 general election. Check the latest advice before you travel.

If you take the ferry from Dar es Salaam, the FCDO warns of past ferry disasters and aggressive ticket touts at ports. Do not board a boat that looks overloaded, and note where the life jackets are.

## Before you go

- **Get the island's insurance if you arrive there first.** According to the FCDO, all non-resident visitors need Tanzanian government inbound travel insurance. If Zanzibar is your first port of entry, you buy it from the Zanzibar Insurance Corporation, on arrival or online beforehand.
- **Pack for the town, not just the beach.** Bring loose clothing that covers shoulders and knees.
- **Check the Ramadan dates** for your year if your trip falls around it.
- **Use a local guide** to learn the stories behind the doors, houses and monuments.
- **Leave plastic bags at home** and keep valuables out of sight on the street.
