Scallop shells, once the badge that proved a pilgrim had reached Santiago, a stamp from every village where you sleep, and at the end the stone square before the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela: the Camino de Santiago, or Way of St James, has drawn pilgrims since the early Middle Ages. Today people walk, cycle or ride it for faith, for a challenge or simply for the time it gives them. If you want the traditional certificate, the Compostela, two things matter: carrying a stamped pilgrim credential, and covering the minimum distance on a recognised route.

## Choosing a route

There is no single Camino. As the Pilgrim's Reception Office in Santiago points out, medieval pilgrims simply set off from their own front doors. The Xunta de Galicia's official Camino website lists the routes that enter Galicia and lead to Santiago:

- **The French Way (Camino Francés)**, described as the route with "the most historical tradition" and the one most recognised internationally.
- **The Portuguese Way** and the **Portuguese Coastal Way**. The inland route follows ancient roads including the Roman Via XIX.
- **The Northern Way**, which follows the coast of Asturias and Galicia.
- **The Primitive Way**, used by the first pilgrims from the Asturian kingdom.
- **The English Way**, starting from Ferrol or A Coruña. This was the route used by Icelandic and Scandinavian pilgrims, who arrived by sea.
- **The Vía de la Plata (Silver Way)** or Camino Mozárabe, coming up from Andalucía and Extremadura.
- **The Winter Way**, and the sea-and-river **Route of the Sea of Arousa and River Ulla**.
- **The Fisterra and Muxía Way**, which begins in Santiago and heads west to the Atlantic coast.

The Pilgrim's Office suggests choosing your route based on how much time you have, whether you want to walk it all at once or in stages over several trips, and the time of year.

## The credential (credencial)

The pilgrim's credential is a folded card with spaces for stamps. It has two practical uses, according to the Cathedral: it gives you access to pilgrim hostels run in the tradition of Christian hospitality, and it is your evidence when you apply for the Compostela.

- **Where to get one.** You can collect an official credential in person at the Pilgrim's Reception Office, or from institutions the Cathedral authorises, such as parish churches, Associations of Friends of the Way of St James, pilgrim hostels and confraternities. Some associations abroad are authorised to issue their own.
- **Stamps.** Stamps are usually collected where you sleep, but churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels and town halls also stamp credentials.
- **Digital option.** The office also offers a digital credential as an app.
- **Group etiquette.** The credential asks groups travelling with a support vehicle, or by bicycle, to find somewhere other than the pilgrim hostels to stay.

## Earning the Compostela

The Compostela is a certificate in Latin issued by the Cathedral of Santiago to people who make the pilgrimage for religious or spiritual reasons. The current rules, as published by the Pilgrim's Reception Office:

- **On foot or horseback:** at least 100 continuous kilometres on the same recognised route, with the final stage being the one that leads into the Cathedral.
- **By bicycle:** at least 200 kilometres, on the same conditions.
- **Starting outside Spain:** if you began on foot on a recognised route outside Spain, the distance required within Spain is at least 70 kilometres.
- **In stages:** you can walk in stages over separate trips, as long as they are in order. If you are walking only the minimum distance, get your credential stamped and dated at the start and end of each stage.

Both the welcome service and the Compostela are free. The office also sells an optional **Certificate of Distance**, recording your starting point, route and kilometres, for €3 at the time of writing (November 2026).

## Arriving in Santiago

The Pilgrim's Reception Office is on Rúa das Carretas, reached from Praza do Obradoiro. It opens every day except 25 December and 1 January. If you arrive on those days, you can collect your Compostela at the Cathedral. To queue, you take a ticket with a QR code that shows your place in real time. The office warns that at busy times it cannot guarantee you'll collect your Compostela on the same day you arrive. For more on travelling in the country, see our [Spain guide](/countries/spain/).

## Safety on the trail

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) notes that temperatures in parts of Spain can change very quickly. It advises checking local weather reports for extreme heat or cold before a hike. Wildfires are common in summer, and causing one is a criminal offence even if accidental. Put cigarettes out properly and don't light barbecues. For any emergency, including a mountain rescue, call 112.

## Before you go

- Choose your route and decide whether to walk it in one go or in stages.
- Get an official or Cathedral-recognised credential before you start, and collect at least the stamps the rules require.
- If you want the Compostela, plan to cover at least the last 100km on foot or 200km by bicycle on a single recognised route.
- Check weather and wildfire warnings, especially in summer, and carry plenty of water.
- Allow a spare day in Santiago in busy periods in case the Compostela queue runs over.
