In midwinter, Finnish Lapland is a world of deep snow, blue twilight and silent forests, where reindeer wander and the sky can burst into green light after dark. It is also cold, remote and short on daylight. The short answer: come between late autumn and early spring for snow and the aurora, understand that the polar night is twilight rather than total darkness, and plan every outdoor day around the cold and the early dark.

## What Lapland is like in winter

Visit Finland describes Lapland as Finland's northernmost region and a year-round destination known for its Arctic wilderness, light phenomena, snow and reindeer. Most of the roughly 200,000 reindeer in Finnish Lapland roam free.

It is also the land of the Sámi, the only indigenous people in the European Union. Their homeland, Sápmi, crosses the north of the region. Visit Finland points visitors to the Siida Centre of Sámi Culture in Inari to learn about Sámi heritage and life.

Winter is the season for skiing, snowboarding and snowshoeing, and for the saunas that dot the region from Rovaniemi to Saariselkä.

## The polar night: kaamos

Kaamos is the Finnish name for the polar night, when the sun stays below the horizon. According to thisisFINLAND, published by Finland's Ministry for Foreign Affairs, it happens only above the Arctic Circle, and its length depends on how far north you are:

- In **Utsjoki**, on Finland's northern border, it lasts almost two months.
- In **Sodankylä**, further south, it lasts only four days.

Crucially, kaamos is rarely completely dark. Sunlight still reaches the upper atmosphere and lights the sky, giving the blue twilight typical of northern Finland, and snow and moonlight add more. For photographers and walkers, the midday hours can be beautiful; just expect them to be short.

## The aurora season

Visit Finland gives the northern lights season as late August to early April. Within that window:

- In Lapland, auroras appear on about every other clear night.
- Northern Finland sees them on roughly 200 nights a year, compared with around 10 to 20 nights in southern Finland.
- The best window on any given night is typically between 9pm and 2am.
- Autumn and spring, especially around the equinoxes, are statistically the most active; midwinter gives the longest viewing windows.

Cloud is usually the deciding factor, so stay several nights if you can. The Finnish Meteorological Institute runs an aurora forecast based on its magnetometer network: a yellow level means auroras are possible but usually dim, and red means they are very likely visible. ## Staying safe in the cold and dark

Metsähallitus is blunt on its Luontoon.fi outdoor site: long exposure to severe cold can be life-threatening, and temperatures can drop to −30°C or lower. Its advice includes:

- **Dress for cold and wet**, and keep your phone inside your clothing, as batteries drain faster in extreme cold.
- **Plan for darkness.** Darkness comes early in the winter months; carry a headlamp.
- **Stay off uncertain ice.** Do not walk on a frozen lake or river unless you are sure it will hold you. Ice over moving water is often dangerous.
- **Watch for avalanches** when snowshoeing or skiing in the wilderness.
- **Tell someone your route**, and sign in at visitor centres or wilderness huts where possible.
- **In an emergency**, use the 112 Suomi app, which sends your coordinates to rescue services. If lost or injured, stay where you are until rescuers arrive.

The FCDO adds that parts of Finland lie within the Arctic Circle, including very remote areas where emergency medical assistance and search and rescue are limited.

## Getting around

If you hire a car, the FCDO notes that you need a UK driving licence, and that hire companies often have stricter rules such as a minimum age, a year of driving experience or an international driving permit. It also warns that Finland's drink-drive limit is significantly lower than England's.

## Before you go

- Decide whether you want deep polar night (the far north, around midwinter) or milder temperatures with strong aurora activity (February to March, according to Visit Finland).
- Pack proper cold-weather layers and a headlamp, and keep spare phone power warm.
- Download the 112 Suomi app and a weather app before you arrive.
- Book at least three or four nights to give clear skies a chance.
- If you will drive, check your hire company's licence and age requirements in advance.
