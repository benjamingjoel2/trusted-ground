A church tower rises above a ring of thick stone walls, with the village houses gathered around it and farmland and wooded hills beyond. Southern Transylvania has more than 160 of these fortified churches, built by Saxon communities over several centuries. Most are best seen slowly, by car or on foot, and many are opened on request by a local keeper rather than kept to fixed hours.

## Why the churches have walls

Transylvanian Saxon settlement began at the end of the 12th century, according to the Fortified Churches Foundation (Stiftung Kirchenburgen), which works to preserve the heritage of the Evangelical Church A.C. in Romania. Transylvania was a heavily contested border region, and the foundation dates the need for defence to at least the Mongol invasion of 1241 to 1242.

The defences grew in stages:

- Early castles were built on high ground; village churches were later surrounded by ditches, ramparts and palisades.
- After the first Ottoman invasions in the early 15th century, palisades were replaced with stone ring walls and defensive towers.
- Inside the walls, storerooms and "fruit houses" held supplies so villagers could shelter during a siege.
- In the Târnava area and the Hârtibaciu Valley, apses and sanctuaries gained tower-like superstructures, and west towers were given battlement galleries.
- Main portals were often walled up and smaller side doors added.

Peace in the early 18th century made the walls redundant. Some were dismantled in the 19th century and their stone reused for schools and parish houses, although many villages kept their towers out of tradition. Today about 160 fortified churches still stand, mainly in southern Transylvania. The foundation warns that more than half are threatened by decay to some degree.

## Biertan

Biertan is the most visited of the fortified churches and the easiest place to start. The cIMeC museum guide, run by Romania's national heritage institute, describes the Evangelical fortified church as a UNESCO World Heritage site, first mentioned at the start of the 15th century and built on the foundations of an earlier 13th-century church.

The church holds a collection that includes a polyptych altar, church furniture, Oriental carpets and the funerary monuments of Lutheran bishops, along with 16th and 17th-century documents and rare books. The collection is administered by the Evangelical Church of Romania and, according to cIMeC, is open 10:00 to 19:00 in summer. No winter hours are listed, so check locally before an off-season visit.

## Viscri

Viscri, known in German as Deutsch Weisskirch ("the White Church"), is a smaller and quieter village, and its church is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, according to the ADEPT Foundation, a conservation organisation working in the area. ADEPT traces the building in stages:

- A 12th-century church replaced an earlier Romanesque church.
- The Lutheran church was fortified in the 15th century.
- A seven-metre ring wall was added in the early 16th century.
- In the 17th century the defences were strengthened with fortified houses, towers and a parapet walk.

Inside, the church keeps a painted panelled ceiling from 1743 and plain, understated furnishings. The appeal here is the whole setting as much as the church itself: the village street, the walls and the surrounding farmland.

## Finding keepers and planning a route

Outside the best-known sites, do not expect a ticket office. Many fortified churches are opened by a local keeper. The Fortified Churches Foundation publishes a map of the churches and a pocket booklet (2020 edition) with short descriptions and contact details for the castle keepers of about 154 churches. Both are available from the foundation's office in Sibiu or by email request.

A few planning points:

- Base yourself in Sibiu, Sighișoara or Brașov, or in village guesthouses, and visit two or three churches a day rather than trying to cover many.
- The foundation's FortiVacation project offers overnight stays in towers and annexe buildings of fortified churches, with Curciu as the model project.
- Some churches charge an entrance fee or ask for a donation; carry small amounts of Romanian lei in cash.
- Treat the churches as places of worship: dress modestly and keep voices low if a service is under way.

## Driving in rural Transylvania

Most villages are easiest to reach by car. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) describes road conditions as variable and warns that in rural areas you should expect horse-drawn carts, livestock and stray dogs on the road. It also notes:

- You need a "rovinieta" road toll vignette for national roads, sold at border points and most petrol stations.
- Drivers must carry a first aid kit, fire extinguisher, warning triangle and fluorescent jacket.
- Winter tyres are required in snow, ice or sleet, commonly from November to March.
- Drink-driving is a serious offence.

## Before you go

- Pick up or request the Fortified Churches Foundation's map and keeper booklet before you set off.
- Check summer and winter opening arrangements for each church; many are open on request only.
- Carry cash in lei for entrance donations and village guesthouses.
- Buy a rovinieta vignette before driving on national roads.
- Allow extra time on rural roads for carts, livestock and potholes.
- Read the latest FCDO advice, including entry requirements, before you travel.
