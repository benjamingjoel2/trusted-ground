High in the Ethiopian highlands, about 2,500 metres up, a group of churches has been carved downwards into the volcanic rock rather than built up from the ground. They are linked by trenches and tunnels, and they are still full of worshippers. Lalibela's rock-hewn churches are among the most remarkable religious sites in Africa. They are also in Amhara region, and at the time of writing (November 2026) the UK FCDO advises against all travel there. This article explains what makes the churches special and what the current advice means.

## A "new Jerusalem" cut from rock

According to the World Monuments Fund (WMF), Lalibela lies about 600 kilometres north of Addis Ababa in Ethiopia's Amhara Region. At its centre is a complex of 11 churches cut from the living rock some 800 years ago. Their construction is traditionally attributed to King Lalibela of the Zagwe dynasty, who reigned around 1181 to 1221. He is said to have set out to create a new Jerusalem on African soil, open to all Ethiopians.

The link to Jerusalem runs through the landscape. The WMF notes that local features carry Jerusalem names: the River Jordan, the church of Golgotha and the Tomb of Adam. The site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1978.

## How the churches are arranged

The WMF describes the churches as sculpted from solid volcanic rock and often connected by long underground tunnels and trenches. The main cluster is divided into groups:

- **A northern group** of five churches.
- **An eastern group** of another five.
- **Biete Ghiorgis**, perhaps the most famous church, which stands on its own.

## A living place of worship

Lalibela is not a museum. The WMF says the churches are still used for daily worship and special ceremonies, and they draw pilgrims and large crowds at holidays such as Christmas and Easter. Culture in Crisis, a heritage protection programme, calls the 12th-century rock-hewn churches Ethiopia's holiest and most heavily visited cultural heritage sites.

If and when travel there is possible again, the usual courtesies for an active holy site apply. Follow the directions of clergy and local guides about where you may go, and be especially careful during services and festivals, when crowds are large.

## Conservation: shelters and repairs

The churches are fragile, and rainwater is a constant threat. The WMF's first-ever project, in the 1960s, was at Lalibela, where a team of Italian conservators documented and stabilised the churches. In 2007 the European Union built temporary modern shelters over four of the churches. The WMF says these keep rain off but do nothing for the churches left uncovered, and their massive steel frames are "highly intrusive".

Since 2007, the WMF and UNESCO have worked together on conservation, site management and training. A pilot project at Biete Gabriel Raphael was completed in December 2015 with Ethiopia's Authority for Research and Conservation of Cultural Heritage. It repaired crumbling exterior stone, laid a new waterproof roof layer and carried out structural repairs, all with traditional lime-based mortars. Culture in Crisis records a further conservation project for the Beta Mika'el and Golgotha churches and their wall paintings.

## Current travel advice for northern Ethiopia

At the time of writing (November 2026), the UK FCDO's Ethiopia advice was last updated on 30 October 2026. It says:

- **Amhara region.** The FCDO advises against all travel to Amhara region, which includes Lalibela. It also advises against all travel to Tigray region and to parts of Afar.
- **Conflict.** Since spring 2023 there has been significant conflict in Amhara between Amhara militia groups (Fano) and the Ethiopian authorities, along with opportunistic crime and banditry. The FCDO says the security situation in northern Ethiopia may deteriorate quickly.
- **Kidnapping.** There have been a number of recent kidnappings in Amhara and Oromia. The FCDO says that if you go to areas where it advises against travel, you will be at "extreme risk".
- **Flights.** Some flights are suspended, and flights across northern Ethiopia may be suspended or cancelled at short notice.
- **Unrest.** Protests and strikes can lead to temporary closures of roads, internet and mobile networks.
- **Consular help.** British Embassy staff in Addis Ababa cannot offer support in person in areas where the FCDO advises against travel.

The FCDO's general insurance guidance warns that if you travel somewhere it advises against all travel, your insurance may be invalidated. Many policies will not cover such destinations at all.

Advice can change in either direction. Check the latest FCDO page, and your own government's advice if you are not British, before making any plans. For the rest of the country, see our [Ethiopia guide](/countries/ethiopia/).

## Before you go

- **Read the latest FCDO advice and map.** It sets out which regions it advises against travelling to, and it changes.
- **Check your insurance wording.** Find out what is covered in any area under an FCDO warning, and get the answer in writing.
- **Get a visa before you fly.** The FCDO says UK nationals need a visa for tourism and recommends applying online through the official Ethiopian e-visa platform for a 30-day single-entry tourist visa.
- **Ask your operator** how they would change your plans if flights to the north were cancelled or the advice changed.
- **Keep paper copies** of your passport and the British Embassy's contact details, as the FCDO recommends.
