Milford Sound/Piopiotahi sits at the end of a single mountain road in the north-west of Fiordland National Park, where Mitre Peak rises 1,692 metres straight out of the water. Getting there is half the experience, and the weather decides much of the rest. Rain is the norm, not the exception, and in winter the road itself can close.

## A fiord shaped by ice and rain

Fiordland National Park was constituted in 1952, covers more than 1.2 million hectares and was declared a World Heritage Area in 1986, according to New Zealand's Department of Conservation (DOC). Fourteen fiords cut into its coast, and Destination Fiordland, the regional tourism organisation, notes that Milford Sound is the only one reachable by road. The fiord runs 16 kilometres from the head of the sound to the Tasman Sea.

The Māori name, Piopiotahi, comes from the piopio, a native thrush that DOC says is now probably extinct. The waters are protected as the Piopiotahi (Milford Sound) Marine Reserve, one of ten marine reserves in Fiordland.

It is a busy place. DOC says Milford Sound attracts over one million visitors a year, and it is working with Ngāi Tahu and other agencies on changes to how tourism there is managed. Expect facilities and arrangements to change in coming seasons.

## Driving the Milford Road

The Milford Road, part of State Highway 94, runs 120 kilometres from Te Anau to Milford Sound. DOC suggests allowing about two hours to drive it without stops; Destination Fiordland recommends three hours, so you have time for viewpoints and short walks. The road passes through the Homer Tunnel, which DOC records was begun in 1935 and finished in 1954.

Things to know before you set off:

- **Te Anau is the last town with full facilities.** Milford Sound itself has limited facilities.
- **Closures happen.** DOC warns visitors to expect closures and delays caused by weather, rockfall, and snow and avalanches in winter. It describes winter conditions between May and September as bringing snow, ice and avalanche risk.
- **The road is busy** and, in DOC's words, needs good driver care.
- **Mobile reception is limited**, although some stopping points and campsites along the road now have coverage.
- **No freedom camping** within 200 metres of the road. DOC campsites along the route can be booked online.
- **Drones** may not be flown without a permit.

The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) notes that New Zealand highways can be narrow, winding and hilly, and that weather can quickly become dangerous in exposed areas.

## Getting onto the water

Destination Fiordland lists several ways to see the fiord: scenic cruises that vary in length and onboard services, overnight cruises, kayak trips for a range of abilities, dive trips and scenic flights by plane or helicopter. If you would rather not drive, combined coach-and-cruise tours are an alternative, and scenic flights also operate from Queenstown and Te Anau.

When comparing trips, look at how long you will actually spend on the water, whether the boat goes out towards the Tasman Sea, and what happens if the road or the weather cancels the day.

## Weather: plan for rain

Fiordland's rain is the reason for its waterfalls. DOC says the area receives an average of about seven metres of rain a year over roughly 200 rain days, driven by the westerly "Roaring Forties". Destination Fiordland adds that the waterfalls are at their most spectacular on rainy days, so a wet forecast is no reason to cancel.

DOC's guidance is that cold, snow, strong wind and heavy rain can occur at any time of year. Summer days are typically between 10°C and 18°C; winter temperatures can fall to between 1°C and 9°C. Avalanche risk runs mainly through winter and spring, especially from late August to early November.

Destination Fiordland's seasonal notes are useful for timing:

- **December to February:** the warmest months, with long daylight hours.
- **March to May:** cooler mornings and evenings, with misty scenes.
- **June to August:** often the most settled weather, with clear skies and snow on the peaks, but short days and possible road restrictions.
- **September to November:** unsettled equinox weather, higher rainfall and occasional snow to sea level.

## Earthquake and tsunami awareness

DOC says a large earthquake or tsunami is extremely unlikely during a visit, but that both pose a risk to life at Milford Sound and your options would be limited. Its advice: if an earthquake feels long or strong, get gone, moving up and away from the water as fast as possible. Do the same if you see or hear a landslide.

## Before you go

- Check road conditions and the forecast on the morning you drive, especially between May and September.
- Fill the tank and stock up in Te Anau.
- Budget two to three hours each way on the road, plus time on the water and any walks.
- Pack a waterproof jacket and warm layers whatever the season.
- Ask your cruise or flight provider what their policy is if weather or a road closure stops the trip.
- Know the "long or strong, get gone" rule before you reach the foreshore.
